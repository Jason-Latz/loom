# Loom Clear Prose

## Purpose

Make Loom lessons easier to enter, follow, and remember. Preserve the research
and the adult intelligence of the reader while reducing the amount each
sentence asks the reader to hold at once.

Use literary references diagnostically. Borrow transferable craft principles,
not a living author's distinctive voice, syntax, imagery, or phrases.

## Treat the craft guidance as suggestions

Only the section labeled **Hard constraints** is binding. Treat every other
section as a collection of possibilities, diagnostic questions, and revision
moves. Select the few that address the passage's actual problem.

Do not make a passage satisfy every suggestion. A strong paragraph may remain
distant from a character, omit imagery, carry several ideas in one sentence,
or end without a formal landing. Preserve successful irregularities. Use the
writer's judgment before any checklist or metric.

Treat examples as demonstrations of a mechanism, not phrases or structures to
repeat. A character-owned *Good* may work once and fail elsewhere. A three-verb
sequence is useful when three real operations matter, not because three is a
preferred number.

## One useful story register: clear-close-third

Consider close third person when a `story` benefits from intimacy. Keep
third-person grammar while bringing the narrative distance close enough that
the reader experiences the scene through the character's attention and private
logic. This can create the intimacy of first person without changing *he*,
*she*, or *they* to *I*.

- Let the viewpoint character determine what the prose notices and in what
  order.
- Move from physical perception into thought without repeatedly announcing
  *he saw*, *she felt*, or *they thought*.
- Use free indirect questions, judgments, and occasional fragments when they
  sound like the character's mind rather than the narrator performing.
- Let the character make small, unannounced value judgments. A functional
  fragment such as *Good* can reveal standards, affection, attention, or relief
  without the narrator explaining those feelings afterward.
- Keep explanation inside an object, action, obstacle, or choice when the
  evidence permits it.
- Prefer sensory imagery the character can actually encounter. Let the image
  change or sharpen the thought instead of decorating it afterward.

First-person closeness is a matter of narrative distance, not pronouns. Do not
use actual first person unless the lesson has a reason to establish an *I*.

### When the plot line needs an anchor

Treat interiority and clarity as separate requirements. Free indirect thought
can bring the reader close to the character, but it must not make the scene's
central problem implicit or hard to recover.

- If a reader could lose the thread, consider a plain sentence that names the
  controlling question, decision, discovery, or danger.
- That sentence often works before or immediately after the character's
  interior questions. It need not appear when the movement is already clear.
- Use interior thought to show why the problem matters to the character, not as
  the only place where the reader can learn what the problem is.
- Preserve simple causal sentences that carry the story forward. Do not trade
  them away merely to make the prose feel closer or more literary.
- Ask what changed and what the character is trying to understand. If either
  answer is unclear, an explicit plot sentence is one possible repair.

For the Kleitos example, the interior question *But whose song?* creates useful
closeness. The passage must still state the full problem: writing can preserve
a song, but someone must choose which performed version to write.

## Hard constraints

1. Preserve every supported fact, qualification, date, relationship, and
   citation unless the user explicitly requests a substantive change.
2. Keep each citation beside the claim it supports. Do not widen its scope.
3. Do not present reconstruction as recorded fact. Never invent a quotation,
   measurement, document feature, historical causal claim, or biography.
4. When `storyContext` plainly labels a composite or reconstruction, allow
   plausible connective action, sensory experience, and interior response that
   fit the documented setting. Keep these inventions modest and never use them
   to smuggle in a factual claim or a confident motive. For a real named person,
   stay within what the record supports.
5. Preserve meaningful uncertainty. Do not turn *may*, *probably*, *one view*,
   or *scholars disagree* into certainty.
6. Use no em dashes or en dashes in Loom copy.
7. For production lesson prose, honor the model assignment in the repository's
   `AGENTS.md`. Do not silently substitute another model for prose authorship.

## Two possible modes

Use one mode for a normal revision. Use both only when the user asks for a
comparison or when the right register is genuinely uncertain.

### Clear-direct

Lead with the literal point. Prefer compact sentences, exact nouns, ordinary
verbs, and explicit causal links. Use little or no figurative language. This is
the default for a conceptually dense `significance` section. Combine its clear
sentence architecture with close third person when revising a `story`.

### Quietly-vivid

Keep the same literal clarity, then add one concrete image, physical action, or
earned rhythmic landing when the evidence supports it. Prefer images carried
by the viewpoint character's body and surroundings to conceptual metaphors
laid over an explanation. Beauty is a rationed resource, not a continuous
surface treatment. This is often useful in `story` and at important paragraph
endings.

Both modes must express the same truth ledger. They may differ in emphasis,
sentence boundaries, rhythm, and the amount of concrete texture.

## Match the demonstration to the craft question

Choose source material that can actually display the requested quality.

- To test viewpoint, intimacy, imagery, or scene rhythm, use `story` material
  with a person, a physical setting, an object, and a consequential choice.
- To test explanatory clarity, qualification, or argument structure, use
  `significance` material.
- Do not use an abstract scholarly dispute to demonstrate embodied narrative
  voice unless the user specifically asks for that constraint.
- Do not manufacture a scene around facts that lack scene-level evidence. Use
  an already established lesson scene or choose a more suitable passage.

## Suggested revision workflow

### 1. Identify the paragraph's job

Name the one thing the paragraph must help the reader understand. If it has two
independent jobs, split it. Good jobs include:

- establish a scene or problem;
- explain a mechanism;
- compare two interpretations;
- show a consequence;
- connect this node to a later one.

Do not make a paragraph introduce a person, define a system, narrate an event,
and announce its legacy all at once.

### 2. Build a truth and perspective ledger

Before rewriting, list the passage's atomic claims in plain language. Record
beside each claim:

- its certainty or qualification;
- its supporting citation marker;
- any required term, date, name, quotation, or numerical value;
- any relationship that must remain explicit.

For a `story`, also notice which existing details carry the viewpoint: a small
judgment, a physical difficulty, a knowledge gap, a mistaken assumption, or a
choice. Mark whether each is documented or part of a labeled reconstruction.
Do not preserve every fact while accidentally deleting everything that makes a
person present.

For an A/B comparison, build the ledger once and use it for both versions.

### 3. Put the thought in walking order

Arrange material as **orientation, expansion, landing**:

1. **Orientation:** give the reader the subject and the paragraph's literal
   point early.
2. **Expansion:** add evidence, mechanism, contrast, or consequence in a
   sequence where each sentence depends on the previous one.
3. **Landing:** end on the implication, unresolved question, or concrete image
   the reader should carry forward.

For a scholarly dispute, name the shared question first. Present one answer,
then the competing answer, then state what remains unresolved.

### 4. Reduce sentence-level load

- Give each sentence one main claim, action, or turn.
- State mechanisms; imply feelings. Name the actor and the concrete operations
  when the reader needs to understand how something changed. Let judgments,
  choices, and attention carry the emotional meaning without explanation.
- Prefer an explicit sequence of meaningful verbs to a vague relationship. For
  example, *Kleitos had kept some lines, forgotten others, and added lines his
  teacher had never sung* names the full mechanism. *Some came from his
  teacher* leaves the reader to reconstruct it.
- Keep a three-part sequence when its parts map real and distinct operations.
  Cut ornamental triads that merely restate one idea three ways.
- Give a long sentence one visible grammatical spine. If it contains multiple
  hinges such as *while*, *although*, *which*, and *because*, split it.
- Name the actor before describing the action when chronology matters.
- Prefer a strong verb to a noun phrase plus a weak verb.
- Repeat the exact technical term when it still means the same thing. Do not
  rotate through synonyms merely for variety.
- Tether abstractions to an object, action, choice, or consequence.
- Define an unfamiliar term at first use or make its meaning recoverable from
  the sentence.
- Use a short recovery sentence after a demanding explanation or before a
  major turn.

### 5. Control the lyricism

Fragments, metaphor, repetition, and personification are allowed when they are
concrete, sparse, and earned by an already clear literal meaning.

Reject a device when:

- the reader must decode the image before understanding the fact;
- two or more metaphors compete in one sentence;
- an abstraction performs a chain of theatrical actions;
- the image implies evidence the sources do not provide;
- the image is a conceptual transformation pasted onto exposition rather than
  something the viewpoint character can perceive;
- every sentence strains to sound memorable.

Keep the sentence if removing the device would make the thought less precise,
less felt, or harder to remember. Otherwise, prefer the literal version.

### Cut prose that performs writing

Treat generic atmosphere and compressed profundity as defects, even when the
sentence sounds polished.

- Cut an atmospheric image if removing it changes nothing about what the
  character knows, wants, fears, remembers, or does.
- Make every image carry information, pressure, characterization, or choice.
  Scenery alone does not earn a sentence.
- Avoid a conceptual claim followed by an exception that partly reverses it.
  State the literal facts in their natural order instead.
- Do not force an aphorism at the end of every paragraph. A concrete decision,
  unanswered question, or unadorned fact can land harder.
- Do not confuse polish with prose. Slight irregularity is preferable to a
  conspicuously balanced sentence, ornamental triad, or rehearsed paradox.

For example, *Beyond him, the sea kept its own time* adds atmosphere but no
story work when the sea affects nothing. Cut it. *His teacher's voice was gone
from the world, except where it remained in him* makes the reader resolve a
metaphorical contradiction. State the sequence instead: the teacher is dead;
Kleitos can still sing some of what the teacher taught him.

### Check for the safe-summary fallback

When a revision becomes accurate but lifeless, consider whether caution has
turned the character into a grammatical placeholder.

- Remove the character's name and pronouns mentally. If the paragraph still
  works unchanged as a museum label, the viewpoint may not be doing anything.
- Look for an existing friction, judgment, limit of knowledge, or change in
  understanding before inventing new decoration.
- Be suspicious of several consecutive sentences that merely position the
  camera and inventory facts.
- An abstract closing summary often explains what the paragraph has already
  demonstrated. A character-bound observation may provide a more natural end.
- Preserve a source passage's useful human asymmetry. In the papyrus scene, the
  copyist can understand the furnace comparison while never having seen a
  brain. That difference does more story work than a general statement about
  preserving knowledge.

### 6. Shape the rhythm

Vary sentence length deliberately. Use short sentences as recovery beats, not
as a metronome. Let an occasional longer sentence carry a list, escalation, or
carefully ordered contrast, then give the reader somewhere to stand.

Use these ranges as diagnostics, not quotas:

| Section | Average sentence | Typical paragraph | Sentences over 35 words |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `story` | 12 to 18 words | 40 to 90 words | Usually under 5% |
| `significance` | 16 to 21 words | 60 to 120 words | Usually under 10% |

Do not pad, chop, or merge prose merely to hit a number. A sentence that is
easy to parse can exceed the range. A short sentence can still be overloaded.

### 7. Audit the result

Compare the revision with the truth ledger and answer all of these:

- Is every original claim present with the same certainty?
- Does every citation still support the sentence immediately around it?
- Can a reader state the paragraph's job after one reading?
- Can a reader state the scene's controlling question without reconstructing
  it from fragments or imagery?
- Does each sentence have one clear spine?
- Is each pronoun's referent obvious?
- Did synonym churn or decorative abstraction return?
- Is there a recovery beat after the densest thought?
- Does the landing follow from the paragraph rather than merely sound grand?
- Did the rewrite introduce a claim that now needs research?

Treat an uncomfortable answer as a signal, not an automatic failure. Revise
only when the change improves the passage as a whole. Run the local metric
script for an additional diagnostic:

```bash
node .agents/skills/loom-clear-prose/scripts/prose-metrics.mjs <lesson-file> [story|significance|both]
```

The script detects pressure points. It does not judge quality.

## A/B comparison format

When the user asks for variants, provide by default:

1. one sentence stating the shared factual core;
2. **A, clear-close-third**, in two paragraphs;
3. **B, a named close-third adjustment**, in two paragraphs;
4. one short note naming the meaningful difference.

Choose one controlled adjustment for B, such as more interior thought, more
sensory texture, or a quieter narrator. Do not change viewpoint, structure,
imagery density, and sentence rhythm all at once. Keep the paragraph break at
the same conceptual seam when possible so the comparison remains legible.

For Loom story comparisons, bias B toward deeper free indirect thought rather
than more narrator-supplied lyricism. Let the character's question or judgment
create the intimacy. Do not add atmospheric sentences merely to make B feel
more literary.

Do not make B more factual, more certain, or more complete than A. The useful
comparison is how the same knowledge moves, not which version knows more.

## Register verdict from the full-lesson pilot (2026-08-16)

The surgeons-papyrus A/B pilot ran two complete candidates from one shared
evidence ledger: A, clear-close-third; B, interior-close-third with more free
indirect discourse. Jason read both and chose A decisively. Standing results:

- Default Loom story register is A: clear-close-third, explicit plot spine,
  embodied but restrained interiority. Do not push free indirect discourse
  density; B's register is retired.
- The View from Above must be simple to understand. Nuance and named debate
  belong there; pithy phrasing does not. Jason rejected, verbatim, a balanced
  antithesis ("the same problem sorted the same way twice, three and a half
  thousand years apart") and meta-cleverness about the text's own grammar
  ("the you it addresses is never the man holding the pen"). Both shapes are
  now hard defects in review, everywhere in a lesson.
- Every ancient person in a story carries a personal name; invented names are
  period-plausible and plainly labeled in storyContext (rule and
  reconciliation in docs/forge-spec.md).

## Relationship to lesson forging

This skill governs readability and prose revision. The `forge-lesson` skill
still governs lesson schema, source research, citation validation, chronology,
and release gates. When both apply, follow `forge-lesson` for factual and
structural requirements, then use this skill for the prose pass.
