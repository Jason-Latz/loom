buried with fallow deer antlers laid across the chest

### A2. `ochre-for-the-dead` — summary

**Current (exact find target):**

```
sometimes with ochre, animal remains, or pierced shells.
```

**Replacement:**

```
sometimes with animal remains, in layers that also held worked ochre and sea shells carried in from the coast.
```

**Resulting full summary (74 words):**

```
Early modern humans buried their dead at Qafzeh and Skhul in the Levant more than 90,000 years ago, sometimes with animal remains, in layers that also held worked ochre and sea shells carried in from the coast. Neanderthals also treated some bodies with care, so the impulse was not ours alone. Deliberate burial reveals shared attention to death and perhaps ritual, but archaeology cannot recover the mourners’ exact beliefs or prove an afterlife doctrine.
```

**Justification.** Bar-Yosef Mayer, Vandermeersch and Bar-Yosef, *Journal of Human Evolution*
56 (2009), 307 to 314, read in full from the PDF. Abstract: the shells are "complete
*Glycymeris* bivalves, **naturally perforated**," brought "some 35 km" from the shore.
Page 308: "The shells were distributed over an area of 6 m2 and there is **no indication
that they were directly associated with any of the human burials**," and "the shells were
originally located in the lowermost Middle Paleolithic levels at Qafzeh Cave, **below most
of the burials**." Both halves of the committed clause ("pierced", and "with" the burials)
are contradicted; the replacement keeps the ochre and the shells, which are real and
in the same deposits, without claiming they were grave goods.

### A3. `the-tended-fire` — hook

**Current (exact find target):**

```
A hearth at Qesem Cave holds ash returned to the same spot for millennia.
```

**Replacement:**

```
A hearth at Qesem Cave holds ash in stacked layers, the same four square meters of floor burned over and over.
```

Word count: n/a (hook).

**Justification.** Shahack-Gross, Berna, Karkanas, Lemorini, Gopher and Barkai,
*Journal of Archaeological Science* 44 (2014), 12 to 21: the feature covers "ca. 4 m2"
and "contains **two superimposed use cycles, each composed of shorter episodes**,"
which the authors call "possibly the earliest superimposed hearth securely identified
to date." The excavation report bounds this hearth to two superimposed cycles; nothing
in it supports a multi-millennial single feature. (Fire use across the site's whole
~220,000-year occupation does span millennia, but that is not what the hook says.)
This is the mildest of the eight accepts: the committed wording is unsupported rather
than flatly refuted, and the replacement gains a sourced concrete number.

### A4. `the-hollow-bone` — hook

**Current (exact find target):**

```
someone drills five holes along a hollow vulture bone and blows
```

**Replacement:**

```
someone cuts five holes along a hollow vulture bone and blows
```

Word count: n/a (hook).

**Justification.** Conard, Malina and Münzel, *Nature* 460 (2009), 737 to 740, read in
full: the fine incised lines beside the holes "probably reflect measurements used to
indicate where the finger holes were to be **carved using chipped-stone tools**."
The holes were cut, not drilled; "drills" names a technology the excavators did not find.

### A5. `the-hollow-bone` — summary

**Current (exact find target):**

```
The bone and ivory flutes from Hohle Fels and neighboring caves, about 42,000 years old, are among the oldest instruments known. Cut to a playable scale, they show music was already an art, not the accident of a whistling bone.
```

**Replacement:**

```
The bone and ivory flutes from Hohle Fels and neighboring caves, made around 40,000 years ago, are among the oldest instruments known. Fine lines incised beside the holes show the spacing was measured before cutting, so music was already an art, not the accident of a whistling bone.
```

**Resulting full summary (79 words):**

```
The bone and ivory flutes from Hohle Fels and neighboring caves, made around 40,000 years ago, are among the oldest instruments known. Fine lines incised beside the holes show the spacing was measured before cutting, so music was already an art, not the accident of a whistling bone. Sound synchronizes feeling and knits a group faster than speech can, which is why every human society sings. The oldest surviving art was not only painted; some of it was heard.
```

**Justification.** Two defects in one sentence pair, both from Conard, Malina and Münzel
2009 (read in full). (a) The 42,000 to 43,000 figure is Higham et al.'s 2012 redating of
the Geissenklösterle **Early Aurignacian**, a horizon *below* the flute-bearing levels;
the paper's own statements for the flutes are that we "can be certain that the flutes from
Hohle Fels pre-date 35,000 calendar years ago" and that they "date from the initial Upper
Palaeolithic settlement of the region, about 40,000 calendar years ago." (b) "Cut to a
playable scale" is refuted in the same paper: "We have not yet been able to produce a
replica of the flute," and the reconstructed Geissenklösterle three-hole flute yields four
basic notes plus three overtones, with Zhang, Xiao and Lee (*Antiquity* 78, 2004) stating
that pitch and scale cannot be determined for Palaeolithic flutes. The replacement swaps a
refuted claim for a stronger attested one from the same page: "as many as four very fine
lines were incised near the finger holes ... probably reflect measurements."

### A6. `the-eyed-needle` — hook

**Current (exact find target):**

```
Threaded with sinew, it turns loose hides into a fitted, layered skin a body can wear into the cold.
```

**Replacement:**

```
Threaded with sinew, it stitches finer seams than an awl can, building the layered skin a body needs to wear into the cold.
```

Word count: n/a (hook).

**Justification.** Gilligan, d'Errico, Doyon, Wang and Kuzmin, "Paleolithic eyed needles
and the evolution of dress," *Science Advances* 10(26), eadp2887 (2024), fetched:
"the production of fitted garments does not require eyed needles" and "fitted garments were
likely manufactured with awls, burins, and other devices before the advent of eyed needles."
The committed hook credits the eyed needle with the transformation from loose hides to a
*fitted* skin, which is precisely the claim the current peer-reviewed authority removes.
The paper does support what the replacement asserts: the needle's benefits are
"facilitating finer sewing by hand" and "the need for underwear in multilayered garment
assemblages." **"Sinew" is deliberately kept** (see R18): the same paper writes of "insertion
of **sinew** or thread," so the material is not contradicted.

### A7. `wolf-at-the-fire` — hook

**Current (exact find target):**

```
an animal is buried in the same grave as the people it hunted with
```

**Replacement:**

```
an animal is buried in the same grave as the people who kept it alive
```

Word count: n/a (hook).

**Justification.** Janssens, Giemsch, Schmitz, Street, Van Dongen and Crombé,
"A new look at an old dog: Bonn-Oberkassel reconsidered," *Journal of Archaeological
Science* 92 (2018), 126 to 138: the dog was a late juvenile of about **27 to 28 weeks**,
carrying enamel hypoplasia lines from three distemper bouts at weeks 19, 21 and 23, and
"survival without intensive human assistance would have been unlikely." The paper states
the dog "cannot have held any utilitarian use to humans" during that period. A sick puppy
dead at six months is the wrong evidence for a hunting partnership; the replacement states
what the grave does support, and is the same paper's own headline finding.

### A8. `pots-before-the-farm` — summary

**Current (exact find target):**

```
The oldest known pottery, from Xianrendong Cave in southern China, dates to about 20,000 years ago.
```

**Replacement:**

```
The oldest claimed pottery, from Xianrendong Cave in southern China, dates to about 20,000 years ago, a figure other specialists put thousands of years younger.
```

**Resulting full summary (59 words):**

```
The oldest claimed pottery, from Xianrendong Cave in southern China, dates to about 20,000 years ago, a figure other specialists put thousands of years younger. Soot and scorch marks make cooking the strongest use we can infer; storage or fermentation remain possibilities, not demonstrated functions. Pottery appeared here among mobile foragers long before either agriculture or settled village life.
```

**Justification.** Kuzmin, "The origins of pottery in East Asia: updated analysis (the 2015
state-of-the-art)," *Documenta Praehistorica* 42 (2015), 1 to 11, open access, verified
independently: he rejects the Xianrendong claim, re-dates the site to c. 14,600 cal BP on a
14,160 to 14,990 cal BP calibration, and awards East Asian primacy to Yuchanyan at
17,830 to 18,190 cal BP. Wu et al., *Science* 336 (2012), and Cohen (2013) hold the
20,000 to 19,000 cal BP figure. The committed text states one side of a live, published
chronological dispute as settled fact; the hedge is the minimum honest repair and does not
disturb the `song-economic-miracle` edge, which holds under either chronology (Jiangxi and
Hunan are both southern China).

## Rejected

Grouped by node. One line each. Numbering is `<node> #<flag number in the flags file>`.

**Reviewer errors worth recording (the flag is not merely out of scope, it is wrong):**

- **R1. `pots-before-the-farm` #2 ("soot and scorch marks" unsupported).** Refuted. The
  reviewer could not open the paywalled *Science* paper and inferred absence. Wu et al.
  2012 do report it: "scorch and soot marks on sherd exterior surfaces indicate that
  Xianrendong pottery likely was used for cooking." The committed summary is correct as
  written and must not be softened. (The abstract's separate "may have served as cooking
  devices" is a hedge on function, not a denial of the marks.)
- **R2. `the-eyed-needle` #3 ("threaded with sinew" is unattested).** Overstated. Gilligan
  et al. 2024, the reviewer's own authority, write of "insertion of **sinew** or thread."
  Plant fibre is the directly excavated material, but sinew is not contradicted, and
  removing it costs the hook its only tactile noun. Keep.
- **R3. `ash-across-asia` #2 (Jwalapuram above-and-below).** The reviewer concedes in the
  same paragraph: "The summary is not false but it is dated by about thirteen years."
  Haslam et al. 2012 correct Locality 22 only; the above-and-below evidence at Locality 3
  stands. Dated framing is a lesson-prose problem, not a data defect.
- **R4. `ash-across-asia` #1 ("Stoneworkers return afterward").** Ironically the hook is
  *more* compatible with the 2012 revision than the summary is: a ~35,000-year sedimentation

## Structural, needs a human decision

None of these are text defects and none are folded into the edits above.

**Era-wide convention (decide once, applies to many nodes):**

- **S1. The BP versus BCE conflation across Era I.** Flagged independently by
  `ash-across-asia` #7, `into-the-last-continents` #1, `the-eyed-needle` #1,
  `pots-before-the-farm` #11 and `wolf-at-the-fire` #2. Every source in this period counts
  in years before present; the atlas's `date` fields treat those figures as BCE, which is a
  systematic ~1,950-year offset. **The sharpest single case is `wolf-at-the-fire`:
  `date: 'c. 20,000 to 14,000 BCE'` excludes its own headline evidence** (Bonn-Oberkassel
  at 14,223 cal BP is about 12,270 BCE; Pınarbaşı at ~15,800 years ago is about 13,775 BCE).
  `pots-before-the-farm` is the counter-case: its date field is a *correct* conversion and
  must not be "fixed." Changing `date` strings does not move `sort` or map placement, so
  this can be decided independently of the chart, but it should be decided as one policy.
- **S2. `the-hollow-bone` date field vs summary.** `date: 'c. 42,000 BCE'` against the
  summary's date. Accepted edit A5 moves the summary to "around 40,000 years ago"; whether
  the date field follows is part of S1.
- **S3. Era I span.** `span: 'c. 300,000 to 10,000 BCE'` vs `first-of-our-kind` at 315 ka
  (reaching 349 ka at the top of its error bar). The reviewer recommends leaving it. Not a
  defect; a header-vs-node rounding question.
