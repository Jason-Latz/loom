# Lesson brief: mound-and-mountain

Research and architecture scaffolding for Fable. Not draft prose. Every claim below carries its evidentiary status. Where this brief and a source disagree, follow the source and say so in the completion report.

## Graph target

- Node id: `mound-and-mountain`. Title: Mound and Mountain. Era 5, date `c. 1050 CE`, sort 1050, region `americas`, x 11. Threads: power, wealth.
- Outgoing graph edge, only one: `inka-threads` ("Andean traditions of labor tax and roadcraft climax in the Inka.")
- Incoming edges, from nine earlier nodes: `great-dispersal` (Era I, independent development), `caral-supe` and `poverty-point` (Era II, platform-and-plaza traditions; earth moved basket by basket), `chavin-oracle` (Era III, pilgrimage and highland exchange), `teotihuacan-maya`, `lords-of-sipan` and `jenne-jeno` (Era IV), `silent-stelae` and `chaco-roads` (Era V).

The node is a hinge with many feeders and one exit. Its job in the atlas is to prove that statecraft is a family of solutions rather than a ladder, and to carry the Andean labor-and-cord repertoire forward to the Inka.

The central claim to land: the Americas kept inventing durable political order in idioms Eurasia never used, and the two halves of this node are not contemporaries. Cahokia begins around 1050 CE. Wari and Tiwanaku belong to the preceding half millennium, roughly 600 to 1000 CE. The lesson must do that arithmetic in the open rather than let the reader assume a shared moment.

## Preferred scene

**Cahokia, the Grand Plaza, around 1050 CE: a plaza-raising feast whose leavings were dumped, all at once, into an open borrow pit.**

The documented object is the borrow pit under what became Mound 51. Pauketat, Kelly, Fritz, Lopinot, Elias and Hargrave excavated its stratified fills and published them in American Antiquity in 2002. What the paper attests: a pre-Columbian borrow pit in the middle of the site; layers of ordinary and extraordinary refuse, from foods and cooking pots to craft-production debris and sumptuary goods; a series of large-scale, single-event dumping episodes tied to activities in the principal plaza. That is a feast entering the ground in one motion, not rubbish slowly accumulating.

Second documented object for the same hand: the beaker. Crown, Emerson, Gu, Hurst, Pauketat and Ward (PNAS, 2012) found theobromine, caffeine and ursolic acid in beaker-shaped vessels at Cahokia and nearby Illinois sites, dating roughly 1050 to 1250 CE. The holly species that yield Black Drink do not grow near Cahokia; the leaves came north, probably through Caddoan intermediaries or from near the mouth of the Mississippi. A caffeinated ritual drink, carried a thousand kilometres, in a vessel shaped for it.

Documentation ledger:

- Attested: the borrow pit and its single-event dumps; plaza association; the categories of material (food, pots, craft debris, prestige goods); Black Drink biomarkers in beakers, 1050 to 1250 CE; the imported holly; the city's grid reorientation and rapid growth around 1050; earth moved in baskets, with no draft animals and no wheeled transport in use.
- Reconstruction: any named person, any dialogue, the weather, the order of the day, who carried what, the specific occasion. Cahokia has no writing, so no participant can be named. Every human being in this scene is a composite and `storyContext` must say so plainly.
- Real named person available: no. None exists for Cahokia at any date.
- Quantities: do NOT state counts of deer, pots, tobacco seeds or swan bones from memory. The 2002 paper carries the tallies; verify any number against it before printing one.

Why this scene: it dramatizes the mechanism. A city with no coin, no script, no draft animal and no standing army had to be assembled out of gathered people, and the pit is the physical residue of the gathering.

### Alternate 1 (strongest documentation; recommended as the second movement)

**Cerro Baul, Moquegua valley, southern Peru, around 1000 CE: the Wari colony brews one last time and burns its own brewery.**

Moseley, Nash, Williams, deFrance, Miranda and Ruales (PNAS, 2005) excavated a Wari brewery on a mesa held for centuries as an embassy-like enclave beside Tiwanaku colonists. Attested: separate compartments for milling, boiling and fermentation; batch capacity around 1,800 litres; numerous tupu shawl pins, insignia of high-status women, indicating elite women in the brewery; a final ceremony in which a last batch was served from pitchers to nobles in the court; up to 28 lords each drank from a decorated kero and cast the vessel into the fire; the buildings were burned and the enclave evacuated around 1000 CE.

Ledger: the brewery, the pins, the pitchers, the keros, the burning and the evacuation are excavated facts. The individual brewer, her name, her thoughts and the sequence of the afternoon are reconstruction. No named person survives.

This is the single best-documented scene available to the lesson. Its liability is that it belongs to the node's second subject and sits fifty years before the node's headline date. That liability is also an opportunity: opening here would force the date math into the first two paragraphs. My recommendation is still Cahokia first and Cerro Baul as the second movement, because the title names them in that order and because ending the story on a fire is a better hinge than ending on a basket.

### Alternate 2 (safest, no composite characters at all)

Open inside the excavation rather than the past: the stratified fills of the borrow pit read downward, layer by layer, as a single afternoon compressed into centimetres. Everything in the scene is then attested and the lesson never invents a person. Weaker on human pressure, stronger on candour. Hold in reserve if the composite feels thin.

### Scene to avoid as a cold open

Mound 72. The deposit includes mass graves (reported figures run past 270 individuals, with a majority read as sacrificial), and the classic beaded-burial narrative has just been overturned (see traps). Opening on sacrificed young women would be sensational, would centre violence in a lesson whose subject is construction, and would sit badly with the descendant nations who collaborate on the site. Use Mound 72 as a beat inside significance, told through the 2016 reinterpretation, not as theatre.

## Story movement

Two movements and a hinge, deliberately unlike the single-workday walk.

1. **The mound.** Cahokia around 1050. Earth in baskets, the plaza levelled and enlarged, the pit open at its edge, the feast going into the ground in one motion, the beaker of imported holly passing. Let the reader feel the arithmetic: nobody here is paid, nobody is taxed in coin, nobody writes anything down, and a hundred thousand basket loads still arrive.
2. **The hinge.** The same human act, a crowd fed at a monumental place, is doing opposite work at the two ends of the hemisphere. At Cahokia it opens a city. Fifty years earlier and a hemisphere south, it closes one.
3. **The mountain.** Cerro Baul around 1000: the last batch, the pitchers, the keros in the fire, the roof going up, the mesa emptied on purpose. Then widen to the highland toolkit that outlives the state that used it: terraces, roads, reciprocal labor, knotted cord.
4. End at the pivot, not the moral. The reader should arrive at the question significance answers: if a state is not coin, script, iron and wheel, then what is it made of, and how would we know?

Governing image: the vessel and the basket. A cup that carries hospitality and a basket that carries earth are the two instruments both worlds actually used. Let the image return once at the hinge and once at the close, changed.

Do not write "mysterious." Do not write "vanished." Do not stage either world as a rehearsal for something European.

## Significance spine

1. **Evidence: what the ground holds, and how thin the numbers are.** Greater Cahokia was three connected precincts (Cahokia, East St. Louis, and the St. Louis group). The Illinois State Archaeological Survey counts about 200 earthen pyramids across the three, roughly 120 at the Cahokia precinct itself, about 80 surviving; the central precinct covered around 16 square kilometres at peak. Population is an argument, not a measurement: ISAS gives a core near 20,000 with a regional total above 50,000; a widely cited study gives roughly 10,200 to 15,300 for the dense central areas after 1050, up from 1,400 to 2,800 before; published maxima across the literature run from about 6,000 to 40,000. Say how the estimates are built (dwelling space and excavated house counts, extrapolated) and let the range stand.

2. **Mechanism: how you build a city with no coin, script, iron or draft animal.** The instruments are feast, labor, sightline and import. Monks Mound: about 30 m high, base roughly 291 by 236 m, footprint near 6 hectares, about 622,000 cubic metres of earth (about 22 million cubic feet), every load carried. Woodhenge: a sequence of red cedar post circles west of Monks Mound found by Warren Wittry during 1960s highway salvage work, circles of 24, 36, 48 and 60 posts at diameters from roughly 73 to 145 m, with equinox and solstice sightlines documented and further astronomical claims speculative. Imports: marine shell, copper (a workshop at Mound 34, the only one known at a Mississippian site), southern holly for Black Drink. Strontium isotope work indicates a substantial immigrant population, so the city drew people as well as goods. Threads in words: power (the capacity to summon and feed a crowd) and wealth (long-distance goods converted into hospitality), with craft (earth, cedar, copper, cord) doing the visible work.

3. **The other half, with the arithmetic on the page.** Wari, out of the Ayacucho basin, and Tiwanaku, on the Titicaca altiplano near 3,850 m, both held sway roughly 600 to 1000 CE (the PNAS Cerro Baul paper states exactly that span). Tiwanaku's monumental core is first-millennium CE; a Bayesian reading of its radiocarbon puts the founding near 110 CE, prominence about 375 to 700, population perhaps 10,000 to 20,000 around 800, and political collapse near 1000, with Janusek treating the polity as running to about 1150. Cerro Baul was evacuated around 1000. So at the node's date of 1050, Wari is finished and Tiwanaku is fragmenting. What survived was not the state but the toolkit: terracing and raised fields, roads, reciprocal labor obligation, chicha hospitality as a political instrument, knotted cord for accounts. The Inka reassembled that repertoire roughly four centuries later (imperial expansion from about 1438). Say the gap out loud.

4. **Named debate (use this one).** Alan Kolata and collaborators argued that a drought recorded in the Quelccaya ice core ended raised-field farming around Lake Titicaca and brought Tiwanaku down. Clark Erickson answered in Antiquity in 1999, under the title "Neo-environmental determinism and agrarian 'collapse' in Andean prehistory," arguing that raised fields were abandoned and revived repeatedly, that farmers had options, and that climate explanations had been allowed to do work political and social history should do. The debate is live, and it is exactly the atlas's kind of argument: whether weather or people ended a world. Present both sides without a verdict. Secondary debates if a second is wanted: whether Cahokia was a state or a very large chiefdom (Pauketat for urban, state-like scale; George Milner for something smaller and less centralized), and whether "empire" fits Wari at all.

5. **Consequence: how quickly a certainty can turn over.** Two recent cases. (a) Mound 72's beaded burial was read for decades as two elite males on a falcon of shell beads, retainers around them, a warrior-chief cosmology tied to the Birdman or Red Horn. Emerson, Hedman, Hargrave, Cobb and Thompson reanalysed the remains and published in American Antiquity in 2016: male and female pairs, numerous females, at least one child. They read the deposit through world creation, renewal and fertility rather than a paramount chief's warrior cult. A whole popular narrative about Cahokian kingship rested on a sexing error. (b) The story that Cahokia destroyed itself by stripping the uplands of timber, causing erosion and catastrophic flooding, was tested stratigraphically by Rankin, Barrier and Horsley (Geoarchaeology, 2021), who found stable ground surfaces and no evidence of human-caused erosion or flood deposits during the occupation. The ecocide story is a modern morality tale the sediment does not support.

6. **Inheritance, and the end of the "vanished" frame.** Cahokia's population fell through the thirteenth century (the first palisade around the core dates near 1175 and was rebuilt several times, each enclosing less) and the site was effectively empty by about 1350. People moved; a people did not end. The Osage Nation works as a primary collaborator with the site's archaeologists and managers, and Dhegihan Siouan nations (Osage, Ponca, Omaha, Quapaw, Kaw) hold migration traditions connected to the region; Muscogee, Choctaw, Chickasaw and Cherokee nations also maintain ties. Phrase this as relationship and claim, not settled genealogy, and never assign a single lineal descendant. Close on the cord: roughly 1,400 pre-Columbian khipus survive; the first unambiguous ones are Middle Horizon and Wari-associated, with wrapped coloured cords and knot conventions nobody has decoded; most later khipus encode base-ten numbers, and whether any encode narrative is unresolved. An information technology that ran states for eight hundred years is still partly unread, which is a better ending than any moral about what the Americas lacked.

## Factual traps

1. **Trap:** Cahokia's people vanished mysteriously, leaving no descendants. **Truth:** the city depopulated over the thirteenth century and was empty by about 1350; the region became part of what Stephen Williams called the Vacant Quarter. The people dispersed into descendant Mississippian and later nations. The Osage Nation is a primary collaborator with the site today, and several Dhegihan Siouan and southeastern nations maintain connections. **Source:** Illinois State Archaeological Survey, Cahokia research pages (https://www.isas.illinois.edu/research_2/cahokia). Verify the Vacant Quarter attribution to Williams before printing the term.

2. **Trap:** Cahokia collapsed because it deforested its hinterland and triggered floods. **Truth:** Rankin, Barrier and Horsley tested the wood-overuse and flooding hypothesis against the site's stratigraphy and found stable ground surfaces with no evidence of human-caused erosion or flooding during the occupation. **Source:** Caitlin G. Rankin, Casey R. Barrier, Timothy J. Horsley, "Evaluating narratives of ecocide with the stratigraphic record at Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site, Illinois, USA," Geoarchaeology 36(3), 2021, 369 to 387, DOI 10.1002/gea.21848 (metadata confirmed at Crossref; Wiley landing page paywalled).

3. **Trap:** the Mound 72 beaded burial holds a male warrior chief, the Birdman, on a falcon of shell beads surrounded by retainers. **Truth:** reanalysis found male and female pairs, numerous females and at least one child, and reads the deposit through world renewal and fertility rather than a paramount male chiefdom or the Red Horn myth. **Source:** Emerson, Hedman, Hargrave, Cobb and Thompson, "Paradigms Lost: Reconfiguring Cahokia's Mound 72 Beaded Burial," American Antiquity 81(3), 2016, 405 to 425.

4. **Trap:** Monks Mound is the largest pyramid in the Americas. **Truth:** it is the largest pre-Columbian earthen mound in the Americas and the largest such structure north of Mesoamerica. Teotihuacan's Pyramid of the Sun and La Danta at El Mirador exceed it in volume. Its base footprint (about 6 hectares) is comparable to the Great Pyramid's, but its roughly 622,000 cubic metres of earth is about a quarter of Giza's roughly 2.6 million. Also: the name comes from French Trappist monks living beside it between 1809 and 1813, not from anything Indigenous. **Source:** ISAS for the "largest Indigenous earthwork in the Americas and largest pyramid north of Mesoamerica" formulation; verify dimensional figures against a published survey before printing them.

5. **Trap:** Cahokia was bigger than contemporary London, and no city in what became the United States surpassed it until 1800. **Truth:** rhetorical comparisons resting on soft numbers at both ends. Cahokia's own estimates span roughly 6,000 to 40,000 depending on method and boundary. Present the comparison as something scholars reach for, with the uncertainty named, or leave it out. **Source:** ISAS (core about 20,000, region above 50,000) against the commonly cited 10,200 to 15,300 figure for the dense central areas.

6. **Trap:** the Americas had no metal. **Truth:** Cahokia had a copper workshop at Mound 34, the only one known at a Mississippian site, working native copper by hammering and annealing; the Andes had a deep metallurgical tradition in gold, silver, copper and bronze. What is absent is iron and the large-scale use of metal for utilitarian tools and weapons. Keep the node's "without wheels, iron, or writing as Eurasia knew it" from sliding into "no metal." **Source:** ISAS and the Mound 34 excavation literature (2002 to 2010).

7. **Trap:** Wari and Tiwanaku were rival empires locked in war. **Truth:** they were contemporaries, roughly 600 to 1000 CE, who both colonized the Moquegua sierra and appear to have coexisted there for centuries without documented direct conflict. Whether "empire" fits Wari is itself argued. **Source:** Moseley, Nash, Williams, deFrance, Miranda and Ruales, "Burning down the brewery," PNAS 102(48), 2005, 17264 to 17271.

8. **Trap:** Wari and Tiwanaku invented labor taxation, terracing and cord records. **Truth:** they scaled an older Andean repertoire. Terracing, reciprocal labor and platform-and-plaza monumentality run back through Moche, Chavin and Caral, which the atlas's own earlier nodes establish. On cords, the first unambiguous khipus are Middle Horizon and Wari-associated, but wrapped batons from Cerrillos (Late Paracas, roughly 350 to 200 BCE) are a plausible earlier relative, and Ruth Shady's claimed Caral proto-khipu of about 2500 BCE has not been thoroughly evaluated. Write "the first unambiguous khipus," not "the first knotted records." **Source:** the atlas's own `caral-supe`, `chavin-oracle` and `lords-of-sipan` edges; Gary Urton, "From Middle Horizon cord-keeping to the rise of Inka khipus in the central Andes," Antiquity 88(339), 2014, 205 to 221 (metadata verified at Crossref).

9. **Trap:** Tiwanaku is thousands of years older than archaeologists admit, and Pumapunku's stonework is inexplicable. **Truth:** Arthur Posnansky's claims of 11,000 to 17,000 years are rejected; statistical assessment of the radiocarbon dates puts the founding near 110 CE, and the megalithic core is first-millennium CE work in sandstone and andesite. Do not dignify the alien-contact literature by naming it; simply give the dating. **Source:** the published Bayesian assessment of Tiwanaku radiocarbon dates; verify the specific citation before printing 110 CE.

10. **Trap:** Cahokia was a maize city. **Truth:** maize mattered, but Gayle Fritz's paleoethnobotany shows a biologically diverse system built on locally domesticated Eastern Agricultural Complex crops (erect knotweed, chenopod, maygrass and others) alongside it, worked largely by adult women and their daughters, whom she argues held real decision-making authority rather than being tribute-paying commoners. **Source:** Gayle J. Fritz, Feeding Cahokia: Early Agriculture in the North American Heartland (University of Alabama Press, 2020).

Two claims encountered in research that I could NOT verify and that Fable should confirm or drop: that Cahokians stopped nixtamalizing maize around 1200 CE, and that the East St. Louis precinct neighbourhood was deliberately dismantled or burned around 1160. Both circulate; neither was confirmed against a publication in this pass.

## Callback design

Only written or already-releasing lessons are legal targets. Note that `silent-stelae` and `chaco-roads` are NOT written and therefore cannot be callback targets, even though the graph wires them to this node. Put them in `threadsOut` instead.

1. **`maize-and-the-americas`** (strongest; use it). That lesson insists, in its own words, that maize "is not the whole meal or the whole labor force" at San Lorenzo, and its most memorable image is two hundred people on ropes with "not one animal" in harness. Cahokia repeats both facts one hemisphere north and three thousand years later: earth moved by counted human labor, and a farming system that Fritz shows was knotweed and chenopod and maygrass as much as corn. Add the date arithmetic: maize was domesticated in the Balsas drainage about nine thousand years ago and only became a staple in the American Bottom in the centuries just before 1050, so roughly eight thousand years separate the plant from the city it helped feed. The angle rewards the reader who remembers Kux's rope.

2. **`invention-of-writing`** (strong, and under-used elsewhere). That lesson explicitly refuses the law that too many things to count must produce a script, and names khipus in the refusal: "complex administration could use memory, messengers, tallies, seals, or systems such as Andean khipus." This lesson is the test case. Ask what a knotted cord does that a clay tablet cannot, what a tablet does that a cord cannot, and why Cahokia, which had neither, still coordinated a hundred thousand basket loads. Uruk's answer was durable inspectable accounts; Cahokia's was assembly, feast and sightline. Both are answers.

3. **`gift-of-the-nile`** (strong alternate). Merer's ruled papyrus grid turned forty men into something an absent administrator could inspect. Cahokia moved earth on a comparable footprint with no such instrument at all. Compare the two machines for turning food and time into a mountain: an administration that writes, and a hospitality that gathers. The footprint arithmetic is a gift here (bases comparable, volumes not), and that lesson already ends on the word affordability.

4. **`teotihuacan-maya`** (good, but spend it in `threadsOut` instead). Its thesis is that archives decide which power we can see, and its closing paragraph already delivers the line about histories that trust only the archives that talk. Cahokia is a genuine third case (no script at all, only earth, post moulds and a dump), but a callback here risks restating that lesson's own ending. Better used as a thread with a fresh why.

Recommended pair for the two callback questions: 1 and 2. Hold 3 in reserve if another Era V lesson has already claimed `invention-of-writing`.

Suggested `threadsOut` (the node has only one forward edge, so backward threads are required to reach three to five): `inka-threads`, `teotihuacan-maya`, `maize-and-the-americas`, `chaco-roads`. `silent-stelae` substitutes cleanly for `chaco-roads` if the dispersal theme suits the ending better.

## Node-data flags

Precise problems in the committed graph. Do not edit the file; report them.

1. **Hook asserts simultaneity the dates do not support.** "A city of earthen pyramids rises by the Mississippi *while* Andean lords weave states out of labor and thread." Cahokia's rapid growth begins around 1050. Wari and Tiwanaku held sway roughly 600 to 1000, Cerro Baul was evacuated around 1000, and Tiwanaku's collapse is placed near 1000 (Janusek runs the polity to about 1150). The "while" is wrong by fifty to a hundred years at best. The lesson must do the arithmetic openly; Codex may want to revise the hook.

2. **Summary says Wari and Tiwanaku "pioneered" labor taxation, terraced agriculture, and knotted-cord records.** Overstated on all three. The atlas's own incoming edges from `caral-supe`, `chavin-oracle` and `lords-of-sipan` say the opposite: these states scaled an inherited Andean repertoire. On cords, Wari-associated khipus are the first unambiguous ones, not the first knotted records. Recommend "scaled" or "welded into statecraft" rather than "pioneered."

3. **Summary's "rivaling Egypt's footprint in earth" is fragile.** True of base footprint (Monks Mound near 6 hectares against Giza's base of about 5.3 hectares) and false of volume (about 622,000 cubic metres against about 2.6 million). The word "footprint" saves it, but readers will hear mass. The lesson should print the volume figure so the comparison cannot be misread.

4. **"Without wheels, iron, or writing as Eurasia knew it" needs a guard rail.** Defensible as written, but the lesson must not let it become "no metal." Cahokia had a copper workshop at Mound 34 and the Andes had centuries of gold, silver, copper and bronze work.

5. **"A planned city of thousands" undersells and over-settles at once.** Thousands is safe, but the estimate range (roughly 6,000 to 40,000 depending on method) is one of the more interesting facts about the site and deserves to survive into the lesson rather than be flattened.

6. **Graph architecture observation.** The node has nine incoming edges and one outgoing edge, making it a near-terminus for the Americas thread until `inka-threads`. There is no wire from `mound-and-mountain` to any later North American node (`mesa-verde-exodus` in Era VI, or anything Mississippian after 1350), so a reader following the graph forward from Cahokia leaves North America entirely and lands in the Andes. Worth a Codex decision; not a defect in this lesson.

7. **Date and sort field.** `date: 'c. 1050 CE'`, `sort: 1050` describe Cahokia only. That is the right call for map placement, but it means the lesson itself carries the whole burden of explaining that half its subject matter predates its own date field.
