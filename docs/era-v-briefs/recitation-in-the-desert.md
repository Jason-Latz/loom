## Preferred scene

**Zayd ibn Thabit and the first collection, Medina, c. 632 to 634 CE.** Open inside the tradition's own memory of how a voice became a book. Abu Bakr summons 
Zayd ibn Thabit, a young Medinan who had served as one of Muhammad's scribes, after fighting at Yamama in the ridda wars kills men who carry the recitation 
in memory. Umar has pressed the idea; Abu Bakr is reluctant; Zayd is more reluctant still, since the Prophet himself never ordered such a thing. Zayd gathers 
the recitation from what is written on palm stalks and thin white stones and from the memories of men who hold it. The sheets pass to Abu Bakr, then Umar, 
then Umar's daughter Hafsa.

Documentation ledger:

- Attested: the narrative is a hadith in al-Bukhari's Sahih (Kitab Fada'il al-Qur'an, hadith 4986), compiled by a scholar who died in 870 CE, roughly two 
centuries after the events. Zayd, Abu Bakr, Umar, Hafsa, and the fighting at Yamama are all real, and this is our earliest connected narrative of the 
collection.
- Reconstruction: every sensory detail. Room, light, weather, the weight or smell of materials, Zayd's hands, anyone's inward state. None of it is in the 
source, and storyContext must say so.
- A real named person is available, which is the scene's strength. Its weakness must be stated plainly: this is what the community remembered about itself, 
written down long after, not an eyewitness minute.
- Verbatim quotation: the hadith contains a famous line in which Zayd says moving a mountain would have been lighter than the task he was given. Do not quote 
it unless you check the exact wording in a published translation of Bukhari 4986. Sunnah.com refused automated fetching in this pass, so I could not confirm 
the English rendering word for word.
- Why this scene: it dramatizes the node's mechanism (recitation becoming scripture) while forcing honesty about source layers from the first paragraph. The 
honesty is not a disclaimer bolted on. It is the story.

### Alternate 1: PERF 558, Egypt, 25 April 643 CE

A bilingual Greek and Arabic papyrus receipt for sixty-five sheep delivered to requisitioning troops, dated Jumada I of "year twenty-two" beside the Greek 
indiction dating, naming the commander Abd Allah ibn Jabir and a scribe signing as Ibn Hadid. Held in the Papyrus Erzherzog Rainer collection, Austrian 
National Library, Vienna.

- Attested: the object, its bilingualism, the sheep, the date, the names. Reconstruction: the day, the animals, the scribe's mood.
- Strength: eleven years after Muhammad's death an administrator in Egypt was already counting years from a migration, in Arabic, beside Greek. It is the 
hardest dated documentary anchor this lesson can reach, and it would make a superb second beat or a hinge inside the significance.
- Constraint: if you use it, find and verify a custodian record. I could reach only encyclopedic and confessional secondary pages, which are not good enough 
for `deeper`.

### Alternate 2: the Constitution of Medina, 622 to 623 CE

The document preserved in Ibn Ishaq through Ibn Hisham's recension (and separately by Abu Ubayd), binding the emigrants from Mecca, the Medinan clans, and 
named Jewish groups into one body for defence and arbitration.

- Attested: the text, its transmission, and an unusually strong consensus that it is authentic in kernel. Wellhausen accepted it in the nineteenth century; 
Michael Lecker's monograph is the standard modern treatment. The usual argument is that its archaic language and its treatment of Jewish groups as part of 
one umma with the believers would be strange choices for a later forger.
- Reconstruction: any scene of its drafting. No session, room, or negotiation survives.
- Strength: it dramatizes the umma as civic invention better than anything else in the record. Weakness: it is a document, not a moment, so a cold open here 
would be a reading rather than a scene. Recommended use: the pivot of the story's second half, or the second beat of the significance.

## Story movement

1. Open with Zayd. Someone is asked to do a thing the Prophet never did, for a reason nobody wanted: the men who hold the recitation are dying faster than 
the recitation can be fixed.
2. Move back to what is being collected. Qur'an means recitation. It arrived in installments across roughly two decades, in rhymed and assonant Arabic built 
for the ear and the memory, performed long before it was a codex. The community formed around a shared sound.
3. Mecca: a sanctuary town with modest regional trade, where protection ran through kin. Preaching against the local gods costs the preacher his standing, 
and the loss of clan protection is what makes staying impossible.
4. 622: the hijra as a rupture of blood loyalty, not a flight. A man leaves the kin who owe him protection and joins people who owe him nothing but faith. 
That is the invention the whole lesson turns on.
5. Medina: the Constitution's move, one umma, mutual defence, disputes referred upward. Then the detail that resists the easy story: the same document keeps 
blood-money owed clan by clan. The new obligation is a layer above kinship, not a replacement of it.
6. 632: death without an undisputed successor, and much of the peninsula's allegiance lapsing at once. The ridda wars follow, and Yamama sits inside them. 
The story closes its own loop, and Zayd's task can now be felt as what it was.
7. End on the pivot, not the resolution. Sheets in a private house, kept by a widow of the Prophet, are not yet the book of a world religion.

Do not stage the cave at Hira as documented fact, do not render revelation as inward experience, do not narrate the angel. If the cave appears at all, let it 
appear as what the tradition remembers, in one clause, and move on.

## Significance spine

1. **Evidence and its layers.** The Quran is the earliest source and is not a biography. Narrative detail comes from sira and hadith written far later: Ibn 
Ishaq died about 767 CE and survives through Ibn Hisham's recension (d. c. 833) and citations in al-Tabari (d. 923); al-Bukhari died in 870. Outside 
witnesses arrive early and thin: the Greek dialogue Doctrina Iacobi, set in Carthage in 634 CE and composed within a few years, reports at second hand that a 
prophet had appeared among the Saracens. Documentary anchors follow fast: PERF 558 in 643, the Dome of the Rock inscriptions in 691 to 692. Say once, 
clearly, that the founder's inner life is not recoverable, then stop apologizing.
2. **Mechanism one, recitation as technology.** An oral scripture in Arabic, addressed to people the text itself describes as having had no scripture of 
their own, is portable in a way a temple or a priesthood is not. Memory is the storage medium and performance is the distribution network. This is the ideas 
thread at its purest, and it is why the community could survive the loss of its founder when a hierarchy of offices might not have.
3. **Mechanism two, the umma as civic invention.** The Constitution of Medina makes believers and their allies one body for defence and arbitration while 
leaving clan liability in place. Faith adds a layer of obligation above kinship that can admit non-kin. That is the load-bearing claim, and it is both more 
accurate and more interesting than "faith replaced tribe." The power thread runs here.
4. **The named debate, when did the consonantal skeleton close?** Islamic tradition credits Uthman (r. 644 to 656). John Wansbrough argued for a text still 
fluid until roughly 800 CE; others proposed codification under Abd al-Malik around 700. Nicolai Sinai's two-part study in the Bulletin of the School of 
Oriental and African Studies (2014) tests those hypotheses against the epigraphic and literary evidence and finds neither incompatible with the conventional 
dating. Manuscripts push the same way: radiocarbon puts the Birmingham folios at 568 to 645 CE with 95.4 percent probability, and the Sanaa 1 codex at 
roughly 606 to 649 CE at the same confidence. Give both edges of the honesty: radiocarbon dates the animal, not the ink (Francois Deroche has recorded 
reservations, and Mustafa Shah has argued the Birmingham script looks more developed than the range implies), and the Sanaa palimpsest's erased lower text 
preserves a genuinely non-Uthmanic textual tradition. What survives all of this is a text stabilized very early and not stabilized magically.
5. **Consequence.** Within a decade of 632 the movement held Syria, Iraq, and Egypt, and within eighty years its reach ran from Iberia to the Indus. But 
conquest is not conversion. Non-Muslim subjects were fiscally useful, and Richard Bulliet's quantitative study (Harvard, 1979) put majority-Muslim 
populations in Iran, Iraq, Syria, and Egypt only around the tenth century. Fred Donner has argued further that the earliest movement was an ecumenical 
community of Believers including monotheists who were not Muslims in the later confessional sense, its boundary hardening only afterwards. Name that as a 
position, not as settled fact.
6. **Wealth and craft.** A scripture creates an economy: copyists, parchment and later paper, reciters, teachers, and eventually a whole apparatus for 
recording variant readings. Zakat made redistribution an obligation rather than a virtue. Pilgrimage kept Mecca economically central after it stopped being 
politically central.
7. **Inheritance.** A community whose founding document is a recited text will argue about its polity by arguing about that text, permanently. And roughly a 
fifth of humanity still dates its years not from a birth, a conquest, or a king's accession, but from the day a group of people walked away from their own 
relatives. Land the ending there, or somewhere equally concrete.

## Factual traps

1. **Trap:** Muhammad was born in 570 CE, the Year of the Elephant, when Abraha marched on Mecca. **Truth:** sura 105 mentions an army with an elephant; the 
synchronism with Muhammad's birth comes from the sira. South Arabian inscriptions, notably the Murayghan text Ry 506, date Abraha's central Arabian campaign 
to 552 CE, and the epigraphic evidence indicates he was dead by about 560. The synchronism cannot stand as chronology, and Muhammad's birth year is not 
securely known. **Source:** the Abraha inscriptions as treated in the epigraphic literature; Sean W. Anthony, Muhammad and the Empires of Faith. Write 
"traditionally c. 570" and flag it, or omit the birth year.
2. **Trap:** Mecca was a great emporium on the international incense and spice route. **Truth:** Patricia Crone's Meccan Trade and the Rise of Islam (1987) 
dismantled that picture, which had reached textbooks through Lammens and Watt, arguing the Meccans dealt largely in leather, hides, and goods of local 
consumption. Most scholars accept the demolition (Fred Donner among them) while rejecting Crone's more radical relocation of Islam's origins to northwestern 
Arabia. The fight was ferocious: R. B. Serjeant's review is a period piece of scholarly rage. Crone herself later argued the Quran's pagan milieu looks 
agrarian rather than mercantile. **Use:** a sanctuary town with regional trade, not a caravan metropolis.
3. **Trap:** the Quran was written down as a book during Muhammad's lifetime. **Truth:** the tradition itself denies this. Its own account is that portions 
were written on scattered materials while the whole lived in memory, and that collection began only after his death. The tradition's candour here is evidence 
of its seriousness, and the lesson can say so. **Source:** al-Bukhari 4986 and the parallel reports.
4. **Trap, the mirror image and equally common:** the Birmingham manuscript proves the Quran is unchanged since the Prophet's lifetime. **Truth:** 
radiocarbon dated the parchment, not the writing. Birmingham's own announcement quotes David Thomas saying the tests yield a strong probability about when 
the animal was alive, not about when the text was inscribed. The folios (Mingana Islamic Arabic 1572a) carry an undotted, unvowelled Hijazi consonantal 
skeleton, and they belong with folios in Paris: one manuscript split between two collections. **Source:** University of Birmingham press release, 2015.
5. **Trap:** Zayd ibn Thabit's collection is documented history. **Truth:** it is a hadith in a collection compiled roughly two centuries later, and rival 
traditions credit other collectors and other codices, notably those associated with Ibn Mas'ud and Ubayy ibn Ka'b. Label it as the tradition's memory of 
itself. **Source:** al-Bukhari, Kitab Fada'il al-Qur'an; Sinai (2014) on how to weigh such reports.
6. **Trap:** the hijra was a flight, and Muslims dated their calendar from it at once. **Truth:** hijra means emigration, the severing of one set of kin ties 
for another, and the term carries that legal weight. The era was instituted under the caliph Umar, conventionally in 17 AH (about 638 CE), six years after 
Muhammad's death, and PERF 558 in 643 dates by "year twenty-two" without naming an era at all. **Use:** a gift to the lesson. Making a migration the origin 
of time was a later administrative decision.
7. **Trap:** the conquests converted the conquered, and Islam spread by the sword. **Truth:** conquest and conversion ran on different clocks. Bulliet's 
conversion curves put majority-Muslim populations in the core conquered provinces around the tenth century, and taxing non-Muslim subjects gave early rulers 
a reason not to hurry. **Source:** Richard W. Bulliet, Conversion to Islam in the Medieval Period (Harvard University Press, 1979).
8. **Trap:** Muhammad was illiterate, full stop. **Truth:** the Quranic term ummi is genuinely contested: unlettered, or gentile, or belonging to a people 
without a scripture. The illiteracy reading hardened into a creedal position in later exegesis. **Source:** Sebastian Gunther, "Muhammad, the Illiterate 
Prophet: An Islamic Creed in the Qur'an and Qur'anic Exegesis," Journal of Qur'anic Studies 4:1 (2002), DOI 10.3366/jqs.2002.4.1.1. Do not assert either 
reading as historical fact; if you touch it, name the dispute in a clause.
9. **Trap:** the Doctrina Iacobi proves Muhammad was alive and leading armies in 634. **Truth:** the passage is a report at two removes inside a Greek 
anti-Jewish dialogue. It attests that Christians around the Mediterranean heard very early that a prophet had appeared among the Saracens; it is not a 
biography, and its details resist harmonizing with the traditional chronology. Sean W. Anthony's study in Der Islam 91:2 (2014), 243 to 265, DOI 
10.1515/islam-2014-0010, is the careful current treatment. **Use:** cite it for the existence and early notoriety of the movement and its prophet, nothing 
more, and do not quote it verbatim unless you check the wording against Vincent Deroche's Greek edition (Travaux et Memoires 11, 1991) or a published 
translation.
10. **Trap:** Ibn Ishaq's biography survives. **Truth:** it does not. We read it through Ibn Hisham's edited recension and through citations in later 
authors. Ibn Ishaq died about 767 CE, Ibn Hisham about 833.

## Named debate to present honestly

Use trap 3 and spine beat 4 together: **when did the Quran's consonantal skeleton close?** It is live, well documented, and exactly on this node's theme. The 
traditional Uthmanic dating, Wansbrough's late crystallization, and the Abd al-Malik hypothesis are three positions; Sinai's 2014 evaluation and the 
radiocarbon results have moved the field toward early closure without making the question trivial. One named debate is a feature, so do not also litigate 
Meccan trade at length. Keep Crone to a sentence in service of the setting.

## Callback design

Ranked. Two become the callback questions; the rest are available for threadsOut sentences.

1. **`israel-one-god`, strongest.** Angle: two portable monotheisms forged under opposite pressures. Judean universalism was sharpened by loss, a temple 
burned and a kingdom ended, so defeat had to be read as the god's sovereignty rather than his weakness. The Quranic movement's monotheism arrives with a 
community that acquires a polity within a decade. Ask what each did with text and law as a result. Sharper still, pair the two documentary images: 
Natan-Yama's Babylonian contract carrying a Yahwistic name inside an empire's paperwork, against PERF 558's Greek receipt dated by the hijra. One community 
learned to survive inside somebody else's administrative language; the other became the administration. The comparison only works if the reader has read both 
lessons, which is the test.
2. **`jesus-to-constantine`, strong, and an improvement on the orchestrator's sketch.** Angle: the order of revelation and rule. Christianity grew for nearly 
three centuries without a state and was then transformed by imperial patronage; this movement had arbitration, defence, and taxation from Medina onward, so 
there was no long-grown independent church for a later empire to adopt. Push past the obvious: that lesson ends with Constantine laying the purple aside to 
be baptized, power stepping outside the office at the very end. Ask what it means that this movement never separated the two roles in the first place, and 
what each arrangement later made possible and impossible. That lesson's threadsOut already points here, so the callback closes a loop the reader was promised.
3. **`tribe-of-strangers`, good, and the widest reach in the atlas.** Angle: the umma as the largest shared fiction yet attempted, with the honest 
complication. At the waterhole a shared name converts strangers into kin below Dunbar's ceiling. The Constitution of Medina tries to write kinship rules for 
a body far above it, and the interesting thing is that it does not abolish the clan: blood-money stays clan by clan and an arbitrating layer is added above. 
Ask what the document keeps, not only what it invents. Use this if you want the lesson reaching deep into the atlas rather than only into Era IV.
4. **`code-of-hammurabi`, reserve.** Angle: law claiming divine warrant. Hammurabi's stele shows a king receiving justice from a god, but the laws are the 
king's; the Quran presents itself as God's own speech with the messenger as reciter rather than author. Ask what changes when the legislator is not the 
author, and who then gets to interpret. Good, but it trespasses slightly on `roots-of-the-law`, so keep it a threadsOut sentence rather than a question.

Recommended pair for `questions`: 1 and 2, with 3 substituting if the finished prose leans harder on the umma than on the book.

Recommended `threadsOut` (four): `caliphate-lightning` and `house-of-wisdom` (the graph edges), plus `israel-one-god` and `jesus-to-constantine` backward. A 
fifth to `roots-of-the-law` is defensible in prose, but the graph draws no such edge; if you want it, say so in the completion report and let Codex decide.

## Node-data flags

Reported, not edited. Codex's call.

1. **Hook.** "A merchant in a cave outside Mecca is commanded: Recite." The cave at Hira is sira and hadith material, not Quranic; sura 96 carries the 
command without the setting. The hook states it as event. The lesson must not repeat the cave as documented fact. Consider a hook rewrite.
2. **Summary, "a community where faith replaced tribe."** Overstated. The Constitution of Medina preserves clan liability for blood-money, and early conquest 
armies were organized through tribal contingents. The accurate claim is that faith became an obligation layered above kinship, capable of including non-kin. 
Recommend rewording.
3. **Summary, "Within his lifetime it unified Arabia."** Overstated. Much of the peninsula's allegiance lapsed at Muhammad's death, which is precisely why 
the ridda wars of 632 to 633 were fought, and Yamama sits inside them. Recommend softening to allegiance across much of the peninsula, secured only after his 
death.
4. **Summary, "with law, charity, and scripture at its center."** "Law" as a system is anachronistic for 610 to 632. Arbitration and revealed rulings, yes; 
fiqh is eighth and ninth century, and this era's own `roots-of-the-law` (c. 820) owns it. Charity and scripture are fine.
5. **Date and sort, 610.** Traditional, and derived by counting thirteen years back from the hijra rather than independently attested. Keep the node date, 
but the lesson must call it traditional on first use.
6. **Threads, `['ideas']`.** The summary itself says the religion governed, and the node's first edge points at an empire. Recommend adding `power`. In any 
case the lesson should write ideas and power explicitly and touch wealth through zakat and pilgrimage.
7. **Edge why to `house-of-wisdom`,** "A faith centered on a book builds a civilization of books." Fine as a wire label, but the mechanism (Abbasid 
patronage, paper after 751, Syriac and Greek translators) belongs to that node. Do not pre-tell it here.
8. **Edge why to `caliphate-lightning`,** "within a single generation." Holds for the first surge, 632 to 661. No change needed.
9. No contradiction found between this node's date, region, or sort and the surrounding era file. `grand-canal-spine` at 605 and `gupta-zero` at 628 sit 
correctly on either side.
