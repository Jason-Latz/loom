## Preferred scene: Hunayn ibn Ishaq hunting a book he never finds

The dramatic object is a failure. Hunayn set out after Galen's *On Demonstration* and, by his own account, did not find it. That documented failure kills the image of a palace where all the world's books already sat on shelves, and it makes the actual craft visible: travel, purchase, collation, revision.

Documentation ledger:

- ATTESTED. Hunayn ibn Ishaq al-Ibadi, born near al-Hira in Iraq in 808 CE, died in Baghdad in 873. A Nestorian (Church of the East) Christian from the Arab tribe of al-Ibad; his father was a pharmacist. Latin Europe later called him Johannitius.
- ATTESTED, in his own words. His *Risala* (Epistle), written for his patron Abu al-Hasan Ali ibn Yahya al-Munajjim in 855 or 856 CE and reissued in a second version in March 864, surveys 129 Galenic works and states which he rendered into Syriac, into Arabic, or both.
- ATTESTED, quotable. On *On Demonstration*: "I sought for it earnestly and travelled in search of it in the lands of Mesopotamia, Syria, Palestine and Egypt, until I reached Alexandria, but I was not able to find anything, except about half of it at Damascus." (Hunayn, *Risala*, in Meyerhof's rendering, quoted in the Dictionary of Scientific Biography entry by G. C. Anawati.)
- ATTESTED, quotable, and the better craft beat. On *On Sects*: he translated it as a young man from a very defective Greek manuscript; at forty-six his pupil Hubaysh asked him to correct it; he gathered several Greek manuscripts, collated them into one good copy, then compared that against the Syriac. He says this became his method in all his translation work.
- ATTESTED. The circle: his son Ishaq ibn Hunayn (d. 911), his nephew Hubaysh ibn al-Hasan al-A'sam, his colleague Isa ibn Yahya. His teacher Yuhanna ibn Masawayh, who insulted the boy from al-Hira and told him to go sell coins by the roadside, then later took him back.
- ATTESTED. Patrons: Ali ibn Yahya; the Banu Musa brothers; physicians of the Bukhtishu family; the caliphs al-Wathiq and al-Mutawakkil, who made him head physician, then jailed him and confiscated his library over the icon affair, then recalled him.
- RECONSTRUCTION, and storyContext must say so: any room, road, weather, smell, gesture, or dialogue; the physical act of comparing copies; the order of the journey's stages; what Hunayn felt on failing.
- No composite is needed. This is a rare node with a real, named, self-documenting protagonist. Use him.

**Alternate 1: the Banu Musa as private buyers.** Three brothers, sons of Musa ibn Shakir, in ninth-century Baghdad: Muhammad (d. 873), Ahmad, and al-Hasan. Authors of the *Book of Ingenious Devices* (*Kitab al-Hiyal*, c. 850). Reported to have paid the best translators, Hunayn and Thabit ibn Qurra among them, roughly 500 gold dinars a month, and to have sent agents to buy manuscripts in Byzantine lands. They brought Thabit ibn Qurra (c. 826 to 901), a Sabian from Harran, into Baghdad science. Use this if the lesson wants to dramatize that the money was not only the caliph's. Ledger: the brothers, the book, the patronage, and the salary figure are attested in the Arabic bibliographic tradition (Ibn al-Nadim's *Fihrist*, c. 987, and al-Qifti), so attribute the figure rather than assert it. Everything sensory is reconstruction.

**Alternate 2: the tested tables.** Al-Ma'mun's observation programs at al-Shammasiyya in Baghdad in 828 to 829 under Yahya ibn Abi Mansur, and on Mount Qasiyun near Damascus in 831 to 832 under Khalid ibn Abd al-Malik al-Marwarrudhi. The result was the *Zij al-Mumtahan*, the Verified (or Tested) Tables. The name is the whole argument: within a few years of Ptolemy reaching Arabic, men were paid to check him against the sky. Strong mechanism, weak interiority. Best used as a late beat inside the preferred scene's lesson rather than as the cold open.

## Story movement

Open on the road or in the failure. No founding panorama, no caliph on a balcony, no skyline of the Round City.

First half: one man, one book he cannot find. Let the reader feel that a Greek text in the ninth century was a scarce physical object with a location, a price, and a condition. Half a copy at Damascus. Alexandria empty. Let the journey establish the movement's geography without a map paragraph.

Pivot at the collation. The *On Sects* passage is the hinge: a translator old enough to correct his own youthful work, gathering defective Greek copies, making one good text out of them, then checking that against his Syriac. Here the reader learns that translation was editing, vocabulary building, and revision under patronage, done by a Christian working in Arabic on pagan Greek medicine for Muslim and Christian patrons.

Second half: widen from the man to the money. Who paid, and why. Caliphs, but also courtiers, physicians' dynasties, and private buyers like the Banu Musa paying monthly salaries. Let the reader arrive at the question the significance answers: if the work was this distributed, what exactly was the House of Wisdom?

End the story on the failure, not on triumph. The half copy at Damascus should still be sitting there when the reader turns the page.

## Significance spine

1. **Evidence.** What we have and what we do not. Hunayn's own *Risala* (855 or 856, second version 864). Ibn al-Nadim's *Fihrist* (c. 987), a bookseller's catalogue of what was actually in circulation. Ibn Abi Usaybi'ah's biographical dictionary of physicians, finished in the thirteenth century, four hundred years after Hunayn, and fond of a good anecdote. Surviving manuscripts. Say plainly that the closer a source stands to the translators, the less it talks about a famous institution; the further away, the more.

2. **Mechanism.** Why here, why then. The movement ran roughly from the 750s into the tenth century, in Arabic, across the whole Abbasid elite, and it began under al-Mansur rather than al-Ma'mun. Applied demand drove it: medicine, astronomy and astrology, land survey, the arithmetic of inheritance shares, timekeeping and the direction of prayer. A Syriac substrate supplied it: Christian schools had been reading Greek philosophy and medicine in Syriac for generations, and much Greek reached Arabic through a Syriac stage. A labor market organized it: named translators, monthly salaries, competing patrons, rival versions. Cheap surfaces made it affordable, which is where the wealth thread bites: a paper manufactory is dated in Baghdad to about 794 to 795, and paper displaced papyrus and parchment in Abbasid administration and bookmaking across the ninth century.

3. **The named debate.** What was the bayt al-hikma? Dimitri Gutas and Kevin van Bladel argue it was a palace library on the Sasanian model, concerned largely with Persian material and Arabic scholarship, neither an academy nor the institutional home of the Greek-to-Arabic translations. The Stanford Encyclopedia's survey states that the court library "cannot be viewed as an institutional center for the translations," while allowing a close relationship between the earliest translators and the court. Against this, Marie-Genevieve Balty-Guesdon and others read the same reports as describing a real research establishment. The decisive small fact: the translators themselves, Hunayn included, never mention it in their own writings, and the term nearly disappears from the sources after al-Ma'mun's reign. Present both sides, name Gutas, and let the reader watch a legend form around a real library.

4. **Craft.** Translation as a technical discipline, and the ideas thread running through it. Ptolemy was put into Arabic at least four times: al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf ibn Matar with Sarjun ibn Hiliya al-Rumi in 827 to 828 for al-Ma'mun, then Ishaq ibn Hunayn between 879 and 890, then Thabit ibn Qurra's correction of Ishaq, which is the version that actually survives in manuscript. A civilization that retranslates one book four times is not archiving it. Note also what the craft got wrong: the *Theology of Aristotle*, produced in al-Kindi's circle and hugely influential, is an adapted Arabic version of parts of Plotinus's *Enneads* IV to VI, and the *Book of Causes* is Proclus. The most consequential Aristotle in Arabic was partly Neoplatonism under a false name.

5. **Consequence.** Not storage, extension. Al-Khwarizmi's *al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wa-l-muqabala*, written in Baghdad around 820 and dedicated to al-Ma'mun, made a subject out of a technique and gave English the word algebra. His separate book on Indian reckoning gave Latin *Algoritmi de numero Indorum* and English the word algorithm. The *Zij al-Mumtahan* checked Ptolemy against fresh observation. Hospitals with teaching functions grew in the same cities. Two generations later, in a rival caliphate, Ibn al-Haytham wrote optics in Cairo that made vision an experimental problem.

6. **Inheritance.** Twelfth-century Latin Europe went to Toledo and Sicily to buy this back, and the vocabulary still shows it: algebra, algorithm, alkali, alcohol, zenith, azimuth. Even Almagest is Arabic. Kill the storehouse myth explicitly here. Arabic-writing scholars did not mind Europe's property for it. They chose what they wanted, criticized it, corrected it, and used it, and what Copernicus inherited was the correction tradition, not the warehouse.

7. **Landing.** Return to the missing half of *On Demonstration*. Nothing survives because it is great. It survives because someone with money wanted a copy, someone with skill made one, and the copy was good enough to be worth copying again. That is still how it works.

Threads to name in words: craft and ideas are the node's pigments, but wealth (salaries, paper prices, patronage markets) and power (caliphal legitimation) should be visible too.

## Factual traps

1. **Trap:** The House of Wisdom was a great academy or research university where the Greek translations were made.
   **Truth:** Genuinely contested and probably wrong as usually told. Gutas and van Bladel argue for a palace library on the Sasanian model, mainly Persian material and Arabic scholarship. The Stanford Encyclopedia states the court library "cannot be viewed as an institutional center for the translations." The translators do not name it in their own writings.
   **Source:** SEP, *Greek Sources in Arabic and Islamic Philosophy* (rev. March 2026); Gutas, *Greek Thought, Arabic Culture* (1998).

2. **Trap:** Al-Ma'mun paid Hunayn a book's weight in gold. (This is the node's hook, stated as fact.)
   **Truth:** The gold-weight story belongs to the later biographical tradition, above all Ibn Abi Usaybi'ah, who died in 1270, roughly four centuries after Hunayn. Hunayn's own *Risala* documents commissions, named patrons, and revision requests, not a weighing scale. The better-attested economic fact is the salary: the Arabic bibliographic tradition reports the best translators earning something like 500 gold dinars a month under the Banu Musa. Do not repeat the hook's claim straight. It can be told as a story about how the movement was remembered.
   **Source:** Ibn Abi Usaybi'ah, *A Literary History of Medicine* (Brill, open access); Vagelpohl, "The user-friendly Galen" (2018).

3. **Trap:** Chinese papermakers captured at Talas in 751 gave the Islamic world paper. (This is the wording of the tang-golden-network edge into this node.)
   **Truth:** Jonathan Bloom argues paper was already known and made in Samarkand and Central Asia before 751, and that the Talas-captives story should be read the way scholars read Cai Lun's invention story, as metaphor rather than event. A Baghdad paper manufactory is dated to about 794 to 795. Paper mattered enormously; the capture story is legend.
   **Source:** Bloom, *Paper Before Print* (Yale University Press, 2001), reported in *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, "Paper ii. Paper and Papermaking." FLAG: Iranica and the Qatar Digital Library both returned 403 to this scout, so the wording above rests on secondary summaries. Confirm against Bloom or Iranica directly before the lesson asserts a date for the Baghdad mill.

4. **Trap:** Ibn al-Haytham worked at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad. (The node summary lists his optics inside a Baghdad sentence.)
   **Truth:** Born in Basra about 965, died in Cairo about 1039 or 1040. The *Book of Optics* was written in Fatimid Cairo, conventionally placed between about 1011 and 1021. That is roughly two centuries after al-Ma'mun and in a rival caliphate with its own House of Knowledge. Either place him correctly, as evidence that the movement outgrew Baghdad, or leave him out.
   **Source:** MacTutor History of Mathematics (University of St Andrews), biography of Ibn al-Haytham.

5. **Trap:** Islamic scholars preserved Greek learning and handed it back intact to Europe.
   **Truth:** Preservation understates and misdescribes it. They chose what they wanted, translated much of it through Syriac rather than from Greek, built a new technical Arabic to hold it, retranslated the same works repeatedly, tested Ptolemy's parameters against new observation, and invented subjects the Greeks did not have. Nor were the Greeks the only donors: Sanskrit astronomy and Middle Persian material arrived in the same decades.
   **Source:** SEP, *Greek Sources in Arabic and Islamic Philosophy*; Ptolemaeus Arabus et Latinus (Bavarian Academy of Sciences and Humanities).

6. **Trap:** Almagest is the Greek title of Ptolemy's book.
   **Truth:** The Greek was *Mathematike Syntaxis*, also circulated as the *Megale Syntaxis*. The name the modern world uses came through the Arabic *al-Majisti*. Europe calls Ptolemy's masterwork by an Arabic name and has done for eight hundred years. This is the most economical proof of transmission the lesson has. Use it once and do not gloss it twice.
   **Source:** Ptolemaeus Arabus et Latinus, record for *al-Majisti* (tr. al-Hajjaj).

7. **Trap:** The *Theology of Aristotle* transmitted Aristotle's metaphysics to Islamic philosophy.
   **Truth:** It is an adapted Arabic version of parts of Plotinus's *Enneads* IV to VI, produced in the circle around al-Kindi (c. 800 to 870) and attributed to Aristotle. The *Book of Causes* is likewise Proclus. The misattribution shaped Arabic and then Latin philosophy for centuries.
   **Source:** SEP, *Al-Kindi* (Peter Adamson, rev. April 2024); SEP, *Greek Sources in Arabic and Islamic Philosophy*.

8. **Trap:** The Islamic hospital descends directly from a great academy-hospital at Gundeshapur, and Baghdad's hospitals belong in a lesson dated c. 820.
   **Truth:** Michael Dols argued in 1987 that the Gundeshapur academy story is largely myth. A Baghdad bimaristan is traditionally dated to Harun al-Rashid's reign, about 805, on later authority; the great Adudi hospital of Baghdad dates to 982, well over a century after this node. Keep hospitals to one honest clause or leave them to a later node.
   **Source:** M. W. Dols, "The origins of the Islamic hospital: myth and reality," *Bulletin of the History of Medicine* 61.3 (1987): 367 to 390; Ragab, *The Medieval Islamic Hospital* (Cambridge University Press, 2015).

Two secondary traps, worth knowing and probably not worth spending words on:

- **Al-Khwarizmi's book on Indian numerals survives in Arabic.** It does not. The Arabic original is lost; what survives is a much-altered Latin version, *Algoritmi de numero Indorum* (MacTutor, al-Khwarizmi). Related: the Indian material had arrived earlier with the *zij al-Sindhind*, brought by an embassy dated 771 or 773 and rendered by al-Fazari with Ya'qub ibn Tariq. That embassy story comes mainly from Ibn al-Adami's later account, so hedge it.
- **The Mongols destroyed the House of Wisdom in 1258 and the Tigris ran black with ink.** The ink-and-blood rivers are a topos of later chronicles, and on the Gutas reading the bayt al-hikma had dropped out of the sources some four centuries earlier. Baghdad's libraries and scholars did suffer catastrophically in 1258. FLAG: this scout reached only popular sources on the topos. The sack is outside the node's date; safest handling is to leave it out.

## Callback design

Recommended pair: **alexanders-comet** and **gupta-zero**. Together they give the lesson one Greek-institutional comparison and one Indian-mathematical one, which keeps the node from reading as a Greeks-only story.

1. **alexanders-comet.** Strongest, because it pre-loads the exact myth this lesson has to break. That lesson already states that Alexander chose Alexandria's site in 331 BCE but did not found its Library, which the Ptolemies built after his death, and its own thread into this node says he "supplied neither a library nor a straight pipeline to Baghdad." Angle: Alexandria was a state collecting texts under one roof; Baghdad was a market of patrons buying, commissioning, and correcting them across many houses. Ask which arrangement actually moved the texts, and why a legend always prefers the roof.

2. **gupta-zero** (Era V, released before this one). Angle: Brahmagupta wrote rules for zero in 628 CE inside Sanskrit astronomy; those methods reached Baghdad through the *zij al-Sindhind* in the 770s; al-Khwarizmi's Arabic book on Indian reckoning is lost while its altered Latin descendant gave English the word algorithm. Ask what an idea has to shed in order to travel, and who ends up getting the credit. CAVEAT: gupta-zero is unwritten as of this brief. Fable must read the finished lesson before drafting the question and must not restate its content. If its released text already covers the Sindhind at length, demote it and use socratic-turn.

3. **socratic-turn.** Angle: Socrates wrote nothing and survives because Plato rewrote him into a form Socrates never chose; Galen wrote enormously and survives partly because Hunayn rewrote him in two other languages, amplifying, glossing, and annotating as he went. Ask what each act of transmission added and what it cost, and whether a text that survives by being changed is the same text. That lesson's own thread into this node already sets the frame: "Survival here means transformation, not storage in a sealed Western vault."

4. **silk-road-pulse.** Angle: its mechanism is the relay, goods moving in short stages with no link seeing the whole. Hunayn tried to walk the whole chain himself, from Mesopotamia to Alexandria, and came back with half a book. Ask what travels better along a relay, a bolt of silk or a text, and what a book needs that a commodity does not.

Substitute if any of the above collides: **byzantium-endures**, whose significance already argues that the eastern empire "was never the only carrier of Greek learning, which also moved through Syriac schoolrooms and, later, Arabic translation." Powerful, but it makes nearly the same anti-vault point as socratic-turn, so do not use both. Do not use han-mandate here: its state-funded-learning angle is real but thin against these four.

## Node-data flags

Report only. Do not edit the graph.

1. **Hook asserts a legend as fact.** "the caliph pays a book's weight in gold for translations" is the late anecdote in trap 2. The lesson must not repeat it straight. Optional replacement hook for Codex to consider, built on the documented failure instead: the greatest translator of the age crossed four provinces chasing one book by Galen and came home with half of it.
2. **Summary misplaces Ibn al-Haytham.** "Al-Khwarizmi's algebra ... Ibn al-Haytham's optics, hospitals and observatories" reads as one Baghdad list. He worked in Basra and Fatimid Cairo, c. 965 to c. 1040. See trap 4.
3. **Summary's "greatest translation movement in history"** is an unattributed superlative. Attribute it or drop it.
4. **Summary's "On Chinese paper"** leans on the Talas transfer, and the incoming edge from tang-golden-network states it flatly: "After Talas in 751, captured Chinese papermakers gave Islam its paper." That edge why is the least defensible sentence pointing at this node. See trap 3.
5. **Incoming edge from mulberry-page** ("cheap sheets in Baghdad made mass translation and record keeping affordable") is defensible but must not imply paper caused the movement. Translations began under al-Mansur in the 750s and 770s; the Baghdad paper manufactory is dated about 794 to 795.
6. **Incoming edge from recitation-in-the-desert** ("A faith centered on a book builds a civilization of books") is a rhetorical claim the lesson should complicate rather than echo. The leading translators were Nestorian Christians and a Sabian from Harran, and much of the material was pagan Greek, Sanskrit, and Middle Persian.
7. **Date and sort.** c. 820 and sort 820 are defensible (al-Ma'mun's reign, al-Khwarizmi's algebra), but the subject spans roughly two centuries. Give the span honestly in the significance rather than pretending to a year.
8. **Outgoing edge to first-universities** ("Arabic learning flows through Spain and Sicily into Latin schools") is accurate, but ornament-of-the-world (929) is the graph's designated Cordoba bridge and commentator-of-cordoba (1180) owns the Averroes transmission. Gesture at Toledo and Sicily in a clause; do not tell that story here.
9. **Existing incoming threadsOut are already honest and must be honored.** alexanders-comet says Alexander "supplied neither a library nor a straight pipeline to Baghdad"; socratic-turn says survival "means transformation, not storage in a sealed Western vault"; the iranshahr edge already hedges Gundeshapur. This lesson must not contradict any of the three.
