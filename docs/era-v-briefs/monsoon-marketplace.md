## Graph target

- Threads in the data: wealth only. The spec still requires two threads named in words. Craft and ideas are both physically
  present here (sewn hulls, kilns retooled for foreign taste, law, faith, navigational knowledge).
- Outgoing edges: treasure-fleets, iberian-wager, angkor-hydraulic. Incoming: gupta-zero (echoes), caliphate-lightning
  (enables), borobudur-mandala (enables).
- Era V neighbors that own adjacent material and must not be poached: sogdian-web (overland brokers), swahili-stone-towns at
  c. 1000 (East African urbanism), cairo-geniza at c. 1000 to 1100 (the merchant archive), roots-of-the-law (jurisprudence),
  borobudur-mandala (Indianization as local choice), song-economic-miracle.

Central claim: the Indian Ocean was not a space that trade crossed. It was a machine with a timetable. Two reversing wind
systems set departure and return dates for the whole basin, and everything social about this trade (long layovers, resident
diasporas, agents, credit, mixed-faith port quarters, order without a hegemon) grew out of obeying that calendar. No empire
owned the ocean. The calendar did.

## Preferred scene
**A. Al-Masudi's crossing from Qanbalu to Oman, 304 AH (916 to 917 CE).** Al-Masudi records that he made the crossing back
from Qanbalu on the East African coast in the ship of Ahmad and Abd al-Samad, kinsmen of Abd al-Rahim ibn Ja'far al-Sirafi,
and that his earlier voyages on that sea went out from Suhar with shipmasters of Siraf. A real named passenger, real named
owners, a real crossing, a real port of registry: the rarest thing in a trade lesson, a documented individual on a
documented voyage rather than a composite.

Documentation ledger:
- Attested: that al-Masudi says he sailed that route in that year in that ship, and names the owners as Sirafis; that he
  describes Qanbalu as holding Muslims alongside Zanj under a local ruling house; that East African exports (ivory,
  ambergris, leopard skins) fed the markets of Oman, India, and China; that the Zanj run was worked from Siraf and Oman.
- Reconstruction: any weather, smell, gesture, conversation, cargo item, or crew member. There is no manifest.
- Contested, say it once: Qanbalu is generally identified with Ras Mkumbuu on Pemba, or with Pemba itself, but that is an
  inference (Wynne-Jones and Fleisher 2021). Do not write "Pemba" flatly.
- Date math to state: the crossing is 916 or 917, about thirty-four years before the node's c. 950 anchor. Al-Masudi kept
  revising the Muruj al-dhahab until his death in 956, so the text sits inside the node's window even though the voyage sits
  just before it. He is not a contemporary of the Chola raid (1025) or of Yijing.
- One verification task for Fable: confirm the Qanbalu passage in a published translation before quoting or paraphrasing
  closely. The secondary confirmation used here states only that by 916 al-Masudi writes of a Muslim community at Qanbalu;
  the shipowners' names need one primary check.

Why it wins: a Gulf family firm working an African route, carrying a Baghdad-connected scholar, in a year fixed by the wind.
Nobody owns the ocean; everybody's names are on the hull.

**Alternate 1: Siraf, c. 950, the port that had to import its water.** Siraf sat on a hot, waterless Persian shore and was
in the ninth and tenth centuries the great Gulf entrepot for the China run. Whitehouse's excavations for the British
Institute of Persian Studies (1966 to 1973) uncovered the congregational mosque, houses, bazaars, and a Sasanian-era
predecessor beneath, plus a very large body of imported ceramics; a Chinese jar lay under the Friday Mosque floor, and some
seventeen thousand sherds from the site, including Tang wares, are held by the British Museum. Ledger: site, mosque,
imported wares, and wealth are attested, any individual merchant is a composite, and the often-repeated tall teak merchant
houses come from tenth-century Arabic geographers rather than the trenches, so attribute them if used. Weakness: no named
person.

**Alternate 2: the Belitung cargo (evidence, not cold open).** A sewn-plank ship of West Asian construction, about eighteen
meters, sank in Indonesian waters carrying nearly sixty thousand objects loaded in China: Changsha bowls in the tens of
thousands, Yue green wares, northern white wares, gold and silver, and three blue-and-white dishes whose cobalt was possibly
mined in Iran and whose lozenge motif suited Middle Eastern taste. Ledger: the objects are real and published, the sewn
construction is real, the voyage is wholly reconstructed, and the wreck is 110 to 125 years earlier than this node with its
own date still argued (trap 2). Use it in significance as proof that one hull ran the long haul, say the date math out loud,
and do not open on it.

## Story movement
Open on the crossing, inside the constraint: the wind, not the merchant, chooses the date, and a ship that misses one season
waits for the other. Then move outward from the single hull to the calendar it obeys. The ninth-century Accounts of China
and India gives the schedule concretely: down the Gulf from Ubulla or Siraf in summer, out of Muscat or Suhar in September
or October on the northeast monsoon, about a month to Kulam Mali on the Malabar coast, about a month onward to Kalah Bar,
then the straits and up to Canton by spring, the return begun between October and December: about eighteen months and
sixteen thousand kilometers for the round trip. That number is the engine: at that tempo a merchant does not travel, he
lives in installments.

Then let the layover do the work. Because the wind will not turn for months, a trader either winters in a foreign port or
leaves behind someone who will. That single fact produces the resident diaspora, the local agent, the partnership, credit
that outlives the voyage, the mixed-faith quarter, the marriage, the second family, the mosque built out of someone else's
stone.

Pivot where the reader feels the question: this ocean's order is made by people who cannot enforce it. No navy patrols the
water, no court has jurisdiction at both ends, and the goods arrive anyway. End the story where the mechanism becomes
visible and unexplained.

Governing image, used lightly and returned to at the hinge and the end: a door in the wind that opens twice a year, and the
human world that grows in the months when it is shut. Avoid Sinbad atmosphere, spice-scented bazaars, the word dhow (trap
3), and any sentence implying the ocean was tranquil.

## Significance spine
1. **Evidence, and what each kind lies about.** Three independent bodies: Arabic sailing and marvel literature written by
   and for men of Siraf and Basra (the Accounts of China and India, 851, continued by Abu Zayd al-Sirafi after he moved to
   Basra in 303 AH, 915 to 916; al-Masudi's Muruj al-dhahab, revised until his death in 956; the collection known as the
   Wonders of India, latest datable story 953); excavated ports at both ends; and one excavated ship. Say plainly that the
   marvel literature exaggerates, that ports keep durable goods and lose the perishable bulk, and that one wreck is one
   wreck.
2. **Mechanism: the wind as an institution.** The monsoon reverses seasonally, so the ocean is a scheduled space rather than
   a continuously open one. The consequences are structural, not decorative: fixed sailing windows force layovers, layovers
   force residence, and residence produces agents, partnerships, credit, intermarriage, and communities of several faiths on
   one waterfront. This is the beat the lesson exists for. Contrast the overland relay, whose binding constraint is distance
   and protection rather than a calendar.
3. **Mechanism: the through-voyage, and bulk.** The Belitung cargo shows a single West Asian sewn hull loaded in China with
   tens of thousands of pieces from one kiln region. Sea carriage made bulk cheap in a way relay caravans never approached,
   which is why ceramics and timber travel by water and why one ocean carries both a scholar and sixty thousand bowls. Craft
   belongs here: Chinese kilns made shapes and motifs for buyers they never met, using cobalt possibly mined in Iran. Demand
   crossed an ocean and retooled a workshop.
4. **The named debate (one, honestly presented).** Recommended: was the pre-Portuguese Indian Ocean a peaceful sea? K. M.
   Panikkar and Fernand Braudel described its maritime custom as having always been extremely pacific, with complete freedom
   of trade, and that picture still drives popular accounts of an ocean spoiled only by European gunnery. Sebastian Prange
   (American Historical Review, 2011) argues from period sources that seaborne raiding was persistent and often
   community-embedded from the twelfth century on, and that treating piracy as an aberration hides the ocean's real
   politics. Rajendra Chola's raids point the same way. Honest formulation: this ocean had no hegemon, not that it had no
   violence. Alternates: who wrote the Wonders of India (trap 1), when the Belitung ship sank (trap 2), or what Srivijaya
   was and where its center lay (Coedes identified it in 1918; Palembang versus Jambi is still argued).
5. **Consequence: governance without a governor.** Nobody taxed the ocean; people taxed its choke points. Srivijaya lived
   off the Sumatran straits and, according to the Chinese Zhufan zhi, attacked ships that tried to slip past its ports
   without paying. Tansen Sen argues this provoked Rajendra Chola's attacks of 1017 and 1025, retaliation for Srivijayan
   interference with direct trade between southern India and Song China, and Rajendra's Tanjavur inscription lists the ports
   struck. A raid is not an annexation: the Cholas did not become the ocean's landlord, and trade rerouted rather than
   stopped.
6. **Consequence: shocks absorbed, not fatal.** The system took real blows in this period and kept working by moving.
   Guangzhou was sacked in 878 to 879 during the Huang Chao rebellion, reported by Abu Zayd with a death toll of 120,000
   foreign residents (al-Masudi says 200,000), interrupting the China run; the 977 earthquake and a longer harbour
   degradation ended Siraf's primacy, with Gulf traffic shifting toward Kish. The network survived both because no single
   node was the network.
7. **Inheritance.** Land on what still works this way. Shipping still carries the overwhelming majority of world trade by
   volume, still runs on schedules, agents, and credit rather than on sovereignty, and still produces port cities whose
   populations do not match the country around them. This is not the ancestor of European empire but of the ordinary,
   unowned, scheduled commercial ocean Europeans later tried to turn into property. Return to the opening image without
   moralizing.

## Factual traps
1. **Trap:** The Book of the Wonders of India was written by Buzurg ibn Shahriyar of Ramhormuz, a Persian sea captain,
   around 950. **Truth:** The attribution rests on the title page of a single manuscript (Aya Sofya 3306) copied in 644 AH,
   1246 CE, three centuries later. Jean-Charles Ducene, working from extensive citations preserved by the fourteenth-century
   Mamluk encyclopedist al-Umari, argues the compiler was Abu Imran Musa ibn Rabah al-Awsi al-Sirafi, a documented scholar
   of Siraf and Basra connections who lived until 377 AH, 987 CE. The latest datable story is 953, so the tenth-century
   dating stands; the authorship does not. Write "the collection known as the Wonders of India" and name the dispute.
   **Source:** Ducene, Afriques 6 (2015).
2. **Trap:** The Belitung ship sank in 826. **Truth:** 826 is a terminus post quem from one inscribed Changsha bowl reading
   baoli ernian qiyue shiliu ri, the sixteenth day of the seventh lunar month of the second year of Baoli, under Emperor
   Jingzong (reigned 824 to 827). The 208 coins give an independent terminus post quem of 759, and one bronze mirror aboard
   was made in 759, long before the voyage. Radiocarbon gives broad ranges (710 to 890 for the ship's timber). Regina Krahl
   argues from the Yue green ware sequence for the 840s; Wilson and Flecker conclude only that the wreck belongs to the
   second quarter of the ninth century. **Source:** source 2.
3. **Trap:** These ships were dhows. **Truth:** Dhow is a European collective label, most likely from Swahili daw, applied
   from outside to a huge variety of Indian Ocean craft; neither Arabic nor Indian Ocean sailors used one collective term,
   they named ship types individually. The attested fact is better anyway: the long-haul hulls of this period were sewn,
   planks stitched with cordage rather than nailed, as the Belitung ship shows. **Source:** Asian Civilisations Museum, Tang
   Shipwreck gallery.
4. **Trap:** The Greek navigator Hippalus discovered the monsoon route. **Truth:** The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea
   credits Hippalus and Roman tradition follows it, but Indian, Arabian, and East African mariners worked these winds long
   before; Hippalus is at best the first Greek in the record to use knowledge others already held, and the name may attach
   to the wind as much as to a man. It matters here because it smuggles in the idea that the ocean waits to be discovered by
   outsiders, exactly the error the iberian-wager edge exists to refuse. **Source:** the Periplus, read as a Greek
   merchant's document rather than as neutral history.
5. **Trap:** Horses were a staple cargo of the tenth-century monsoon trade. **Truth:** The well-documented
   Gulf-to-South-India war-horse trade is thirteenth to fifteenth century: Marco Polo and Wassaf on the thousands shipped
   annually into Ma'bar, and the contract by which Jamal al-Din of Kish undertook to send 1,400 horses to the Pandya king.
   Scattered earlier evidence exists; nothing supports horses as a headline commodity at c. 950. Also a node-data flag.
   **Source:** the scholarship on Bahri horses and the Kayal trade.
6. **Trap:** The Cairo Geniza lets us watch this trade in detail. **Truth:** The India Book documents cluster roughly 1080
   to 1160, that is 130 to 210 years after this node, and they belong to cairo-geniza in the graph. Do not import a Geniza
   merchant, letter, or partnership into a 950 scene; if the lesson wants the texture of long-distance partnership, take it
   from the Arabic sailing literature. **Source flag:** Goitein and Friedman, India Traders of the Middle Ages (Brill,
   2008), not fetched for this brief; verify before citing.
7. **Trap:** 120,000 foreign merchants died when Guangzhou fell in 878 to 879, proving the size of the community there.
   **Truth:** Abu Zayd gives 120,000 and al-Masudi 200,000, and Tang Chinese sources record Huang Chao's occupation without
   recording a massacre of foreigners; the figures are the standard hyperbole of distant atrocity. What survives is better
   anyway: Guangzhou held a resident foreign population of Muslims, Jews, Christians, and Zoroastrians large enough that its
   destruction was news in Basra. **Source:** source 1, read critically.
8. **Trap:** Siraf was killed by the earthquake of 977. **Truth:** The earthquake is real and the port never recovered its
   scale, but coins show continued reduced activity afterward, the shift of Gulf traffic to Kish is an eleventh and
   twelfth-century process, and geoarchaeological work on the harbour argues for relative sea-level rise and coastal erosion
   degrading the anchorage across roughly 850 to 1000, so decline was underway before the shock. One sentence of honesty is
   enough. **Source:** Siraf harbour geoarchaeology in the Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports (2020); authorship not
   fetched, verify before naming.
9. **Trap:** This was globalization before Europe, in Janet Abu-Lughod's sense. **Truth:** Abu-Lughod's Before European
   Hegemony analyzes a world system of 1250 to 1350, three centuries after this node. K. N. Chaudhuri's Trade and
   Civilisation in the Indian Ocean runs from the rise of Islam to 1750 and is the safer frame here. Use the framings,
   attribute them to their actual centuries, and do not let a 1300s model narrate a 950s ocean.
10. **Trap:** Chinese ships carried the China trade. **Truth:** The only excavated long-haul carrier of this era, the
    Belitung ship, is a West Asian sewn-plank vessel that loaded in China; large Chinese ships become major Indian Ocean
    players later. Keep to what the wreck shows, or date the Song-era shift from a source this brief did not check.

## Callback design
Only earlier lessons are eligible. The four ranked below were read in full; the two Era V candidates at the end are
conditional, because their prose does not exist yet.

**1. silk-road-pulse (strongest, read in full).** That lesson's governing mechanism is the relay: a bolt of silk moves hand
to hand, each trader knowing only his neighbor, and connection means overlapping local circuits rather than a road. Angle:
what changes when the medium changes. One monsoon hull could carry an entire cargo the length of the route, so the sea
allowed through-voyages and bulk the relay never could, yet it imposed a constraint the land did not, a calendar, and still
produced no owner, no single protector, and brokers at every port. Ask which mechanism is closer to a modern supply chain,
and make the answer distinguish continuity of route from control of route.

**2. phoenician-web (read in full).** That lesson makes repeated return the whole social technology of a maritime network:
sanctuaries, interpreters, resident agents, the disputed ingot left ashore to guarantee a next season. Here, when the wind
fixes the return date rather than the merchant's judgment, the same trust problem is solved by residence instead of by
return, and the diaspora quarter replaces the unfinished bargain on the beach. One question answered by two different
constraints, which is why it rewards both readings.

**3. invention-of-money (read in full).** Its mechanism is verification compression: the stamp moves part of the checking to
before the transaction. The monsoon ocean is the limit case: eighteen months and several jurisdictions separate a payment
from its settlement, and no coin closes that gap. Angle: what must exist besides money when no court has authority at both
ends. Keep the answer to agents, partnership, reputation, and legal opinion; do not import dated Geniza practice.

**4. bronze-web (read in full; the orchestrator's first suggestion).** Angle: what breaks a web versus what a web survives.
Uluburun's network had a palace layer, and around 1200 BCE much of that layer ended. The monsoon system had no palace layer
to lose, so this era's shocks (a sacked Chinese port, a failing Gulf harbour, a Chola raid) moved traffic rather than ending
it. Ranked fourth only because bronze-web is already the callback target of phoenician-web, silk-road-pulse, and
invention-of-money.

**Conditional alternates, for the orchestrator to judge once the Era V lessons exist:** golden-sahara offers the finest
pairing in the era if its text supports it, a sea of sand beside a sea of water, each with a season, a convoy discipline, a
tax at the edge, and a diaspora doing the trusting; tang-golden-network offers the Guangzhou waterfront as the seaward twin
of the Tang capital, with 878 as the hinge. Neither could be read.

## Node-data flags
Precise, for Codex. Do not edit the file.
1. **summary, "Srivijaya and Chola taxed the straits" is wrong about the Chola.** Srivijaya taxed the straits. The Cholas
   were a Coromandel land power that raided Srivijayan ports in 1025 (and, on Sen's reading, 1017) and briefly held Kadaram;
   they never ran a straits toll regime. Repair: "Srivijaya taxed the straits until a Chola fleet burned through them in
   1025."
2. **summary, "horses" is an anachronistic headline cargo for c. 950.** The documented Gulf-to-South-India horse trade is
   thirteenth to fifteenth century. Repair: timber, aromatics, ivory, or glass, all attested here.
3. **summary, "dhows" is a European collective term of much later coinage.** Suggested repair: "sewn-plank ships riding
   seasonal monsoons," which is both accurate and more concrete.
4. **summary, "porcelain" is loose.** The Belitung bulk cargo is Changsha painted stoneware; true porcelain sits among the
   northern white wares. Not worth changing, but do not call fifty thousand Changsha bowls porcelain.
5. **hook, "the winds blow reliably one way half the year and back the other half."** Defensible as the node's compression,
   but the lesson should not repeat "reliably" unqualified: monsoons fail, the height of the southwest monsoon was dangerous
   rather than convenient, and the sailing windows were narrower than the seasons.
6. **threads: ['wealth'] only.** Craft and ideas are structurally present in the material. A second thread would improve the
   chart's honesty here. Codex's call.
7. **edge to angkor-hydraulic** ("Indian religion, script, and kingship sailed east on these winds"). The route claim is
   fine, but the Indic transmission it describes is largely a first-millennium process well before c. 950, and
   borobudur-mandala (c. 800 to 830) already owns Indianization as local choice. The threadsOut sentence should present the
   sea lane as carrying an older transmission, not one that happened at 950.
8. **Chronology cross-check.** Yijing studied at Srivijaya 671 to 695, about 260 years before this node, so he is background
   and not a contemporary. Belitung is 110 to 125 years earlier, the Chola raid 75 years later, Siraf's earthquake 27 years
   later, and al-Masudi died in 956. State any gap you rely on.
9. **Map crowding, already logged in docs/graph-expansion-365.md:** monsoon-marketplace (x 76, sort 950) sits close to
   flying-man (x 74, sort 1025) in the sasia band. QA only.
