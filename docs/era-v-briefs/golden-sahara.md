# Lesson brief: golden-sahara

## Graph target

- Node id golden-sahara. Title "Gold Roads of the Sahara". Date field c. 900 CE, sort 900. Region africa, x 44.
- Threads in data: wealth only. The forge spec still requires at least two of the four threads named in words, so
  power and ideas must be earned inside the significance section. See Node-data flags.
- Outgoing graph edges: mansa-musa-hajj (transforms), middle-passage (collides).
- Incoming edges the lesson should feel behind it: caliphate-lightning, baqt-of-nubia, bantu-and-aksum,
  city-without-citadel (Jenne-jeno), meroe-of-the-queens, carthage-ledger, green-sahara, kingdom-of-kerma.

Central claim: the Sahara was not a wall that the camel finally broke. It was an expensive sea whose ports were
oases, and the West African states at its southern shore grew rich by taxing the crossing, not by owning the gold
or the salt. The gold is West African, the carriers are Saharan, the coin at the far end is Islamic, and no actor
controls more than one stretch of the chain. Do not write a lesson about Africa's contact with the outside world.
Write one in which West Africa is the origin and the Mediterranean is downstream.

## Preferred scene

Open at Tadmekka (the site of Essouk, in the Adrar des Ifoghas of northeastern Mali) around 900 CE, at the moment
gold dust becomes money. A worker melts gold into a row of shallow cavities in a fired clay mould, casting small
blank discs. The coins carry no ruler's name, no mint, no date. Pure gold, weighed, anonymous, made to travel.

### Documentation ledger

Attested by the sources:

- Three ceramic coin moulds excavated at Essouk-Tadmekka, radiocarbon dated to roughly 850 to 950 CE, with gold
  residues in the cavities. Published as Nixon, Rehren and Guerra, Antiquity 85 (2011), which describes them as
  the first concrete evidence of coin production in pre-colonial West Africa.
- The coins were cast as blanks, not struck with a device. The excavators read them as trade instruments rather
  than political statements, meltable or restrikeable at a northern mint.
- Al-Bakri, writing in 1067 to 1068, says the dinars of Tadmekka are called "bald" because they are pure gold with
  no stamp. Text and object agree, which is rare and is the whole reason to build the scene here. Tadmekka itself
  was a real market town on the caravan road toward Gao and the Niger bend, occupied across this period.
- A related paper (Rehren and Nixon, Journal of Archaeological Science, 2014) reports crucibles from the same
  context read as gold refining with crushed glass as flux. I could not open the publisher record: treat the
  glass-flux detail as flagged, use it only if independently confirmed, and keep it out of the scene if not.

Reconstruction, and must be labeled as such in storyContext: any named person (Tadmekka's goldworkers are
anonymous; the worker is a composite drawn from the excavated moulds, crucibles and the town's archaeology); the
workshop's sounds, smells, light, weather, any waiting buyer; any statement about what the maker thought the coin
was for. The excavators infer the caravan trade. The maker left no note. No named person is available at Tadmekka.
Two real named people belong elsewhere in the lesson: al-Bakri himself, and Tunka Manin, whom al-Bakri reports as
ruling Ghana in his own day, having come to the throne in 1063 after his maternal uncle Basi.

### Alternate 1 (strong, and the natural second panel)

Al-Bakri in al-Andalus in 1067 to 1068, assembling the kingdom of Ghana out of other men's reports. Documented:
the Kitab al-Masalik wa-l-Mamalik and its date; that he lived in Córdoba and Almería and never crossed the Sahara;
that he worked from merchants' accounts and earlier geographers including the now lost al-Warraq; and the content
of the Ghana passage, which is extraordinarily specific. Two towns six miles apart with continuous habitation
between them. Twelve mosques in the Muslim town, with salaried imams, muezzins, jurists and scholars. The king's
town called al-Ghaba, with groves and thickets holding the tombs of the kings, and a mosque by the court for
visiting Muslims. The king's interpreters, his treasurer and most of his ministers Muslim while the king is not.
An audience under a domed pavilion: ten horses in gold cloth, ten pages with gold-decorated shields and swords,
vassal kings' sons with gold plaited into their hair, pedigree dogs in collars of gold and silver studded with
metal balls, the audience announced by a drum called duba cut from a long hollow log. Subjects greet the king by
kneeling and sprinkling dust on their heads; Muslims greet him by clapping. One dinar on each donkey load of salt
entering and two on each leaving, five mithqals on a load of copper, nuggets reserved to the king and only dust
left to the people, and the best gold coming from Ghiyaru, eighteen days off. Reconstruction: al-Bakri's room, his
informants' faces, his doubts, any moment of composition.

Recommended structure: Tadmekka as the cold open, al-Bakri as the second panel, object first and text second. The
reader meets the mechanism in metal before the famous description, and the essay can ask honestly what a man in
Córdoba could and could not know. It also fixes a chronology problem: the node is dated c. 900 and the moulds are
c. 850 to 950, while al-Bakri is 1068. If al-Bakri opens, the lesson drifts 168 years off its own node.

### Alternate 2 (usable, weaker)

The Almoravid attack on Awdaghust in 1054 to 1055, which al-Bakri describes and criticizes as the work of men
practicing a debased Islam. Vivid and documented, but violent, off-date, and it pulls the lesson toward the
Almoravid question before the trade mechanism has been built. Do not build a salt-mine scene from Ibn Battuta: his
account of houses roofed in salt slabs is 1352, four and a half centuries late, and describes Taghaza, not
attested by that name until about 1275. See trap 3.

## Story movement

Panel one, Tadmekka, c. 900. Gold dust arrives from the south in quills and leather, is weighed, refined, and cast
into blanks. Nothing on the disc says who made it or where. Let the reader feel that absence as a decision rather
than a lack of skill: the blank is built to be acceptable to someone whose ruler you do not know and whose script
you do not read. Value travels; authority does not. End the panel with the blanks going north into country where
the water is a week apart.

Panel two, al-Andalus, 1068. A learned man who has never seen the desert writes down a court he will never enter,
on the authority of men who have stood in it. Give the reader the twelve mosques, the Muslim treasurer serving a
non-Muslim king, the gold on the dogs' collars, the drum. Then turn the lens: this is hearsay, carefully
collected, and hearsay collected carefully is one of the real ways the medieval world knew itself. What the
passage actually reveals is not splendor but a tax table. One dinar in on a donkey load of salt, two dinars out,
five mithqals on copper, nothing recorded on gold. Ghana is a tollbooth on a desert, not a mine.

Pivot into significance where the reader can see that the king in the pavilion did not own the goldfields, that
Ghiyaru was eighteen days away, and that the system turned on a commodity nobody in the north thought about: salt.
Governing image, used lightly and returned to once at the end: the unstamped coin, valuable everywhere precisely
because it declares nothing.

## Significance spine

1. Evidence. Name what we have and what we do not. Excavated moulds, crucibles, coins, beads, imported ceramics
   and the sites themselves (Tadmekka, Awdaghust/Tegdaoust, Sijilmasa, Koumbi Saleh, Gao). Arabic geography
   written almost entirely by men who never crossed: al-Fazari's late eighth century phrase about the land of
   gold, surviving only because a later author quoted it; al-Khwarizmi around 830; al-Bakri in 1068. No West
   African document from this period, no located royal town, and a contested identification of Koumbi Saleh with
   al-Bakri's city of Ghana. Say this once, plainly, and move on.
2. Mechanism one: the desert as a sea with ports. Water, not distance, sets the route. Sijilmasa in southern
   Morocco (founded 757 or 758) at the northern shore; Awdaghust and Tadmekka at the southern; the Niger towns
   behind them. A caravan is a capital-intensive voyage needing guides, guarantees, credit and rights to water.
   Power belongs here: Ghana's revenue is a border levy, and the Almoravid attack on Awdaghust in 1054 to 1055 is
   an attempt to hold a port, not to conquer a hinterland.
3. Mechanism two: why salt for gold is not a joke. Gold is abundant in Bambuk and useless as a nutrient; salt is
   scarce in the wet savanna and necessary for people and herds. Two commodities, each cheap at one end and dear
   at the other, each dense in value per kilogram, is the exact profile a desert crossing can carry. Ghana taxed
   salt, not gold, and reserved nuggets to the king while dust circulated, which al-Bakri read as price control.
4. Named debate: the Almoravid conquest of Ghana in 1076. A historiographical detective story, not a hedge. David
   Conrad and Humphrey Fisher argued in 1982 and 1983 that no source, Arabic or oral, unambiguously reports a
   conquest. Pekka Masonen and Fisher traced in 1996 how the story entered the literature through nineteenth
   century readings (William Desborough Cooley's Negroland, 1841) of ambiguous passages in al-Zuhri and Ibn
   Khaldun, then hardened by repetition. Koumbi Saleh has no destruction horizon. What the evidence supports
   instead: Almoravid pressure on the northern ports, route realignment, Islam arriving with merchants over
   generations, and a Soninke polity weakening from causes including drought and lost tolls rather than one sack.
   Say that the argument continues: Sheryl Burkhalter argued in 1992 that the revisionists read silences too
   confidently.
5. Consequence: the gold goes somewhere. West African gold fed Islamic mints, above all at Sijilmasa, and later
   the Almoravid dinar, whose reputation rested on that supply. Tadmekka's blanks are the physical form of an
   export designed to be restruck by whoever received it. The gold's political meaning was made in the north, its
   economic meaning in the south. That asymmetry deserves a sentence, not a lecture.
6. Ideas: Islam moved along the same roads, but as a merchant's religion first. Al-Bakri's Ghana in 1068 is a
   two-town arrangement in which Muslim jurists, scribes and treasurers work for a king who does not share their
   faith and whose ancestors lie in a sacred grove. That is not a halfway house toward conversion. It is a durable
   working arrangement: literacy, contract law and credit imported as a professional service. Royal conversions
   came later and layered (Takrur's War Jabi by about 1040; the Muslim rulers on the imported marble stelae at
   Gao-Saney belong to the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries).
7. Inheritance. Close on the unstamped coin. Modern trade still runs on instruments engineered to be accepted by
   people who do not recognize the issuer, and on the fact that where a commodity is dug is rarely where its value
   is named. Do not moralize forward to the Atlantic trade: the middle-passage edge exists, and one clean sentence
   about ships later bypassing the desert is enough.

## Factual traps

1. Trap: the Almoravids conquered Ghana in 1076 and forced its conversion. Truth: no source unambiguously reports
   a conquest, the claim was built in nineteenth century scholarship out of ambiguous lines in al-Zuhri and Ibn
   Khaldun, and no destruction layer has been found. Current framing is pressure, route realignment and gradual
   weakening. Source: Conrad and Fisher, History in Africa 9 (1982); Masonen and Fisher, History in Africa 23 (1996).
2. Trap: the camel arrived and the Sahara suddenly became crossable, on a date. Truth: camel remains in the
   Maghreb go back to roughly the fifth to third centuries BCE (Carthage), the Garamantes of the Fezzan ran
   Saharan networks before large camel caravans, imported glass beads reached Kissi in northern Burkina Faso in
   third to seventh century CE contexts, and Saharan copper was arriving at Jenne-jeno by about 400 CE. Camel
   transport made high-volume regular caravan trade economic across the later first millennium CE; it did not open
   a closed door one morning. Source: Mattingly, "The Garamantes and the Origins of Saharan Trade" (2017). The
   repo's own city-without-citadel node already states that Saharan copper reached Jenne-jeno by 400.
3. Trap: Taghaza, the town built of salt blocks, supplied Ghana. Truth: the name Taghaza is not attested until
   about 1275 (al-Qazwini, as Taghara), and the salt-architecture description is Ibn Battuta's from 1352.
   Al-Bakri's eleventh century salt mine is Tatentāl, twenty days from Sijilmasa, and coastal Awlil supplied slab
   salt up the Senegal. Use those, or say "Saharan salt mines" without a name.
4. Trap: Koumbi Saleh is the capital al-Bakri described. Truth: it is the best candidate, and the excavations
   (Thomassey and Mauny 1949 to 1950, Berthier 1975 to 1981) revealed a substantial stone-built town with a large
   mosque, but no inscription ties it to Ghana, the royal town with its palace and burial mounds has never been
   located, and the identification is increasingly disputed, partly because al-Idrisi's twelfth century capital
   sits on a river. Population figures of 15,000 to 20,000 rest on thin ground; state the uncertainty, not a number.
5. Trap: Ghana grew rich on its gold mines. Truth: it grew rich on tolls. Al-Bakri records one dinar on a donkey
   load of salt entering and two on one leaving, five mithqals on a load of copper, and no levy on gold; nuggets
   were reserved to the king while gold dust circulated. The best gold, he says, came from Ghiyaru, eighteen days
   from the king's town, that is, outside it. Source: al-Bakri in Levtzion and Hopkins, pp. 79 to 81.
6. Trap: Bambuk and Bure both fed Ghana. Truth: Bambuk, on the Senegal and Falémé, is the goldfield of this
   period. The Bure fields on the upper Niger opened later, in the eleventh or twelfth century, and belong to the
   story that runs into Mali. Pair the fields only if the lesson dates them.
7. Trap: gold was exchanged by silent trade, buyers and sellers never meeting. Truth: the story descends from
   Herodotus on the Carthaginians (and probably describes northwest, not West, Africa), then reappears in Arabic
   and European authors as literary inheritance rather than observation. Historians treat it as a topos. Source:
   P. F. de Moraes Farias, "Silent Trade: Myth and Historical Evidence," History in Africa 1 (1974). If the lesson
   uses it at all, use it as a story that travelled, which is itself a good line.
8. Trap: al-Bakri saw Ghana. Truth: he worked in al-Andalus from merchants' reports and earlier geographers,
   finishing his book in 460 AH, 1067 to 1068 CE. His army figure, 200,000 men including more than 40,000 archers,
   is rhetorical scale, not a muster roll. Treat every number in the passage as a claim with a chain of custody.
9. Trap: this gold minted the coins of Europe. Truth: for this node's period it minted Islamic coin, at Sijilmasa
   and beyond, and later the Almoravid dinar. Latin Europe had almost no gold coinage between the Carolingian
   period and 1252, when Genoa and Florence began striking gold. Date the European half if you use it.
10. Trap: ancient Ghana is modern Ghana, or Ghana was founded around 300 CE. Truth: the polity, called Wagadu by
    the Soninke while Ghana was a royal title, lay in present-day southeastern Mauritania and western Mali,
    hundreds of miles northwest of the modern republic, which took the name in 1957. The earliest datable notice
    is al-Fazari's late eighth century phrase, surviving only in later quotation, then al-Khwarizmi around 830.
    Earlier founding dates and dynastic chronologies come from later oral tradition and colonial-era scholarship.


## Callback design

Exactly two callbacks are required. Ranked candidates; take the top two.

1. invention-of-money (recommended). Angle: the mark and the blank. That lesson's mechanism is verification
   compression, a stamp doing some of the checking before the transaction begins, and it insists the mark did not
   create trust. Tadmekka's coins are the inverse experiment: deliberately bald, no device, no issuer, no date,
   valuable because they are weighed pure metal any mint in any jurisdiction can restrike. Ask why a merchant
   crossing between authorities might prefer a coin that claims nothing. The answer teaches that a stamp is a
   local promise, and that the further a thing travels the less a local promise is worth.
2. bantu-and-aksum (recommended). Angle: two African trade frontiers, two kinds of archive. Aksum's king announced
   his new god on the gold every trader had to handle, addressing foreign merchants in Greek on the rims. Ghana's
   king kept a sacred grove and a drum, and hired Muslims to keep his accounts and speak for him. Both courts had
   to be legible to merchants of another faith and language, and each solved it differently: one put the court's
   claim on the money, the other left the money silent and imported the scribes. Then reuse that lesson's second
   layer: an official object proves what a court wished announced, never what a household believed, and al-Bakri's
   page proves only what a careful man in Córdoba was told.
3. northmen-networks (strong, but verify against the finished text first). Angle: the Islamic monetary system
   pulled bullion from two opposite frontiers in the same decades. Abbasid silver moved north into Scandinavia and
   was buried there by the hoard; West African gold moved north to be struck as dinars. In both cases metal moved
   toward the mint and the mint's authority did not move back. If that lesson centers raiding and state formation
   rather than silver, drop this and take candidate 4.
4. silk-road-pulse (reserve). Angle: relay versus crossing. That lesson's key mechanism is that almost nobody
   travelled the whole way and every hand knew only its neighbors. The Sahara partly inverts it, because in the
   deep desert there is nothing to hand off to and a caravan must go end to end, then restores it at both shores,
   where Wangara traders bring dust up from the goldfields and Maghribi merchants take the metal onward. Ask what
   a network looks like when one link in the middle cannot be subdivided. Reserve 5, thematically close, is
   phoenician-web: ports and repeated return, intermediaries who own neither end of the trade.

Recommended pairing: invention-of-money and bantu-and-aksum. Place the money callback fourth (mechanism sharpened
by contrast) and the Africa callback fifth (synthesis, widest view).

## Node-data flags

1. The hook appears to invert the direction of trade. "Camel caravans cross a sea of sand carrying salt north and
   gold south" reads most naturally as salt travelling north and gold travelling south. The actual flow is the
   reverse: Saharan salt moved south into the Sahel, West African gold moved north to the Maghreb and the
   Mediterranean. This is the most important flag in this brief. The lesson must state the direction correctly
   even though the hook does not, and Codex should rewrite the hook. Suggested replacement, Codex's call: "Camel
   caravans cross a sea of sand carrying salt south and gold north, and empires rise at the ports of that sea."
2. Summary overclaims the camel. "The camel made the Sahara crossable" is contradicted by the Garamantes, by third
   to seventh century imported beads at Kissi, and by the repo's own city-without-citadel node, which says Saharan
   copper reached Jenne-jeno by 400 CE. The lesson should say the camel made regular high-volume caravan trade
   economic, and should not imply a sealed desert before it.
3. Summary contains an anachronism. "West African gold, which minted the coins of the Islamic world and Europe
   alike" is right for Islamic mints in this period and premature for Europe, which had essentially no gold
   coinage between the Carolingians and the Genoese and Florentine issues of 1252. The node spans Ghana and Mali,
   so the claim is salvageable, but only if the lesson dates the European half explicitly.
4. Node date versus evidentiary center of gravity. The node is dated c. 900 CE and the richest text (al-Bakri) is
   1068. The Tadmekka moulds (c. 850 to 950) sit exactly on the node date, which is the main structural reason to
   open there. The lesson must not present al-Bakri's court description as a description of the year 900.
5. Threads. The node carries wealth only, but the forge spec requires at least two threads named in words. Power
   (border tolls, control of ports, Almoravid pressure on Awdaghust) and ideas (Islam arriving as a professional
   service before it arrived as royal faith) both belong in significance. A second pigment would be defensible.
6. Edge why to mansa-musa-hajj: "Ghana's routes mature into Mali's golden imperial age" glosses a real
   discontinuity, including the Sosso interval and a shift of supply toward Bure. Acceptable as an edge label, but
   the lesson's threadsOut sentence should not imply an unbroken handover.
7. Edge why from caliphate-lightning: "Islam and its trade cross the desert with the camel caravans." True as a
   direction, but the lesson should correct the implied speed. Al-Bakri's Ghana in 1068 still has a non-Muslim
   king with Muslim ministers, and the earliest attested Sahelian royal conversions are Takrur under War Jabi by
   about 1040 and the Muslim rulers on the Gao-Saney stelae at the end of the twelfth century. No contradiction
   found in the node's date field, region, sort value, or the middle-passage edge.
