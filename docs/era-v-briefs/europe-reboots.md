Central claim: 800 is neither a restoration nor a rubble field. A Frankish king reassembles rule out of estates, monasteries, bishops, counts, oaths, books, and reused Roman marble, then borrows a title that no longer has an apparatus attached to it. The title outlives the machine; the machine has to be improvised from other materials. That gap between name and apparatus is the lesson.

## Preferred scene

**Two days at St Peter's, 23 and 25 December 800.** Not the crowning alone: the crowning plus the oath that came forty-eight hours before it.

On 25 April 799 Pope Leo III was ambushed during a procession in Rome by a faction tied to his predecessor's family, who tried to blind him and cut out his tongue, the mutilations that would disqualify him from office. He escaped, crossed the Alps, and reached Charlemagne at Paderborn; he was escorted back to Rome that November. Charlemagne arrived in Rome in late November 800 and held a council on 1 December at which both sides were heard. No one would try a pope. On 23 December Leo cleared himself by a purgation oath before the assembly, and his accusers were exiled. On Christmas Day, in Old St Peter's, he crowned Charlemagne, the congregation acclaimed him, and (Liber Pontificalis, Royal Frankish Annals) the pope performed a prostration or genuflection before him, the annals adding that this followed the custom of ancient emperors. Charles the Younger was anointed king in the same ceremony.

That is the mechanism in one room, and it needs no invented character. A pope who has just had to swear he is not a criminal makes an emperor; the emperor is made by the man who saved him from mutilation. Legitimacy here is not inherited. It is manufactured on the spot out of two parties' need for each other.

### Documentation ledger

Attested: the 799 attack, the flight to Paderborn, the return, the December 800 synod, the purgation oath of 23 December, the coronation, the acclamation, the anointing of Charles the Younger. Sources: Liber Pontificalis (Life of Leo III), Annales regni Francorum (which dates the Frankish year from Christmas and so files the coronation under 801), Annales Laureshamenses, Einhard ch. 28. The acclamation formula is preserved in the Liber Pontificalis: "Karolo piissimo Augusto a Deo coronato magno et pacifico imperatori vita et victoria." The papal gesture is attested but the Latin verb does not settle prostration against genuflection, so choose neutral wording or say so. The Lorsch annalist supplies the legal argument: the imperial name had ceased among the Greeks, who had a woman ruling, so the title stood vacant and Charlemagne, holding Rome and the imperial seats, could take it.

Reconstruction, and must be labelled in storyContext if used: cold, incense, candle count, crowd noise, any face, any interior thought, any spoken line not in the sources. There is no eyewitness transcript, and the four narratives disagree about sequence and initiative.

Named real people available: Leo III, Charlemagne, Charles the Younger, and Alcuin by letter (he was at Tours, not in Rome; in 799 he wrote to Charlemagne about the three highest powers in the world, papal, imperial and royal, ranking the royal above the other two now that both had faltered).

### Alternate one: Aachen, among the columns

Charlemagne's church of St Mary at Aachen, begun in the 790s and traditionally consecrated in 805, was raised partly out of imported antique material. Einhard (ch. 26) says he had the columns and marbles brought from Rome and Ravenna because he could find none suitable elsewhere; a letter of Pope Hadrian I in the Codex Carolinus, dated 787, grants him mosaics and marbles from Ravenna. Brigitte Buettner's 2024 study confirms the material fact from the far end: when French troops stripped roughly forty columns out of the chapel in 1794, thirty-two of them, the ones in the eight gallery arches, were already Carolingian spolia from Rome and Ravenna. Ten are still in the Louvre.

Ledger: the reuse, the 1794 removal, and the failed restitution are documented; no eighth-century mason is named, so any workman is a composite and storyContext must say so. Do not assert that every column is spolia; some elements were newly carved. Strength: the perfect material image, marble quarried under one empire and re-erected under a claim to another, then taken a third time by a French regime that also wanted to be Rome. Weakness: it needs an invented person, and Era IV already used a composite artisan watching a building go up (byzantium-endures).

### Alternate two: gold on purple, seventeen years early

The Godescalc Evangelistary (Paris, BnF, nouv. acq. lat. 1203) was commissioned by Charlemagne and Hildegard on 7 October 781 and finished on 30 April 783, written in gold and silver on purple-dyed parchment. Its dedication poem names the scribe Godescalc and the royal patrons and ties the book to the Italian journey, the meeting with Hadrian I, and a son's baptism. Ledger: patron, scribe, dates and materials come from the manuscript's own dedication poem, so a real named craftsman is available, which is rare; the workshop and any working day are reconstruction. Do not call this "the Aachen court school" in 781 to 783, when the court was still itinerant. Strength: purple and gold are Constantinople's imperial idiom, in Frankish hands seventeen years before any crown. Weakness: off-node by two decades, and it pulls the lesson toward books and away from power.

**Recommendation:** open at St Peter's. Let the Aachen columns be the governing image that returns in the significance and at the very end. Hold Godescalc in reserve if the coronation opening turns ceremonial rather than tense.

## Story movement

1. Open on 23 December, not 25 December: a pope swearing he is not what he has been accused of being, in front of a Frankish king who has no jurisdiction over him and is nonetheless the reason the oath will be believed.
2. Two days. Then the crown, the acclamation, the papal obeisance. Report what each source says and let the disagreement stand inside the scene rather than resolving it.
3. Turn to Einhard's famous sentence and hold it at arm's length: written a generation later by a courtier trained on Suetonius, at a court with its own reasons to resent a papal claim on the making of emperors. Do not call it a lie; show what kind of statement it is.
4. Widen. What did the new emperor actually govern? An itinerant court, counts with local land and local kin, missi dominici riding in pairs to audit them, capitularies read aloud, oaths of fidelity from free men, monasteries as archive and school system, bishops as administrators. No census, no salaried civil service, no gold coinage, no Roman land tax.
5. Cut to the material: the reused columns, the letter of 787, the silver denier reformed in 793 to 794 into a heavier pure-silver coin struck at a reduced number of supervised mints. The reboot is literally assembled from older parts plus new controls.
6. End at the pivot: the title exists again, the machine does not, and everything after depends on what can be improvised in the gap.

Motif: reuse. Columns, script, law, liturgy, a title. Let it return with changed meaning at the hinge and at the close.

Two cautions on movement. First, the scene has two protagonists and the lesson is better if neither is the hero: the pope needs the king's protection, the king needs the pope's hands, and the sentence that describes the moment should hold both needs at once. Second, resist the drumbeat. This is a node where the temptation to announce that everything changed on Christmas morning is nearly irresistible and completely wrong, since the western imperial title lapsed again within a century and had to be restarted in 962.

## Significance spine

1. **Evidence.** Four narratives of one morning, none neutral: the Liber Pontificalis (papal, and it makes Leo the agent), the Royal Frankish Annals (court, and it dates the event to 801 because the Frankish year began at Christmas), the Annales Laureshamenses (which supplies the legal argument), and Einhard a generation later (which supplies the reluctance). Teach the reader to notice that each version serves its author's institution.

2. **The vacancy argument, and what it hides.** Irene ruled at Constantinople as sole emperor from 797 to 802, having deposed and blinded her own son. The Lorsch annalist argues that the imperial name had therefore ceased among the Greeks. That is an argument, not a fact: Constantinople had an emperor, and its law, tax rolls, armies and courts were running. The West asserted a right to fill a seat it had declared empty. Charlemagne's own chancery then hedged: from 801 his style is emperor "governing the Roman empire," not emperor "of the Romans," a formula that steps around the Byzantine claim. When Michael I's envoys acclaimed him at Aachen in 812, in exchange for Frankish concessions over Venice and Dalmatia, they called him basileus and withheld "of the Romans."

3. **Mechanism: rule without an apparatus.** Set what Rome ran on beside what the Franks had. Missi dominici, sworn fidelity, capitularies, counts, bishops, and above all monasteries doing the copying, schooling and record keeping. Land and office, not salary and census. This is not a degraded Rome; it is a different machine built from Roman parts, which explains both its reach and its fragility.

   Useful concrete anchors for this beat, all safe to state: the missi dominici were regularized in the capitulary programme of 802, riding in pairs, one lay and one cleric, so that neither could be captured by the local network he was auditing. The general oath of fidelity was extended in the same years to all free men, which is a state asking its subjects to bind themselves because it cannot afford to watch them. And royal estates, not a treasury fed by land tax, supplied the court, which is why the court moved: an itinerant king eats his revenue where it grows.

4. **Named debate (use this one, and only this one).** Henri Pirenne argued in Mahomet et Charlemagne (1937; English 1939) that the Germanic kingdoms preserved the Roman Mediterranean and that the Arab conquests broke it, forcing western Europe inward and northward and thereby producing Charlemagne. Archaeology and a rereading of the documents have gone hard against the sealed-sea version: Richard Hodges and David Whitehouse (Mohammed, Charlemagne and the Origins of Europe, 1983), then Michael McCormick (Origins of the European Economy, 2001), show contacts, shipping and communications persisting and reviving, with McCormick inverting Pirenne's slogan into a claim about stimulus rather than isolation. Chris Wickham's Framing the Early Middle Ages (2005) reframes the period as regionally fragmented but functioning economies rather than collapse. Present it as a live argument about mechanism, not about whether the Middle Ages were dark. Handle the northern emporia carefully: Dorestad boomed and peaked around 820, but Simon Coupland's 2002 reassessment argues Quentovic was economically minor in exactly those decades. Do not present the two ports as twins.

5. **Consequence: the books.** About 500 manuscripts survive copied in Merovingian Gaul before about 750; about 7,000 survive copied in the Carolingian empire between about 750 and 900 (David Ganz's figures). For most classical Latin authors the oldest surviving manuscript is Carolingian. The correction was policy: the Admonitio generalis of 789, Alcuin at court from 782 and at Tours from 796, and a script, Caroline minuscule, legible to a stranger. State the ceiling honestly: this was correctio for clergy and administration, not mass literacy.

6. **Inheritance, told without teleology.** Louis the Pious tried to legislate unity in the Ordinatio imperii of 817; his sons fought anyway, and after Fontenoy in 841 the treaty of Verdun on 10 August 843 split the realm three ways. What follows is jurisdictional plurality, not liberty; the node's own edge to charter-and-parliament says as much, and the lesson must resist the story in which division automatically breeds freedom. Close on reuse: Caroline minuscule was revived around 1400 by Italian humanists who believed they were copying ancient Rome, which is why the roman type on this page descends from a Frankish reform mistaken for antiquity. Later people kept mining Charlemagne's empire for parts, exactly as he mined Ravenna.

## Factual traps

1. **Trap:** Charlemagne founded the Holy Roman Empire in 800. **Truth:** the phrase does not exist yet. Sacrum imperium first appears in March 1157 in Frederick Barbarossa's chancery, sacrum Romanum imperium from 1184, and the term becomes standard chancery usage only in 1254; the continuous line of western emperors begins with Otto I's coronation in 962, after a gap of decades. **Source:** Sulovsky, History Compass 17.8 (2019).

2. **Trap:** he was crowned "Emperor of the Romans." **Truth:** the Liber Pontificalis acclamation is "Karolo piissimo Augusto a Deo coronato magno et pacifico imperatori." From 801 his own diplomas style him "Karolus serenissimus Augustus a Deo coronatus magnus pacificus imperator Romanum gubernans imperium," emperor governing the Roman empire, pointedly not imperator Romanorum, the title Constantinople reserved to itself. In 812 Michael I's envoys acclaimed him basileus and withheld "of the Romans." **Source:** the chancery formula is standard in the literature (diploma of 29 May 801); the 812 settlement follows the Pax Nicephori.

3. **Trap:** Einhard's line proves Charlemagne was ambushed by the crown. **Truth:** Einhard says Charlemagne declared he would not have entered the church that day, feast or no feast, had he known the pope's plan. The Vita Karoli was written long after the event (proposals run from about 817 to about 833, with many favouring the later 820s), by a courtier imitating Suetonius, under Louis the Pious, when the papacy's role in making emperors had become a live grievance. Against literal surprise: Charlemagne had been in Rome a month, had presided over the December synod, and his son was anointed in the same ceremony. Most historians read the sentence as objection to the manner and the claim, or as later apologetics, not as evidence of ignorance. **Source:** Einhard ch. 28 (Turner translation, Fordham), read against Nelson and Collins on the divergent accounts.

4. **Trap:** Charlemagne could not read. **Truth:** Einhard ch. 25 says he kept tablets and notebooks under his pillows to practise forming letters and made little progress because he had begun too late; that is about handwriting. The same chapter has him learning Latin well and understanding Greek, and ch. 24 has him delighting in Augustine, especially the City of God, read aloud at table. Einhard is also building a portrait, so do not swing to the opposite overclaim. **Source:** Einhard chs. 24 and 25, Turner translation.

5. **Trap:** Alcuin invented Caroline minuscule at Aachen. **Truth:** proto-Caroline forms appear at Corbie before Charlemagne's reign, and the script emerged across several centres rather than by one man's design. Alcuin's Tours promoted and standardized it; he did not invent it. **Source:** Ganz, New Cambridge Medieval History II (1995), 786 to 808.

6. **Trap:** Renaissance humanists revived ancient Roman handwriting. **Truth:** they revived Caroline minuscule, largely through eleventh and twelfth century copies, in the belief that they were looking at books from the shops of ancient Rome. Poggio Bracciolini's littera antiqua dates from the first years of the fifteenth century; by 1418 nearly half the Medici library was catalogued as lettera antica, and modern roman type descends from that misidentification. **Nuance to keep:** some scholars stress conscious stylistic preference over pure error, so write "believed" rather than "were fooled."

7. **Trap:** Islam closed the Mediterranean and forced Europe inward. **Truth:** the strong Pirenne version does not survive the archaeology; contacts, shipping and coin flows persisted and revived, and the northern emporia grew alongside southern contact rather than instead of it. **Trap inside the trap:** the textbook pairing "Dorestad and Quentovic" flattens the evidence. **Source:** Coupland, Early Medieval Europe 11.3 (2002), 209 to 232; McCormick, Origins of the European Economy (2001).

8. **Trap:** Charlemagne proposed marriage to Irene and nearly reunited the empires. **Truth:** the story appears only in Theophanes the Confessor, an iconophile chronicler writing shortly afterward, and is uncorroborated; report it as a report if at all. Better attested: Nikephoros deposed Irene on 31 October 802 while Frankish and papal envoys were in the city.

9. **Trap:** the marble seat at Aachen is Charlemagne's throne, and Odo of Metz designed the chapel. **Truth:** the cathedral's own custodians say the attribution of the throne to Charlemagne himself cannot be unequivocally verified; the chair is securely the coronation throne of the German kings from 936 to 1531. Odo of Metz is named as builder in an inscription round the dome known in a tenth-century form, not in a contemporary building record. **Source:** Domkapitel Aachen.

10. **Trap:** the Aachen chapel was consecrated by Leo III in 805, full stop.
    **Truth:** 805 is the standard date and is repeated everywhere, but it rests on later tradition rather than a contemporary dedication record, and the start date is usually given as a range in the 790s (commonly about 792 to 796). Write the construction span as a range and attribute the consecration date rather than asserting who performed it.

11. **Trap:** Charlemagne, the Father of Europe, united the continent.
    **Truth:** the epithet pater Europae comes from a court poem of his own circle, not from posterity's neutral judgement, and the realm he assembled excluded Britain, Ireland, Scandinavia, most of Iberia, all of the Slavic east, and the Roman empire still running from Constantinople. Modern uses of Charlemagne as a founder of European unity are nineteenth and twentieth century political constructions and should be named as such if the lesson touches them at all.

12. **Trap:** a gentle emperor of schools and books. **Truth:** the Royal Frankish Annals record 4,500 Saxons beheaded at Verden in 782 on Charlemagne's order; the old attempt to rescue him by positing a scribal confusion with a word for deportation is generally rejected, though some scholars think the number inflated. The Saxon wars ran roughly 772 to 804 and included forced baptism and deportations. One clause is enough, but the lesson must not omit it.

## Callback design

Callbacks may reach only written lessons. Ranked, with angles.

1. **byzantium-endures** (strongest; ship it). In 800 two courts claim one Roman name, and the Latin argument for the western claim is that Irene's throne does not count. The earlier lesson already taught that "Roman" was a membership manufactured and maintained by institutions, citizenship, law, church, taxation, a name kept in business, rather than by blood or language. Ask the reader to weigh what each side actually held: Constantinople had law schools, tax rolls, courts and a Greek-speaking population that called itself Romaioi; Aachen had a coronation, a pope, and a church built out of Ravenna's columns. Someone who has read both can say why the eastern claim was the stronger one and why the western claim nonetheless proved durable.

2. **iron-and-alphabet** (ship it, unless the draft goes heavily governmental). That lesson's hard-won point was that a smaller sign inventory lowered one wall and left teachers, materials, institutions and purposes standing behind it. Caroline minuscule is the same argument eighteen centuries later: a clearer, teachable, stranger-legible hand hugely raised the survival odds of Latin texts without producing a literate population. Ask what a script reform actually buys and what it does not. This also fences the lesson off from claiming a Carolingian literacy revolution.

3. **pax-romana-machine** (substitute for 2 if the lesson foregrounds governance). Both are thin centres, and the comparison is about what they rest on. Pliny's empire was thin at the top and thick with self-governing cities, census, tax rolls and correspondence, and it refused a fire brigade because it feared organization beneath it. Charlemagne's was thin at the top and rested on estates, monasteries, bishops and counts whose power was land, and it needed exactly the local organization Rome distrusted. Ask what an emperor in 800 could not order that Trajan could, and why "empire" names two different machines.

4. **jesus-to-constantine** (reserve). Patronage binds the patron. Constantine's favour gave the church reach and taught it imperial habits; in 800 the institution he favoured places the crown. Ask which direction the leverage runs, and what changed between an emperor convening bishops at Nicaea and a bishop of Rome crowning an emperor at St Peter's. Keep it short so it does not trespass on road-to-canossa.

5. **steppe-hammer**: use as a thread rather than a callback, since the graph already wires it forward into this node. If it is nonetheless wanted as a callback, the angle is that the kingdoms which formed inside the western provinces after 376 kept Roman law, Roman taxes and Roman bishops while the Roman name went elsewhere, so 800 is that process reaching for the name it had done without for three centuries.

6. **invention-of-writing** (weak; listed only to be dismissed). The script comparison is better served by iron-and-alphabet, which already carries the do-not-overclaim-literacy argument this lesson needs.

Shipping recommendation: 1 and 2, in that order, with 3 held ready.

Phrasing rule for both shipped callbacks: the prompt must be answerable only by someone who read both lessons, so it should name the specific thing the earlier lesson established (the Romaioi and their tax rolls; the twenty-two signs that still needed teachers) rather than asking the reader to recall a title. The answers should teach in two to four sentences and should not flatter the West. On callback 1 in particular, the honest answer is that Constantinople had the better claim and Aachen had the better afterlife, and the reason is not merit.
