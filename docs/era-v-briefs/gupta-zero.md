Central claim to build on: the achievement of 628 is not the invention of zero and not the invention of a placeholder. It is the promotion of zero from a
mark that guards an empty column into a quantity that obeys laws, inside a single closed arithmetic shared with negative numbers. Brahmagupta wrote down
what happens when you add to nothing, subtract from nothing, multiply by nothing, and try to divide by nothing. Three of the four went well.

## Preferred scene

**The verses on cipher, Bhillamala, 628 CE.** Brahmagupta, thirty years old, composing the eighteenth chapter of the Brahmasphutasiddhanta in Sanskrit
verse. The dramatic object is the rule itself: a compressed, memorizable line that has to be exactly right, because it will be recited by people who cannot
check it against the man who made it. The pressure is that his rules run cleanly through addition, subtraction and multiplication, then arrive at division,
where the sentence he is composing has no ending. He finishes it anyway: a positive or a negative divided by cipher is a fraction with cipher for its
denominator. He has given the object a name instead of a value. And in the same meter he writes that cipher divided by cipher is cipher, which is false,
and which nobody will be able to prove false for a very long time.

Documentation ledger:

- **Attested.** Brahmagupta's dates (born 598, died c. 668 to 670). That he states in the text that he wrote the Brahmasphutasiddhanta at Bhillamala,
  modern Bhinmal in Rajasthan, in 628. That Bhillamala was then a capital under a ruler named Vyaghramukha. That the work runs to twenty-five chapters,
  that chapter 18 is the algebra and holds the rules for cipher and for negative and positive quantities, and that the rules are in verse. That he wrote a
  second work, the Khandakhadyaka, in 665 at the age of sixty-seven.
- **Attested and quotable.** The rules themselves, in translation. Use Colebrooke's 1817 English if quoting, and check the wording on the page image rather
  than from any paraphrase, this brief's included.
- **Reconstruction, and must be labelled as such in `storyContext`.** Any room, light, weather, dust board, palm leaf or birch bark, pupil, patron,
  gesture, mood, or order of composition. There is no eyewitness account of Brahmagupta working and no seventh-century manuscript of the text; what we read
  descends through much later copies.
- **Forbidden.** A moment of discovery. The verses record no hesitation and the prose must not read his mind. The available drama is the drama of the
  sentence: a rule-form that has always closed cleanly and now will not.

**Alternate 1: Bakhshali, May 1881.** A labourer digging in a field or a ruin near Bakhshali, eighty kilometres north east of Peshawar, turns up seventy
badly damaged folios of birch bark covered in Sharada script and in dots. The find goes to the Lieutenant Governor of the Panjab, then to A. F. R. Hoernle,
then in 1902 to the Bodleian, where it is MS. Sansk. d. 14. All documented in the Oxford radiocarbon report. The pressure is evidentiary: the one object
that could date the Indian zero refuses for a hundred and forty years to say when it was made. Strong if Fable wants the named debate as the opening image.
Weak in that it opens twelve hundred years off the node's date, with an object rather than a person at the centre.

**Alternate 2: Stele K-127, Sambor on the Mekong, 683 CE.** A donation record cut in Old Khmer listing slaves, five pairs of oxen and white rice, dated 605
in the Saka era, which is 683 CE, with a small circle in the tens place of that 605. Found 1891, read by George Coedes in 1930, lost during the Khmer Rouge
years, found again by Amir Aczel in an Angkor storehouse, later brought to Phnom Penh. The stone, date, contents and recovery history are documented; the
carver is entirely reconstruction. Use only with the correction attached: these are Indian-derived numerals used in Cambodia, so the stone attests how far
and how fast the system travelled, not a Cambodian invention. Some accounts read the date as 604 Saka, 682 CE.

Recommendation: open with the verses, and let the Bakhshali evidence-fight arrive as the honest complication in significance, where it belongs.

## Story movement

**First movement, the system working.** Give the reader the debt and fortune vocabulary before the abstraction, because it is Brahmagupta's own and it is
what makes negative quantities citizens rather than nonsense. A debt minus nothing is a debt. A fortune minus nothing is a fortune. A debt subtracted from
nothing is a fortune. The reader should feel a set of rules snapping shut, each obvious once said and impossible to say before someone decided nothing was
a thing you could operate on.

**Second movement, the system reaching.** Zero added, subtracted, multiplied: clean. Then division. The verse gives a positive or negative divided by
cipher a name rather than an answer, and gives cipher divided by cipher the answer zero, which is wrong. Do not editorialise. Let the reader watch a very
good mind write one true sentence and one false one in the same meter, and feel why the false one was so hard to see.

**Pivot to end on.** The rules are now portable. They are in verse because verse survives copying and travel. Whatever else is uncertain about the next
four centuries, these sentences will outlive the man, be recited in places he never heard of, be argued with in Arabic, and reach a Latin counting house
with his name stripped off. End where the reader wants to ask how a rule travels.

Do not stage a scene of the numeral being drawn. No seventh-century manuscript of this text survives, and the earliest firmly dated zero glyphs in the
record are later than 628 and not in India.

Governing image, used lightly: the unfinished sentence. It returns when al-Khwarizmi's Arabic book survives only in someone else's Latin, and again at the
end, where a modern machine still answers division by zero with a word instead of a number.

## Significance spine

1. **Evidence: what survives, and what came before him.** The Brahmasphutasiddhanta of 628 reaches us through later copies, and reached European readers
   through H. T. Colebrooke's 1817 translation of its algebra chapter. Brahmagupta did not start from nothing. Aryabhata, in 499, had already stated the
   positional principle flatly, that from place to place each is ten times the preceding, and had named the decimal places; but the numerals Aryabhata
   actually writes in the Aryabhatiya are an alphabetic notation built to fit large numbers into verse, not decimal digits with a zero glyph. Sanskrit had
   long used words such as shunya and kha for the empty position inside word-numerals. What 628 adds is not a symbol. It is a law.

2. **Mechanism: the difference between a placeholder and a number.** A placeholder answers "is this column empty?" A number answers "what happens if I
   operate on it?" Babylonian scribes had a medial sign for an empty place by the later first millennium BCE and never used it at the end of a numeral.
   Greek astronomers, Ptolemy among them, used a small circle in sexagesimal fractions and treated it as punctuation, not quantity. Maya scribes had a zero
   inside a base-twenty system, independently, with no contact and no influence on anyone in this lesson. All of those are marks. Brahmagupta's chapter
   puts zero and the negatives into one arithmetic where each can be added to, subtracted from and multiplied by anything else. That is the hinge, and it
   is a hinge of craft as much as of ideas: a rule terse enough to memorise is a technology.

3. **Honest limits: not first about negatives, wrong about division.** Negative quantities were already in use in China. The Nine Chapters on the
   Mathematical Art, in the form received from the Han period, works with negatives as red and black counting rods and states the positive and negative
   rules inside its method for systems of linear equations. Brahmagupta's contribution to negatives is a compact sign-rule system inside a text that
   travelled west, not their first use on earth. As for division, his positive-or-negative-divided-by-cipher is a formal object with no value assigned, and
   his cipher divided by cipher is cipher is simply false. Bhaskara II, around 1150, pushed the quotient toward the infinite and still did not arrive
   anywhere a modern mathematician would sign. Division by zero remains undefined, and undefined is the correct, hard-won answer rather than a failure.

4. **Named debate: how do you date the Bakhshali manuscript, and who announces it?** In 2017 the Bodleian announced radiocarbon dates putting folios of the
   Bakhshali manuscript as early as 224 to 383 CE, and the story went round the world as the world's oldest zero. Five specialists (Kim Plofker, Agathe
   Keller, Takao Hayashi, Clemency Montelle, Dominik Wujastyk) published a response in History of Science in South Asia that year, arguing that the
   earliest dated folio of a composite manuscript is not the date of the manuscript, that the latest datable folio better indicates when the scribal work
   was done, that the manuscript's zeros work as arithmetical operators rather than mere placeholders, and that the library had gone to press release and
   video without peer review. In October 2024 the Oxford Radiocarbon Accelerator Unit and the Bodleian published the full report: the original
   determination on folio 16 was inaccurate, folio 17's measured age is an outlier and has been excluded, and the five reported folios calibrate at 95.4
   per cent confidence to 773 to 986, 901 to 1032, 890 to 1014, 885 to 995 and 774 to 991 CE. Their phase model puts the start of production at 799 to 892
   and the end at 900 to 1102. The report is careful that a bark date is a terminus post quem for the writing, not the writing's date. Leave the reader
   with the mechanism, not only the verdict: bark is older than the ink on it, a manuscript of many leaves has no single date, and a headline is not a
   finding.

   Optional second complication, one sentence at most: David Pingree's claim that the Yavanajataka of 269 or 270 CE held the earliest reference to decimal
   place value with a symbol for zero rested on his own emendation of a defective manuscript, and Bill Mak's work on newly discovered manuscript material
   has removed both the reading and Pingree's dating.

5. **Consequence: how the rules moved, and how much they were rebuilt on the way.** Later Arabic accounts describe an Indian expert arriving at the court
   of al-Mansur in Baghdad in 771 or 773 with a siddhanta, translated with the help of al-Fazari and Ya'qub ibn Tariq into what became the Zij al-Sindhind
   tradition. Handle with care: the story comes from later sources, and the translated text is best described as a Brahmapaksa siddhanta closely related to
   Brahmagupta's rather than as his book itself. Then al-Khwarizmi wrote a book on calculating with Indian numerals whose Arabic original is lost and which
   survives in Latin adaptations known from their opening words, Dixit Algorizmi, which is where the word algorithm comes from. Then Latin Europe:
   Fibonacci's Liber Abaci in 1202. The word itself carries the relay in its shape, from shunya to the Arabic sifr to the Latin zephirum and cifra to zero
   and cipher. Nobody handed anybody a finished thing; each stage tested it, renamed it, and partly rebuilt it.

6. **Inheritance.** Positional notation with a zero digit is the substrate under every calculating machine, every column of accounts, every address in
   memory. The other inheritance is the unfinished sentence. Ask a modern computer for a number divided by zero and it will not give you a number: it gives
   a word, undefined, or an error, or a special value invented so the machine can keep going. That is Brahmagupta's verse, still open, and the honest
   ending is that the hard part of inventing nothing was never the notation. It was agreeing what nothing is allowed to do.

Threads to name in words, per the spec: **craft** (a rule short enough to memorise, a notation compact enough to compute with, a verse form built to
survive copying) and **ideas** (an abstraction given laws). **Wealth** is available free through debt and fortune and is worth one clause: this is a
mathematics whose vocabulary for the negative comes from owing money.

## Factual traps

1. **Trap:** calling this Gupta-era mathematics because the node id says so. **Truth:** Gupta imperial power was gone long before 628. Brahmagupta wrote at
   Bhillamala under a local ruler named Vyaghramukha, and sources differ on whether to label that dynasty Chapa, Chavda or Gurjara. Say Bhillamala, modern
   Bhinmal in Rajasthan, name Vyaghramukha, do not say Gupta, and do not resolve a dynasty label the sources leave open. **Source:** MacTutor, Brahmagupta
   biography, University of St Andrews.

2. **Trap:** Brahmagupta invented zero, or India invented zero alone. **Truth:** placeholder zeros existed before and elsewhere: a Babylonian medial sign,
   a small circle in Greek sexagesimal astronomy including Ptolemy's tables, and an independent Maya zero inside a base-twenty system. Brahmagupta's
   novelty is arithmetic rules for zero as a number. **Source:** MacTutor, A history of Zero, University of St Andrews.

3. **Trap:** the Bakhshali manuscript proves an Indian zero in the third or fourth century CE. **Truth:** superseded. The 2024 Oxford report withdraws the
   folio 16 determination as inaccurate, excludes folio 17 as an outlier, and calibrates the five reported folios between 773 and 1032 CE, with a modelled
   production phase starting 799 to 892 and ending 900 to 1102. Bark dates are termini post quem. **Source:** Chivall, Llado-Buisan, Howell and Evison,
   Radiocarbon dating of the Bakhshali manuscript, University of Oxford, 14 October 2024.

4. **Trap:** Brahmagupta said a number divided by zero is infinity, or that he solved division by zero. **Truth:** he said a positive or negative divided
   by cipher is a fraction with cipher for denominator, which names the object without valuing it, and he said cipher divided by cipher is cipher, which is
   wrong. The move toward the infinite belongs to Bhaskara II around 1150, and is still not the modern answer. **Source:** MacTutor, Brahmagupta biography
   and A history of Zero.

5. **Trap:** negative numbers were an Indian invention. **Truth:** the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art, in its received Han-period form, already
   computes with negatives, distinguishing them by red and black counting rods, and states the positive and negative rules within its method for
   rectangular arrays of linear equations. Brahmagupta systematised signed arithmetic in a text that travelled; he did not first think of it. **Source:**
   Mathematical Association of America, A Classic from China: the Nine Chapters; Britannica, East Asian mathematics.

6. **Trap:** the oldest zero is the one carved at Gwalior in 876 CE. **Truth:** that is the oldest in an Indian inscription. Stele K-127 from Sambor on the
   Mekong is dated 605 in the Saka era, 683 CE, with a circle in the tens place, and the Old Malay Kedukan Bukit inscription from Sumatra carries the same
   Saka year. Both are roughly two centuries older than Gwalior, and both use Indian-derived numerals, so they attest the export of the system rather than
   a separate invention. Some accounts read K-127 as 604 Saka, 682 CE; if the number matters to a sentence, hedge it or avoid the last digit. **Source:**
   Amir Aczel, The Origin of the Number Zero, Smithsonian Magazine, December 2014.

7. **Trap:** the earliest reference to decimal place value with a zero symbol is in the Yavanajataka of 269 or 270 CE. **Truth:** that reading was David
   Pingree's own emendation of a defective manuscript, and Bill Mak's reconsideration in the light of manuscript material found in 2011 and 2012 challenges
   both the reading and Pingree's date and authorship framework for the text. **Source:** Bill M. Mak, History of Science in South Asia 1 (2013), 1 to 20,
   DOI 10.18732/H2RP4T.

8. **Trap:** Brahmagupta's Brahmasphutasiddhanta was carried to Baghdad in 773 and translated as the Sindhind at the House of Wisdom. **Truth:** three
   over-claims at once. The embassy story comes from later Arabic accounts, not a contemporary record. The Sanskrit text behind the Sindhind is best
   described as a Brahmapaksa siddhanta closely related to Brahmagupta's work rather than that work itself. And the 770s translations under al-Mansur
   precede by about half a century the Baghdad translation culture that the graph's `house-of-wisdom` node dates to c.
   820. Write transmission as a relay across generations, not a delivery.

9. **Trap:** Aryabhata used a zero symbol in 499. **Truth:** he states the positional principle and names the decimal places, but the numerals he writes in
   the Aryabhatiya are a syllabic notation built to compress large numbers into verse, not decimal digits with a zero glyph. Place value in words is not
   the same object as a digit on a surface; keep notation, arithmetical use, and formal rules distinct throughout the lesson. **Source:** Aryabhatiya,
   Ganitapada, on the decimal places; the alphabetic notation appears in the Dasagitika.

## Callback design

Only lessons on the eligible written list may be `callbackTo` targets. `counting-in-sixties` is unwritten and cannot carry a callback question even though
the graph draws its edge into this node. Take two of the following; 1 and 2 are the strongest pairing, and 3 is the best option if the era's callback
budget needs spread away from Eras II and III.

1. **invention-of-writing** (rank 1). The earlier lesson does the setup for you: it insists that archaic scribes used at least five metrological systems,
   so a numerical mark did not denote one context-free number, and that this was a step toward abstraction rather than the moment number peeled away from
   the world. Angle: ask what the Uruk tablet still tied a number to that positional notation cuts loose, and what must exist before ten signs can write
   any number at all. The answer teaches that Uruk separated count from counted thing on the surface but kept the counting system welded to the commodity,
   while place value makes a sign's meaning depend on nothing but its position, which is exactly why the empty position must itself become a sign.

2. **invention-of-money** (rank 2). That lesson's mechanism is verification compression: a claim prepared elsewhere arrives partly checked. Brahmagupta
   does something adjacent and different, making owing and having the same kind of quantity, so a debt can be computed with rather than merely recorded.
   Angle: which abstraction had to overcome the stronger intuition, that a stranger's stamped metal is worth something, or that there is a number below
   nothing? The answer should refuse a winner and name the shared move: both take a social relation and give it a form that can be operated on by someone
   who was not present when it was made.

3. **silk-road-pulse** (rank 3, best for era spread). Its core sentence is that the network was connected the way a chain is, link by link, no link seeing
   the whole, and that a bolt of silk arrived at the western end with its origins worn off. Brahmagupta's rules travelled that way, and the proof is that
   al-Khwarizmi's Arabic book on Indian reckoning is lost and survives only in Latin adaptations. Angle: what can a mathematical rule do on that relay that
   a bolt of silk cannot? It can be tested and corrected at every handoff, so it arrives improved rather than merely worn.

4. **code-of-hammurabi** (rank 4, alternate, most surprising). Both nodes are lists of terse rules in fixed form, and both raise the question of whether a
   list of cases is a system. The Hammurabi lesson already carries the argument that the stele may be classified scholarship rather than binding law, since
   no surviving judgment cites it. Angle: what enforces a rule in each case? Nobody punished a Babylonian judge for ignoring the stone; arithmetic punishes
   a wrong rule for zero the first time anyone computes with it. Use only if Fable wants the comparison to be about what makes a rule binding.

## Node-data flags

- **The node id is a lie and the lesson must not repeat it.** `gupta-zero` is permanent and cannot change, but nothing in the prose, title, hook or summary
  may call Brahmagupta a Gupta mathematician or place him in a Gupta court. The relocation of this node to Era V at 628 CE was made for exactly this reason
  and is recorded in docs/fable-era-iv-handoff.md.
- **Summary clause to soften: "marks for empty positions before Brahmagupta."** Defensible for the concept, since Sanskrit word-numerals used shunya and
  kha for the empty position and Aryabhata states the positional principle in 499. Not well supported for a dated Indian *mark* before 628: the earliest
  firmly dated zero glyphs are 683 CE in Southeast Asia and 876 CE at Gwalior, and the Bakhshali manuscript now sits in the ninth to eleventh centuries. If
  Fable proposes replacement summary copy, this is the clause to fix, ideally by saying words for the empty position rather than marks.
- **Summary clause "division by zero defeated him" is close but blunt.** He did not fail to write anything. He wrote two things, one empty and one false,
  and that distinction is the best moment in the lesson.
- **Edge why to `house-of-wisdom` is accurate but chronologically loose.** "Sanskrit astronomical and mathematical works entered Abbasid translation
  networks, where scholars including al-Khwarizmi adapted Indian numerals and methods" is true, but the Sindhind translations belong to the 770s under
  al-Mansur while the `house-of-wisdom` node is dated c.
  820. The lesson must not compress those into one event.
- **Incoming edge why from `counting-in-sixties` slightly overstates the Babylonian gap.** "Babylonian place value left empty slots ambiguous for
  centuries" is right for the early centuries, but Babylonian scribes did adopt a medial placeholder sign in the later first millennium BCE, never used at
  the end of a numeral. The lesson should get this right even though the edge label is not the lesson's text. No file edit proposed.
- **Date, sort, region, x and threads all check out.** 628 CE, sasia, craft and ideas need no change.
- **No callback may point at `counting-in-sixties`, `panini-grammar`, `euclid-elements` or `sanskrit-cosmopolis`.** They are unwritten. They remain fine as
