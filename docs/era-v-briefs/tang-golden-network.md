# Lesson brief: tang-golden-network

Era V, The Believing World. Research and architecture scaffolding for Fable, not draft prose. Where this brief and a source disagree, follow the source and say so in the completion report.

## Graph target

- Node id: `tang-golden-network`. Title: Tang: The Open Empire. Date field: c. 700 CE (sort 700).
- Region easia, x 88. Threads: power, wealth (ideas and craft must be made explicit in significance).
- Outgoing edges: `house-of-wisdom` (collides), `song-economic-miracle` (transforms), `heian-and-genji` (enables).
- Incoming edges: `han-mandate`, `silk-road-pulse`, `dharma-in-chinese` (Kumarajiva's translations),
  `grand-canal-spine`, `sogdian-web`.

The central claim to build toward: Tang openness was an administered condition, not a temperament. A licensed,
walled, drum-timed capital let foreign faiths, merchants, and goods in through named offices and revocable
permissions, and the same administrative capacity that granted them could withdraw them. The lesson must not
present the Tang as a proto-multicultural utopia, and it must not present the examination system as having
arrived in its mature form. Both are the standard textbook errors this node is positioned to correct.

## Preferred scene

**The Xi'an stele, Chang'an, 4 February 781 CE.** A Church of the East monk named Jingjing, who signs himself
Adam, has written a nine-foot limestone account of his religion's 150 years in China: Chinese prose, Syriac
glosses, Chinese cosmological vocabulary, an imperial idiom. The calligraphy is cut by Lü Xiuyan. The money
comes from Yisi (Yazdbozid), a Christian officer from Balkh who served under the general Guo Ziyi in the
campaigns against An Lushan and was rewarded with Tang rank. The inscription tells how Alopen reached Chang'an
in 635 with books and images, how Taizong received him, and how an edict of 638 licensed the religion.

Why this scene wins: it is anchored to a surviving object with three named real people, it dramatizes the exact
mechanism (a foreign faith earning its place by writing itself in the state's own language), and it carries the
node's hinge inside it, because the man who paid for the stone made his career in the war that broke the open
empire. It also gives the lesson an ending: the stele was buried around 845, when Wuzong's edicts swept up
Christians, Manichaeans, and Zoroastrians along with the Buddhists, and stayed buried until the 1620s.

Documentation ledger:

- Attested: the stele exists (Beilin Museum, Xi'an); its date, 4 February 781; its title, Stele to the
  Propagation in China of the Luminous Religion of Daqin; Jingjing as author, Lü Xiuyan as calligrapher, Yisi as
  patron and as an officer under Guo Ziyi; the Syriac clergy names; the 635 arrival and Taizong's reception;
  burial around 845; rediscovery near Chongren Temple between 1621 and 1625.
- Reconstruction: the carving day, weather, tools, dialogue, anyone's feelings, the ceremony. Also the stele's
  original standing place, which is not securely known (accounts differ between a Chang'an monastery and
  Zhouzhi). Do not assert where it stood.
- Do not assert: that Alopen's name is Chinese for Abraham (an old guess), or that the stele was a public
  monument the city saw. Say what it claims, not what it proves about numbers of Christians.
- One further detail if Fable can verify it independently: Jingjing is reported to have worked with the Buddhist
  monk Prajña on a translation around 786 to 787 that the court rejected as a confused mixture of religions. A
  perfect limit case for regulated pluralism if it holds up. Unverified in this pass.

Alternate 1: **The Western Market at the noon drum, c. 700.** Sits exactly on the node's date and hook.
Institutionally documented: two supervised markets, market offices setting and recording prices, rows of trades,
drums opening the market around midday and a gong closing it before dusk, foreign shops, Iranian wine sellers in
Tang verse, the excavated Western Market site. Anchors for real objects: Sogdian groom and camel-driver tomb
figurines, the sancai camel carrying musicians from the tomb of Xianyu Tinghui (died 723), and the Turfan market
price lists and contracts from the Astana tombs, which preserve actual prices and actual litigants. Any
individual walking this scene is a composite and must be labeled. The drum counts vary between popular
retellings (300, 400, 600 beats); use the mechanism, not a number, unless Fable can pin the count to scholarship.

Alternate 2: **He Zhizhang takes first place in the jinshi examination, 695.** A real named person (c. 659 to
744), later one of the Eight Immortals of the Wine Cup. The trap that makes this scene dangerous is what makes
it interesting: in 695 the court was at Luoyang, not Chang'an, because Wu Zetian had made Luoyang the Divine
Capital. Nothing about his examination day is documented. Use him inside significance as a real face on the exam
route, not as the cold open.

## Story movement

Open on the stone and the hand cutting it, not on the city. Let the reader discover Chang'an through what the
inscription has to do to be legible there: a Syriac faith arriving with a Persian-language liturgy and leaving
in Chinese, dated by an emperor's reign, framed by a cosmology the court could read. The first turn is backward,
to 635 and 638, to the arrival and the licence, and then outward into the city that licensed it: the grid, the
wards, the markets, the other faiths holding their own permissions.

The second turn is the money. Yisi paid for this, and Yisi got his rank in the war of 755 to 763. Let the war
enter through the patron's biography rather than a battle narrative, so the hinge arrives as a change in who
can afford a monument and why.

End the story where the reader can feel the question: an empire that could grant a religion a place could also
revoke it, and did, in 845, when the stone went into the ground. Do not narrate the burial as certainty about a
date or a hand; the stele's survival is the fact, and it was found again in the 1620s.

Governing image: permission. Everything here is licensed, timed, registered, and revocable, from a market's
opening drum to a religion's right to exist. Return to it at the hinge with its meaning changed.

## Significance spine

1. **Evidence.** Name what the city is known from, because this is a lesson about administration and the reader
   should feel the paper under it: the stele; the census registers (Jingzhao prefecture, 742: 362,921 households
   and 1,960,188 people, which is the metropolitan prefecture and not the walled city); the excavated grid; the
   Tang Code of 653; the Turfan and Dunhuang documents; tomb figurines; the poets. Handle the population claim
   here. Modern estimates for the population inside the walls run roughly 500,000 to 1,000,000, and "a city of a
   million" is honest shorthand for the metropolitan area, not a measured intramural count.
2. **Mechanism one, the grid.** Sui Daxing, laid out from 582, inherited by the Tang as Chang'an: roughly 84
   square kilometers, about 108 walled and gated wards, two supervised markets, drum signals opening the gates
   at dawn and closing them at night, curfew enforced inside the wards. This is openness inside a cage. The city
   admitted the world and then told it when to be indoors.
3. **Mechanism two, recruitment, and the correction.** The examinations begin under the Sui and run annually
   under the Tang. Wu Zetian, who ruled in her own name from 690 to 705, personally examined candidates at
   Luoyang in 690, the beginning of the palace examination. But the jinshi degree produced only a few dozen
   graduates a year against thousands of ranked posts, and most officials still entered by the yin privilege (a
   high official's sons), recommendation, clerical promotion, and imperial summons. The examinations made a
   shared literary culture and a prestige ladder; they did not make an open elite. That happens under the Song.
   Vedal on examination poetry is the way into what was actually tested: composition, on set topics, to strict form.
4. **Named debate (present this one honestly).** The Tang to Song transition. The old thesis, from Naito Konan
   and long standard, holds that an aristocracy of great clans was displaced by examination-recruited gentry.
   Nicolas Tackett, working from thousands of excavated tomb epitaphs with network and mapping methods, argues
   the great clans adapted to the seventh and eighth centuries far better than anyone thought, dominated the
   bureaucracy to the end, and vanished not through examination competition but through physical destruction in
   the collapse of the 880s. Give the reader the shape of the disagreement, not a verdict.
5. **Mechanism three, regulated pluralism.** Statute 48 of the Tang Code handled offences between foreigners of
   the same origin under their own law, and between foreigners of different origins under Tang law. Manichaeism
   arrived in 694 and in 732 was restricted by edict to foreigners, with Chinese subjects forbidden to convert.
   Zoroastrian and Church of the East communities held their permissions on similar terms. This is the argument
   the lesson exists to make: Tang openness was a legal category, administered, graded, and revocable, and it
   produced real room to live as well as real limits.
6. **Consequence.** The rebellion of 755 to 763 and its long tail. Registered population falls from about 52.9
   million in the mid 750s to about 16.9 million in 764, which is a collapse of registration and control as much
   as of lives. The equal-field system fails; the salt commission under Liu Yan grows salt revenue to more than
   half the state's cash income by around 780; Yang Yan's two-tax reform of 780 taxes land and households twice a
   year instead of trying to see every adult. The state stops counting people and starts taxing wealth. The
   economic center moves toward the Yangtze, and coastal trade at Guangzhou and Yangzhou grows even as the
   overland corridor contracts. "Turned inward" is too simple: say what turned, and where.
7. **Inheritance.** The grid was the era's most portable export: Heijo-kyo at Nara from 710, Heian-kyo from 794,
   and the Silla capital at Gyeongju all answer to it, and Japan's embassies ran until 838 before being formally
   abandoned in 894. The examination idea passed to the Song, which finished it. And the modern picture of a
   golden cosmopolitan Tang is itself an artifact: every age that wants to argue about openness reaches for this
   city. Close on the stone, on permission granted and permission withdrawn.

Thread work: power and wealth are the node's pigments, but say in words how craft (the grid, the stone, the
sancai kilns, papermaking) and ideas (Buddhism translated, the exam curriculum) rode the same permissions.

## Factual traps

1. **Trap:** The examination system matured under the Tang. **Truth:** The Tang ran the examinations annually
   and Wu Zetian added the palace examination in 690, but the jinshi produced only a few dozen graduates a year,
   the great clans supplied most officials to the end of the dynasty, and yin privilege, recommendation, and
   clerical promotion remained the main roads to office. Maturity is a Song achievement. **Source:** Tackett,
   The Destruction of the Medieval Chinese Aristocracy (Harvard University Asia Center, 2014), DOI
   10.1163/9781684170777.
2. **Trap:** An examination scene set in Chang'an under Empress Wu around 700. **Truth:** Wu Zetian made Luoyang
   the Divine Capital and ruled from there; the palace examination of 690 was held at Luoyang, and He Zhizhang's
   695 degree belongs to that court. The shuttling between capitals was partly a grain problem: feeding Chang'an
   past the Sanmen rapids was hard enough that emperors moved east to eat. **Source:** Chinese accounts of the
   690 Luocheng Hall examination and He Zhizhang's biography. Verify the 690 venue against a Western scholarly
   account before naming the hall in prose.
3. **Trap:** The An Lushan rebellion killed 36 million people, or "millions," as a body count read off the
   census. **Truth:** Registered population falls from about 52.9 million (mid 750s) to about 16.9 million (764),
   but that gap measures collapsed registration, flight, warlord autonomy, and lost territory as much as death.
   The war was catastrophic; the number is not a mortality figure. **Source:** the census figures as transmitted
   in the Tang histories, with the standard caution (C. P. Fitzgerald onward) that the post-rebellion registers
   cannot bear that weight.
4. **Trap:** Chinese papermakers captured at Talas in 751 gave the Islamic world paper. **Truth:** The claim
   comes from al-Tha'alibi in the eleventh century, three hundred years after the fact. Paper was already in use,
   and probably in production, in Samarkand and Sogdiana before 751; Sogdian, Arabic, and Chinese paper documents
   survive from the region from earlier, and early Islamic paper used rag fibers rather than the bast fibers of
   Chinese paper. **Source:** Jonathan M. Bloom, Paper Before Print (Yale University Press, 2001). This is also
   the node's own edge text; see node flags.
5. **Trap:** Tang China was uniquely tolerant, a medieval multiculturalism. **Truth:** Tolerance was a licensing
   regime with hard edges. Manichaeism was restricted to foreign practitioners by edict in 732; Statute 48 of the
   Tang Code sorted foreigners into legal categories; rebel forces massacred thousands of Arab and Persian
   merchants at Yangzhou in 760; and the Huichang edicts of 845 laicized on the order of 260,500 monks and nuns
   and closed about 4,600 monasteries while also suppressing Zoroastrians, Manichaeans, and the Church of the
   East. **Source:** Wallace Johnson's translation of the Tang Code; the 732 edict in the Manichaean
   scholarship; the Tang histories on 760 and on the Huichang suppression.
6. **Trap:** A million people lived inside Chang'an's walls. **Truth:** The 742 census of Jingzhao prefecture
   gives 1,960,188 people across the whole metropolitan prefecture, not the city; estimates for the population
   inside the walls run commonly from about 500,000 to 1,000,000. Say which figure is which. **Source:** the
   Tang census as transmitted; Xiong, Sui-Tang Chang'an (Michigan, 2000), for the urban reconstruction.
7. **Trap:** Poetry was the heart of the Tang examination from the start. **Truth:** Some form of belles-lettres
   composition (zawen) was required from 681, and regulated verse and rhapsody became standard in the jinshi
   examination only around the Tianbao era of 742 to 756; the precise year is genuinely debated. At c. 700 this
   is a development in progress. **Source:** Nathan Vedal, "Never Taking a Shortcut: Examination Poetry of the
   Tang Dynasty," Tang Studies 33 (2015), 38 to 61, DOI 10.1179/0737503415Z.00000000014.
8. **Trap:** Calling the Church of the East "Nestorian." **Truth:** The label is a polemical exonym that most
   current scholarship avoids; the stele calls the religion Jingjiao, the Luminous Religion, and locates it in
   Daqin. Use Church of the East, and mention the older label once so the reader can find the literature.
   **Source:** the stele's own title, translated at the Fordham sourcebook; current usage in the scholarship.
9. **Trap:** After 755 China closed itself off. **Truth:** The overland corridor contracted and Tibet took the
   Hexi region, but Guangzhou and Yangzhou grew as maritime ports, salt and commercial taxes rose, and the
   economy's center moved south rather than shutting. "Inward" describes lost reach in Central Asia, not
   withdrawal from trade. **Source:** the salt commission and two-tax record; ninth-century Arabic accounts of
   Guangzhou.

## Callback design

Ranked. The lesson takes exactly two; a strong candidate not used as a callback should carry a threadsOut entry.

1. **han-mandate (use this one).** Angle: the earlier lesson ends on a door "fifty men wide" that opened both
   ways, and calls Han recruitment a forerunner. Ask what actually changed in the seven centuries between a
   proposal for fifty academy disciples and an annual examination calendar with a palace round, and what did not
   change at all. The reward for having read both is precise: the routes the Han lesson listed (a father's rank,
   recommendation, clerkship, summons) are still the main roads under the Tang, and the reader can name them.
   The answer should refuse the meritocracy story in both directions: the Han was not yet an exam state, and the
   Tang was not one either.
2. **crown-of-cyrus (use this one).** Angle: both empires let subject communities translate the ruler into their
   own sacred language. Bel-iddin's cylinder makes Marduk the author of Cyrus's conquest; Jingjing's stele makes
   the Luminous Religion legible in Chinese imperial and cosmological idiom, under an emperor's edict. Ask what
   the two texts share as instruments, and what the 732 edict and the 845 suppression reveal about the terms. The
   answer should land on accommodation as revocable statecraft that still produced genuine room to live, which is
   the Cyrus lesson's own closing test applied to a different empire and century.
3. **silk-road-pulse (strong alternate; otherwise a threadsOut entry).** Angle: that lesson insisted the network
   was a chain of relays, connected because many local circuits overlapped and no one saw the whole. Chang'an's
   Western Market is what one end of that chain looks like when it becomes an address: a market office, a price
   list, a registered foreign merchant, a legal category. Ask what a relay network gains and loses when one of
   its ends becomes a licensed marketplace inside a walled city.
4. **jesus-to-constantine (fourth; use only if 2 is dropped).** Angle: imperial patronage transforms what it
   favors. Constantine's favor gave the church reach, buildings, courts, and councils, and bound it to the
   throne; Tang patronage gave Buddhism monasteries, land, and exemptions, and in 845 an emperor took the metal
   back. Ask what a religion trades for protection. Overlaps candidate 2, so do not use both.

Not recommended: byzantium-endures. The imperial-capital-as-machine comparison is real, but the Justinian lesson
already spent it on law and the dome, and a second "capital shows the state" callback repeats.

## Node-data flags

Do not edit the graph. Reported for Codex.

1. **Summary, "the examination system matured."** Contradicted by the research above. Under the Tang the
   examinations were annual, prestigious, and small; the great clans continued to supply most officials, and
   maturation belongs to the Song. Possible replacement for Codex to consider: "the examinations became an
   annual route into office without yet displacing birth and patronage." The lesson must not repeat the claim.
2. **Summary, "killed millions."** False precision. The 52.9 million to 16.9 million fall in the registers
   measures administrative collapse as well as death. Suggested direction: emptied the registers and broke the
   state's ability to see its own people.
3. **Summary, "poetry became a civil service skill."** True of the middle eighth century, not of the node's
   c. 700 date. Chronologically misleading as written; the lesson should date it.
4. **Summary, "China turned gradually inward and commerce moved south."** The southward shift is right.
   "Inward" overstates: maritime trade at Guangzhou and Yangzhou grew after the rebellion. Recommend nuance in
   the lesson even if the summary stands.
5. **Edge to `house-of-wisdom`: "After Talas in 751, captured Chinese papermakers gave Islam its paper."** The
   strongest node-data problem: an eleventh-century anecdote rejected by current scholarship. Recommend Codex
   rewrite the why toward what is defensible: papermaking spread west through Central Asia across the seventh
   and eighth centuries, and Abbasid book culture was built on paper that reached it through Sogdiana rather
   than through one battle. Fable's threadsOut sentence must not repeat the legend; correcting it is better.
6. **Hook, "Nestorian priests."** Contested exonym. The lesson should use Church of the East and mention the old
   label once.
7. **Hook, "a city of a million."** Defensible only as metropolitan shorthand. The lesson must say which
   population it means.
8. **Incoming edge from `grand-canal-spine`,** "Chang'an could feed a million people because southern grain
   moved north along the Sui canals," is sound and worth using: the last leg into Chang'an, past the Sanmen
   rapids on the Yellow River, was the chronic bottleneck, and it is part of why the court spent so much of the
   late seventh century at Luoyang. Good supporting mechanism, and it explains Wu Zetian's capital without a
   digression.
9. **Edge to `heian-and-genji`** is accurate; if the lesson dates the embassies, the last sailed in 838 and the program was formally abandoned in 894.
