# Lesson brief: tragic-stage

Research and architecture scaffolding only. No lesson prose.

## 1. Node contract

- ID: `tragic-stage`. Title: The Tragic Stage. Era III, sort -458, date field `c. 458 BCE`, region `europe`, thread `ideas`.
- Hook mechanism: a mass civic audience watches a chorus turn war, sacrifice, kinship, law, and divine justice into public argument; judges and competition decide a winner.
- Summary mechanism: Athenian tragedy is a state-supported festival institution; Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides are survivors of a much larger field; comedy and tragedy operate within civic speech and democratic life.
- Outgoing targets:
  - `socratic-turn`, type `collides`: Aristophanes's comic Socrates and Plato's later *Apology* belong to different genres and dates; the caricature should be treated as cultural background, not as a documented cause of the prosecution.
  - `rebirth-of-the-ancients`, type `echoes`: later readers recover Greek drama and make it part of Renaissance moral and civic education; the transmission is discontinuous and mediated.
- Core architecture: drama is not merely entertainment or a mirror of democracy. It is a costly civic practice that trains attention to competing speeches, motives, kinship, war, and consequence.

## 2. Chronology

- 508 to 507 BCE: Cleisthenes's reforms, roughly fifty years before the node, reorganize Athenian civic membership.
- 490 to 479 BCE: Persian Wars and their aftermath, a generation before the *Oresteia*. Aeschylus's war memory is not the same thing as a documentary report of one battle.
- 472 BCE: Aeschylus's *Persians*, a surviving tragedy about a recent war, predates the node and demonstrates that mythic and historical material could coexist in the theater.
- 458 BCE: conventional performance date for Aeschylus's *Oresteia*, including *Agamemnon*, *Libation Bearers*, and *Eumenides*, at the City Dionysia.
- 423 BCE: Aristophanes's *Clouds*, which caricatures Socrates, is about 35 years after the node.
- 399 BCE: Socrates's trial and death, nearly six decades after the *Oresteia* performance.
- c. 335 to 330 BCE: Aristotle's *Poetics*, roughly a century later, offers retrospective theory based on a selected surviving tradition, not a program for the 458 festival.

## 4. Debates and traps

- **Democracy mirror trap.** Tragedy is funded and organized by civic institutions, but plays can criticize, complicate, or refuse democratic self-congratulation. Do not make every chorus the voice of “the people.”
- **Audience trap.** The theater audience included citizens and visitors, with exclusions shaped by status and gender; exact attendance and the effect of the theorikon need source-specific treatment.
- **Origin trap.** “Drama was born from Dionysian worship” is too tidy. The Acropolis Museum gives institutional context, but the historical origins of drama are reconstructed from later and partial evidence.
- **Aeschylus trap.** The text of *Agamemnon* is not a report of a real sacrifice. It is a fifth-century performance that uses inherited myth to think about war, household authority, and justice.
- **Iphigenia trap.** Do not use the story as proof that Greek society actually sacrificed daughters at Aulis. The poem's sacrificial scene is evidence of dramatic argument, not event history.
- **Aristotle trap.** *Poetics* is later, selective, and theoretical. “Catharsis” is contested in translation and interpretation; do not present one English gloss as settled psychology.
- **Survival trap.** Seven plays by Aeschylus, seven by Sophocles, and eighteen by Euripides survive from hundreds of known or inferred works. The surviving canon is not a representative sample.
- **Socrates trap.** Aristophanes's comic caricature predates the trial but does not prove what jurors remembered or why they voted. Keep genres and dates separate.
- **Renaissance trap.** Later European recovery of tragedy is an echo, not continuous preservation of the original civic institution.

## 5. Scene evidence

- Place: Theater of Dionysus on the south slope of the Acropolis; sanctuary and civic festival context are secure.
- Objects and roles: chorus, masks and costumes where source-supported, orchestra, skene, festival officials, wealthy choregos funding a chorus, judges, crown or prize, and public procession.
- Textual anchor: *Agamemnon* opens with a watchman on a roof, a relay of beacon fires, and a chorus of Argive elders; the performance date and mythic time must not be confused.
- Civic pressure points: war dead, household grief, Iphigenia, Clytemnestra's authority, public speech, divine justice, and the transition from vendetta to civic judgment in the trilogy.
- Real people: Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, Aristophanes; individual private audience reactions are unavailable.
- Reconstruction boundary: an unnamed citizen spectator can be a composite only if `storyContext` says so. Exact seat, weather, neighbor, applause, and winning announcement require disclosure unless directly sourced.

- DOI metadata was checked against publisher records; direct Crossref API retrieval was unavailable. Repeat before citations.
- The graph hook says judges and chance decide the winner. The packet has not yet verified the exact selection procedure for the 458 contest. Keep “judged competition” broad unless a festival inscription is opened.
- No scene should claim to reconstruct an individual spectator's inner response. The strongest evidence is the text, festival institution, and material theater, not invented audience psychology.
