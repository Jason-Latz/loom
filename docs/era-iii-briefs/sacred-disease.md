# Reconnaissance packet: sacred-disease

## Node contract

- Title: The Sacred Disease.
- Date in seed: c. 400 BCE. Sort: -405. Region: Europe. Threads: ideas, craft.
- Hook facts to preserve: a Greek medical author rejects the special supernatural status of epilepsy and argues for observable causes, course, and prognosis.
- Seed summary: the Hippocratic corpus contains many treatises by many hands from about 400 BCE onward. It argues for natural causes and observed cases but is not the first clinical tradition or a clean break with religion. Humoral theories were later wrong; case histories, ethics, and environmental reasoning had a long afterlife. Hippocrates himself is hard to recover.
- Outgoing targets: `house-of-wisdom` and `first-universities`.
- Edge logic: transmission through Galen, Arabic translation, Latin learning, Salerno, Bologna, and Paris. Do not imply a straight Greek to Europe pipeline or a single Hippocrates authoring the corpus.

## Chronology

- The treatise is commonly placed around 400 BCE, but the date is approximate and authorship is unknown. Use “late fifth or early fourth century BCE” only if the writer keeps it qualified.
- Hippocrates is traditionally dated c. 460 to 375 BCE. That biography does not date the individual treatise or prove his authorship.
- The Hippocratic collection grew and was transmitted over later Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine, Arabic, and Latin periods. Do not call a fourth-century work a medieval hospital manual.
- Galen lived c. 129 to 216 CE, several centuries after the node. He was a later synthesizer, not a contemporary witness to the author of On the Sacred Disease.
- Early medieval Arabic and Latin translation activity belongs to later centuries. It is the bridge to the outgoing nodes, not part of the original Greek clinical scene.

## Debates and traps

- “Hippocrates discovered medicine” is false and teleological. Egyptian, Mesopotamian, Greek, and other healing traditions predate the corpus; the treatise is one argument within a plural field.
- “Sacred disease” probably refers to epilepsy, but the ancient author never supplies a modern diagnostic label. Keep the identification conventional and qualified.
- Natural cause does not mean secular disbelief. The treatise can call nature divine while rejecting exploitative purifications and incantations as explanations.
- Do not call humoral physiology a successful scientific theory. Its later influence and its empirical case attention are separate claims.
- Do not attribute the corpus or this treatise to Hippocrates without “traditionally attributed” or “a writer in the Hippocratic milieu”.
- The famous Oath is not a transparent description of every Hippocratic practitioner. Its divine opening and surgical restriction make the corpus internally diverse.
- Avoid “clinical method invented here”. The work offers observation, regimen, and prognosis, not modern trials or anatomy.

## Scene evidence

- A patient with seizures, foam, distorted eyes, loss of speech, and loss of motor control as described by the old translation. These are textual observations; do not invent a named patient.
- A medical writer confronting purifiers, magicians, and charlatans who prescribe prohibitions such as black clothing, goat skin, crossed limbs, or ritual restrictions.
- The text's physical vocabulary: air entering veins, brain as a seat of sensation and motion, phlegm, heat and cold, seasonal regimen.
- A scribe or physician working with case observations and recipes in a late fifth or early fourth-century Greek medical milieu may be used only as a composite if explicitly labeled.
- Later manuscript material: Arabic Hippocratic works and Galenic synthesis are useful objects for the transmission section, not for the original scene.
