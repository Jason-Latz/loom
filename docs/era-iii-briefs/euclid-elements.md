## Chronology

- Euclid is usually placed in Alexandria around 300 BCE, during the reign or institutional aftermath of Ptolemy I. His birth and death dates are uncertain; avoid the precise 325 to 265 BCE dates unless a source is named.
- The Elements compiles earlier work by figures such as Eudoxus and Theaetetus. Those predecessors are earlier than Euclid; the compilation is later than their results.
- The oldest surviving direct copies are much later than the composition. Ancient papyri and ostraca preserve fragments; ninth-century Greek manuscripts are later witnesses, not Euclid's autographs.
- Arabic translation and commentary belong mainly to the Abbasid and later medieval periods, centuries after Euclid. Latin translations from Arabic and Greek follow later still.
- Non-Euclidean geometry emerged in the nineteenth century CE. It is a reception history of the fifth postulate, not a discovery available to Euclid.

## Debates and traps

- Do not say Euclid invented geometry, proof, or the Elements' individual theorems. The compilation and arrangement are the central achievement.
- “Axiomatic” is useful but must be anchored in the actual definitions, postulates, common notions, and propositions. Do not project modern formal logic wholesale onto the ancient text.
- The Elements is not only plane geometry. Books on number, proportion, incommensurables, solid geometry, and other topics matter.
- Avoid the myth of a single pristine text. The work survives through textual transmission, editorial reconstruction, commentaries, and translations.
- Do not call Arabic scholars passive transmitters. The Arabic tradition translated, commented, altered, criticized, and extended Euclidean material.
- The fifth postulate was not “disproved” by non-Euclidean geometry. Alternative consistent geometries show independence from the other familiar axioms under modern formalization.
- Alexandria was a multilingual, Ptolemaic institution, not simply “Greek science” detached from Egypt or the wider eastern Mediterranean.

## Scene evidence

- Alexandria around 300 BCE; papyrus, reed pen, diagrams, straightedge and compass constructions are plausible material evidence, but no Euclid autograph or classroom transcript survives.
- Book I definitions of point, line, surface, circle, and angle; five postulates; common notions; proposition sequence.
- Book VII definitions of unit, number, prime, and composite; subtractive proof of coprimality.
- Proclus' later report about Euclid arranging Eudoxus and Theaetetus; use as a later witness, not a contemporaneous biography.
- Later objects for transmission: Arabic and Latin manuscripts, Ratdolt's 1482 printed edition, and modern digital facsimiles.
