## 1. Node contract

- ID: `zapotec-dawn`. Title: The Zapotec Dawn. Era III, sort -495, date field `c. 500 BCE`, region `americas`, threads `power`, `ideas`.
- Hook mechanism: Monte Albán's leveled mountaintop setting, captive imagery, glyphs, and calendrical signs join urban concentration to political knowledge.
- Summary mechanism: Oaxaca Valley communities established Monte Albán around 500 BCE; early monuments carry hieroglyphic names and calendar dates; the earliest writing question is debated against Olmec and other early Mesoamerican signs.
- Outgoing targets:
  - `teotihuacan-maya`, type `enables`: later regional cities share or adapt calendrical and writing technologies; the Zapotec neighborhood at Teotihuacan is much later than Monte Albán's founding.
  - `fifth-sun-aztecs`, type `echoes`: later Aztec calendar practice preserves an interlocking Mesoamerican calendrical logic; do not claim direct institutional continuity from Monte Albán to Tenochtitlan.
- Core architecture: city, writing, and calendar are co-produced technologies of authority, but the early signs are not all deciphered and the reason for the mountaintop relocation is not settled.

## 2. Chronology

- c. 600 BCE: San José Mogote Monument 3 and a calendrical glyph are earlier than Monte Albán's founding horizon in the Met synthesis; use this to avoid saying Monte Albán invented writing in Oaxaca.
- c. 500 BCE: Monte Albán founded or consolidated on a mountain site at the junction of Oaxaca's three valleys; exact founding process is not a single documented event.
- c. 496 to 221 BCE: the 2025 Latin American Antiquity study analyzes early and middle Preclassic Zapotec hieroglyphic texts and lunar-day count sequences; this overlaps the node and offers a more precise chronology for some inscriptions.
- c. 200 to 500 CE: the Zapotec neighborhood at Teotihuacan belongs to a later period. Do not present it as evidence for Monte Albán's foundation.
- c. 100 BCE to 550 CE: Teotihuacan's urban florescence overlaps later Monte Albán phases, not the whole city history.
- 800 to 850 CE: UNESCO and INAH place decline or abandonment of Monte Albán's ruling role here, far beyond the node date.
- Maya Long Count and later Mesoamerican inscriptions have different local histories. Shared calendrical structures do not imply one origin point.

## 4. Debates and traps

- **First city trap.** Monte Albán is among the earliest large cities in the Americas and a major early Mesoamerican urban center, but “first city” depends on definitions and comparison sets. Use the graph's wording carefully.
- **First writing trap.** San José Mogote signs precede the city; Olmec and Isthmian evidence is debated; early Zapotec signs are not all deciphered. Say “among the earliest securely dated writing or writing-like systems” only with the source and qualification.
- **Decipherment trap.** A glyph that identifies a person, place, or date is not automatically readable as a sentence. Keep “hieroglyphic” descriptive unless a reading is established.
- **Calendar trap.** The 260-day and 365-day cycles are distinct and interlock after 18,980 days or 52 calendar years. Do not call one a lunar calendar or claim the 260-day count is simply a solar observation.
- **Captive trap.** Danzantes imagery is often called captive or slain figure imagery, but identities, event sequence, and political function need source-specific wording.
- **Mountain trap.** Defensibility, visibility, ritual centralization, factional relocation, and labor coordination are competing explanations. Do not state that defense alone caused the move.
- **Mesoamerica as one state trap.** Shared calendars and visual conventions show interaction or convergent structure, not a single empire or a direct Zapotec state spreading everywhere.
- **Teotihuacan trap.** The Zapotec neighborhood at Teotihuacan is evidence of later migration or community presence, not proof that Monte Albán controlled Teotihuacan.
- **Aztec continuity trap.** Later Aztec use of interlocking calendars is an echo at the level of regional tradition, not an uninterrupted institutional chain.

## 5. Scene evidence

- Place: leveled ridge or mountain-top complex above Oaxaca's three valleys; archaeological setting includes plazas, temples, tombs, roads, water capture, and defensive works.
- Objects: greenstone or volcanic-tuff slabs, early carved captive figures, hill glyphs, calendrical signs, pottery, tomb goods, and quarry stone brought uphill.
- San José Mogote alternative: an earlier carved slab around 600 BCE with a reclining captive figure and “1 Earthquake” calendrical glyph; this can establish prehistory without placing the object at Monte Albán.
- Craft evidence: extraction and transport of greenstone or cantera, large-scale leveling and masonry, water management, and differentiated residences or tombs.
- Named people: modern archaeologists and epigraphers; no named Zapotec ruler is securely established for the c. 500 BCE scene in the candidate sources.
- Reconstruction boundary: a named builder, captive, priest, spoken language, political speech, or exact founding day is invented unless a primary inscription and its reading are supplied.
