# Reconnaissance packet: nok-terracotta

## Node contract

- Title: The Nok Terracottas.
- Date in seed: c. 900 to 300 BCE. Sort: -440. Region: Africa. Threads: craft, ideas.
- Hook facts to preserve: central Nigeria; elaborate coiffures; triangular eyes; nearby iron furnaces.
- Seed summary: Nok communities on the Jos Plateau made the earliest known large terracotta sculpture in sub-Saharan Africa and some of the earliest West African iron smelting. Furnaces are dated by the fifth century BCE. Independent invention versus trans-Saharan transmission remains unresolved. Excavated evidence currently indicates farmers without cities or kings; the sculptures' purpose is disputed.
- Outgoing targets: `bantu-and-aksum` and `golden-sahara`.
- Edge logic: echo, not descent. Do not say Nok is the single source of Bantu ironworking or later West African states. Use Nok to widen the map of African craft and ritual histories.

## Chronology

- The seed's c. 900 to 300 BCE range overlaps the broader Nok chronology in older museum literature, but current research also reports earlier settlement and later disappearance. Keep the node's date as a graph label, not as a claim that every figurine belongs to the same interval.
- The 1943 discovery of a head near the Nok village is modern research history, not ancient chronology. Bernard Fagg's Taruga excavations in 1961 are later evidence about an ancient site.
- Taruga's iron furnaces and nearby figurines should not be described as contemporaneous proof that the sculptures were made for smelting rituals. Association is archaeological; function is interpretive.
- The fifth century BCE furnace date in the seed is compatible with the node range. Do not place Nok alongside Athens, Panini, or the Warring States as if the evidence provides a single exact phase.
- Breunig's current synthesis treats dispersed farmers from the mid-second millennium BCE and standardized figurines from about 900 BCE onward. This extends before the seed label and should be marked as a broader research chronology.

## Debates and traps

- Do not repeat “oldest advanced civilization in Africa” or “first African civilization”. The evidence shows remarkable craft without a demonstrated city, king, or state.
- “Nok culture” is an archaeological label derived from a modern village name, not a securely recovered ancient self-name.
- Avoid the old assumption that figurines were automatically ancestors, deities, chiefs, or smelting guardians. Context is rare because early collecting and mining removed many objects from provenience.
- The disease-like faces are visual observations, not diagnoses or evidence for a medical cult.
- Do not infer a Bantu migration route from Nok terracotta style. The outgoing graph edges are later echoes, not a documented cultural pipeline.
- Chronology is a live issue. Published dates may refer to charcoal, associated sediment, thermoluminescence, or a mixed deposit. The writer must identify the dated material.
- Looting and colonial collecting are part of the modern evidence problem. Do not turn museum holdings into neutral samples of the original corpus.

## Scene evidence

- Jos Plateau and central Nigerian savanna; local clay mixed with gravel.
- Hollow, coil-built bodies; sun drying; kiln firing; air holes through eyes, nostrils, and mouth to reduce cracking.
- Triangular eyes, perforated pupils, large heads, individualized faces, elaborate hairstyles, jewelry, kneeling or seated postures.
- Taruga: thirteen iron-smelting furnaces; figurine fragments reported near and inside furnaces.
- Ifana: deposit reported as twelve terracotta fragments and two ceramic vessels near a burial; fragments reportedly broken and placed on stones with charcoal. Treat as reported interpretation, not settled ritual fact.
- Modern recovery scenes: tin mining, alluvial deposits, fragments used or discarded locally, and Fagg's Jos Museum work. These are evidence about discovery and preservation, not ancient life.
