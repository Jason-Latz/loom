# Reconnaissance packet: upanishadic-turn

## Node contract

- Graph node: **The Upanishadic Turn**, c. 700 to 500 BCE, `sasia`, ideas.
- Graph facts: early Upanishadic materials were composed orally in northern India and explore self, death, rebirth, karma, release, and the relation between `ātman` and `brahman`. Some influential passages identify them; other passages do not.
- Outgoing targets: `buddha-middle-path`, because Buddhist teaching participates in the same wider debate while denying a permanent essence; `panini-grammar`, because precise preservation and analysis of Sanskrit traditions helped generate later grammar.
- Seed conflict: “the Upanishads” is a corpus of layers, authors, genres, and dates, not a single book or a single doctrine.

## Chronology

- Early prose Upanishads, especially Bṛhadāraṇyaka and Chāndogya, are commonly placed around 700 to 500 BCE, but exact dates are uncertain.
- The Buddha’s dates are disputed, often placed in the fifth century BCE or somewhat earlier. Do not make every Upanishadic passage a direct response to the historical Buddha.
- Later Upanishads, roughly 300 to 100 BCE in the IEP’s staging, are not evidence for the ideas available in the earliest layer.
- Śaṅkara lived c. 700 CE, more than a millennium later; his non-dual reading powerfully shaped later reception but cannot be projected backward as the only early Upanishadic meaning.
- Oral composition and oral preservation continued after writing existed. “Oral” does not mean “unstructured,” and “written” does not mean “fixed at once.”

## Debates and traps

- “Upanishadic philosophy rejected ritual” is too simple. Early texts reinterpret ritual, internalize it, and sometimes move toward speculative inquiry while remaining embedded in Vedic authority.
- “All Upanishads teach ātman equals brahman” is false. The identification is famous and influential, but the early corpus contains competing accounts and does not speak in one voice.
- Karma and rebirth are not a single invention at one date. Their vocabulary and moral architecture develop across Vedic, Upanishadic, Buddhist, Jain, and later Hindu contexts.
- Avoid calling the Upanishadic turn the birth of Indian philosophy as though prior Vedic thought lacked reflection. The change is one of emphasis, genre, and debate, not the appearance of thought from nowhere.
- Do not force direct influence from the Upanishads into Buddhism. The traditions share a debate-world; textual borrowing and specific rebuttal require evidence.
- Do not back-project Śaṅkara’s Advaita into every early passage.

## Scene evidence

- Real texts: Bṛhadāraṇyaka, Chāndogya, Taittirīya, Aitareya, and Kauṣītakī as early prose Upanishads; later texts belong to later stages.
- Real dialogue figures: Yājñavalkya, Uddālaka Āruṇi, Śvetaketu, Gārgya Bālāki, Ajātaśatru, Indra, and Virocana appear in textual dialogues; their historical status is not equivalent to a dated biography.
- Real setting categories: teacher and student, household ritual, forest or retreat, royal court, debate, recitation, and death instruction. Select a setting that the chosen passage supports.
- Sensory anchors available from the texts: repeated recitation, fire ritual, breath, bodily analogy, silence, and memorized question and answer. These are textual motifs, not excavated descriptions of one school.
- Primary question clusters: what survives death, whether the self is body, breath, consciousness, person, or cosmic principle, and whether knowledge can release a person from rebirth.
