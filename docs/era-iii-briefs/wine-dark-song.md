# Reconnaissance packet: wine-dark-song

## Node contract

- Graph node: **The Wine-Dark Song**, c. 750 to 650 BCE, `europe`, ideas.
- Graph facts: the Iliad and Odyssey crystallized from generations of oral formulaic song, probably entering written form between the mid-eighth and mid-seventh centuries BCE. The poems became a shared Greek past across scattered communities.
- Outgoing targets: `polis-experiment`, because panhellenic poems, shrines, and festivals helped scattered communities imagine a shared cultural world; `tragic-stage`, because Athenian drama reworked epic legend; `socratic-turn`, because Plato’s critique makes poetry’s civic and moral authority a direct philosophical problem.
- Seed conflict: “Homer” may denote a historical poet, a traditional name, or a shorthand for a long performance tradition. Do not treat the blind singer as a secure biography.

## Chronology

- The Trojan War tradition is older than the surviving poems; the historical date of a war, if any, is not the date of the Iliad’s composition.
- The dominant scholarly placement of the Iliad is late eighth century BCE, with some arguments for the seventh century. The Odyssey is generally later than the Iliad.
- Alphabetic writing reappeared in the Greek world by the eighth century BCE, but “written down” is not a single event. Dictation, performance, copying, and later stabilization may all matter.
- The Athenian Panathenaic festival and the later fifth-century tragic stage are later reception contexts, not the original setting of the epic tradition.
- The Iliad contains 24 books and approximately 15,693 lines in the standard Greek text. The line count is a textual convention, not a measure of one uninterrupted ancient performance.

## Debates and traps

- Do not write “Homer was blind” as fact. The blind singer is a character and a later biographical tradition, not secure evidence about the composer.
- Oral formulaic poetry is not merely memorized text. Formulas can be a compositional toolkit used in live performance, and a fixed written text can emerge from a variable tradition.
- “Writing preserved the Iliad exactly” is too strong. The surviving text is the result of selection, copying, scholarly editing, and transmission.
- Do not conflate the Mycenaean Bronze Age with the eighth-century performance world. The poems preserve old material in a later social and linguistic form.
- The Iliad is not a national constitution. Shared gods and heroes could support Greek identity while different poleis fought, traded, and excluded people differently.
- Avoid the teleology that oral poetry is an immature form waiting for writing. Oral composition is a high-skill technology with its own aesthetics.

## Scene evidence

- Real text object: a 24-book hexameter poem, roughly 15,693 lines in the standard text.
- Real technique: formulaic epithets, repeated scenes, type-scenes, meter, and modular diction that help performance and composition.
- Real performance setting: an aoidos or rhapsode before an audience is historically plausible, but a specific named singer or date must be composite unless sourced.
- Material transition: alphabetic writing, writing surfaces, and later text stabilization can frame the “song becoming text” mechanism; avoid inventing a surviving first manuscript.
- Sensory evidence: dactylic hexameter cadence, repeated names and epithets, sung speech, bronze armor, dust, ships, and fire are present in the poem, but any selected image must be tied to a cited passage rather than treated as archaeology.
- Useful human figures: Achilles, Hector, Priam, and Odysseus are literary characters; Homer is disputed. Keep literary and historical status separate.
