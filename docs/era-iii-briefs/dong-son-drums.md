# Reconnaissance packet: dong-son-drums

## Node contract

- Title: The Bronze Drums of Dong Son.
- Date in seed: c. 500 to 100 BCE. Sort: -350. Region: East Asia. Threads: craft, wealth.
- Hook facts to preserve: northern Vietnam and Red River delta; large bronze drums; one-pour casting claim requires verification; feathered dancers, long boats, and southward distribution.
- Seed summary: Dong Son communities combined bronze casting, wet-rice farming, and river trade. Large drums occur from Yunnan to eastern Indonesia. Their uses remain debated and are often associated with status and ceremony. The distribution shows a South China Sea exchange world before later Indian and Chinese court dominance. Han conquest took the Red River homeland in 111 BCE; related casting continued elsewhere.
- Outgoing targets: `qin-hammer` and `monsoon-marketplace`.
- Edge logic: collision with Qin and Han expansion, then echo into later maritime exchange. Do not make the drum distribution a single ethnic migration map or equate all Heger drums with one production center.

## Chronology

- The graph's c. 500 to 100 BCE range is a working slice. The broader Dong Son cultural sequence is often extended earlier and later, while individual drums have different dates and find contexts.
- Qin annexation of northern Vietnam is conventionally dated 214 to 211 BCE, and Han conquest of Nanyue and the Red River region to 111 BCE. The seed specifically names 111 BCE; do not describe Han rule as present throughout the entire node.
- Bronze drums found in island Southeast Asia may have been imported, locally copied, or made in multiple centers. A find date is not a production date.
- “Before Indian and Chinese courts became major forces there” is a regional comparison, not a claim that China had no contact or that Indian trade began only later.
- Wet-rice farming has a longer chronology than the named drum horizon. Do not imply the drums invented rice agriculture.

## Debates and traps

- Do not use “Dong Son people” as a secure ethnic or linguistic identity. Dong Son is an archaeological culture label.
- “One pour” is a technical claim that needs a specific casting study or object report. Do not repeat it from the seed without verifying whether the drum is cast in one piece, assembled, or reconstructed.
- Heger classification is a history of scholarship, not a neutral map of cultural ownership. Some drums were made locally in styles inspired by other drums.
- Do not equate distribution with migration, conquest, or a single maritime empire.
- The drum's purpose is unsettled. Status, ceremony, warfare, rain, burial, feasting, and sound are hypotheses with uneven evidence.
- Avoid nationalist teleology that makes Dong Son the uncomplicated origin of modern Vietnamese identity. The Oxford record itself describes a contested protohistory.
- Han conquest did not erase local casting traditions; do not present 111 BCE as an instant cultural replacement.

## Scene evidence

- Red River delta and northern Vietnamese river systems; wet rice, waterways, and river transport.
- Bronze drum surfaces with star or sunburst motifs, boats, feathered human figures, animals, and geometric bands. Verify the exact motif set on a chosen object before detailed description.
- Large hollow cast bronze objects; mold construction and alloy analysis should be consulted before describing the technical process.
- Timor-Leste finds as evidence of long-distance movement and uncertain archaeological context.
- Museum object records in Vietnam, New York, London, and Pasadena; distinguish full drums, models, and later copies.
