# Lesson brief: scythian-gold

Research and architecture scaffolding only. No lesson prose.

## 1. Node contract

- ID: `scythian-gold`. Title: Gold of the Grass Sea. Era III, sort -530, date field `c. 700 to 300 BCE`, region `sasia`, threads `wealth`, `craft`.
- Hook mechanism: mobile pastoral communities used elite burial, horse equipment, textiles, tattoos, and animal-style gold to materialize status without relying on permanent cities.
- Summary mechanism: Scythian and related steppe peoples occupied a broad zone from the Black Sea to the Altai; they traded grain, hides, and horses with Greek colonies and Persia; frozen Pazyryk burials preserve organic materials.
- Outgoing targets:
  - `silk-road-pulse`, type `echoes`: steppe exchange and style movement predate later named Silk Roads; do not imply one continuous route.
  - `genghis-exchange`, type `echoes`: compare recurring political advantages of horse mobility and pastoral logistics; do not collapse Scythian, Saka, and Mongol societies into one people.
- Core architecture: replace the failed “nomads lacked civilization” frame with mobility as an economic and political specialization, while retaining internal differences and the evidence gap.

## 2. Chronology

- c. 700 to 300 BCE: graph date for Scythian and related steppe worlds; not a single ethnic polity with one capital.
- 7th to 6th centuries BCE: Scythian animal-style art and northern Black Sea interaction with Greek communities are securely within the early Iron Age horizon.
- 513 BCE: Darius I's campaign against Scythians, conventionally placed during the reign of Darius; Herodotus's narrative is later and rhetorically shaped.
- c. 440 BCE: Herodotus writes the surviving Greek account of Scythians, roughly a century after much of the early material in the graph date and during the later part of the Scythian period.
- Pazyryk burials: commonly placed in the 5th to 3rd centuries BCE, overlapping the graph date. Pazyryk is a related Scytho-Siberian or Saka cultural horizon, not proof that every Black Sea Scythian wore tattoos or used the same burial form.
- 4th to 3rd centuries BCE: major royal kurgans and Greek-Scythian art exchanges continue; do not date every famous gold object to the same generation.
- Hellenistic and later Sarmatian periods postdate the core “Scythian” frame. The British Museum phalera record itself warns that some objects are Sarmatian or later.

## 4. Debates and traps

- **Nomad trap.** “Nomad” can erase seasonal routes, farming, local production, political hierarchy, and trade. Use mobile pastoralist where the evidence permits and keep agro-pastoral variation visible.
- **Scythian label trap.** Greek authors used “Scythian” across different groups; modern archaeology uses Scythian, Saka, and Scytho-Siberian in overlapping but non-identical ways. Do not make the Black Sea and Altai one village.
- **Gold trap.** Gold survives and attracts looting, so elite burials overrepresent wealth and craft. The evidence cannot measure ordinary households from royal kurgans alone.
- **Greek-made object trap.** Greek workshops produced some objects for steppe clients. Greek style is not proof of Greek ownership or Greek cultural dominance.
- **Herodotus trap.** His account includes informant knowledge, literary ethnography, and Greek analogies. Use it as a source for Greek representations and possible practices, not as an unfiltered voice of the steppe.
- **Pazyryk trap.** Permafrost preserves textiles, tattooed skin, and horse tack that usually disappear. Absence of such material in other regions is not evidence of absence.
- **Griffin trap.** A repeated animal motif does not have one proven mythological meaning. The Met essay supports interaction and motif history, not a decoded Scythian theology.
- **Violence trap.** Herodotus's descriptions of sacrifice, executions, and warfare are not the whole society. They also should not be softened into a romantic “free horse people” image.

## 5. Scene evidence

- Place options: northern Black Sea royal kurgan; Altai Pazyryk barrow; Greek colony interface on the Black Sea. Do not merge them without `storyContext` disclosure.
- Objects: gold plaques and phalerae, felt carpet, horse bridle and saddle covering, wooden chariot or pole, tattooed mummy skin, animal-style predator or griffin imagery.
- Preserved materials from Pazyryk: wool and felt textiles, wood, leather, fur, horse corpses, mummies, and decorated tack; these are archaeological details, not generic nomad decoration.
- Documentary person: Herodotus, writing later in Greece; never give him a first-person view inside a Scythian tomb unless explicitly reconstructing a later literary encounter.
- Safe bodily evidence: tattoos preserve lines and animal forms; the 2025 paper may be used for tool and technique questions, not private meaning.
- Reconstruction boundary: unnamed mourners, exact burial sequence, spoken language, weather, and owner biography are invented unless a specific excavation record is used and disclosed.
