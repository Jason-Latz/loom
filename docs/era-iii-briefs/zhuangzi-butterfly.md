# Reconnaissance packet: zhuangzi-butterfly

## Node contract

- Title: Zhuangzi's Butterfly.
- Date in seed: c. fourth century BCE. Sort: -320. Region: East Asia. Thread: ideas.
- Hook facts to preserve: the butterfly dream is a philosophical device, not a decorative anecdote; the question asks whether Zhuang Zhou dreamed the butterfly or the butterfly dreams Zhuang Zhou.
- Seed summary: the Zhuangzi contains a fourth-century BCE core associated with Zhuang Zhou and later layers. It uses parables, jokes, monsters, artisans, and arguments to loosen confidence in fixed distinctions. It treats death with unusual calm and later shaped Daoism and Chinese Buddhist vocabulary.
- Outgoing targets: `garden-and-stoa` and `neo-confucian-turn`.
- Edge logic: echo with Hellenistic therapy through altered perspective, not contact or shared origin. Collision with Neo-Confucianism through later responses to Daoist and Buddhist challenges.

## Chronology

- Zhuang Zhou is generally placed in the latter half of the fourth century BCE, roughly contemporary with Mencius and later Mohists. Exact biographical dates are uncertain.
- The received Zhuangzi is a layered compilation. Guo Xiang's edition dates to the early fourth century CE, about six centuries after the core figure; do not present the 33-chapter received text as a transcript by one author.
- The butterfly passage is in chapter 2, traditionally among the “Inner Chapters”, but authorship of even the Inner Chapters is a scholarly hypothesis, not a secure autograph.
- “Daoism” as a unified religious or philosophical school is a later classification. Avoid using it as if Zhuang Zhou belonged to a modern institutional denomination.
- The Garden and Stoa are later Hellenistic developments, generally founded around 300 BCE, after the likely Zhuang Zhou core. The node edge is a thematic comparison, not contemporaneity or influence.

## Debates and traps

- Do not write “Zhuangzi wrote the Zhuangzi”. Attribute the passage to “the Zhuangzi” or “the chapter traditionally linked to Zhuang Zhou” unless the sentence is explicitly about tradition.
- The butterfly is not securely a symbol of reincarnation, soul, or modern simulation theory. The passage ends with transformation and a difference, not a neat answer.
- Do not turn “relativism” into “anything goes”. The text investigates distinctions, language, skill, perspective, and changing forms of life; its ethical implications are debated.
- Daoism is not a single fourth-century institution. Avoid projecting later religious forms backward.
- The Butcher Ding, useless tree, and other vignettes are not empirical case reports. They are literary-philosophical devices.
- Do not claim direct influence on Greek philosophy, Buddhism, or modern Western philosophy. Later Chinese Buddhist reception is a transmission question, not proof of original intent.
- Avoid the inherited “mystical anti-rational sage” stereotype. The text engages language, argument, and rival theories while using humor to unsettle them.

## Scene evidence

- A dreamer waking without a secure boundary between waking Zhuang Zhou and dreamed butterfly.
- Chinese textual form: chapter 2, compact parable, paired perspectives, final question, transformation language.
- Literary objects and figures: butterfly, fish, talking skull, butcher, wheelwright, cicada catcher, useless tree, Confucius as a recurring character.
- The text's material history: layered chapters, later editorial arrangement, commentary by Guo Xiang in the early fourth century CE.
- Use sensory evidence only for the text's images: winged movement, knife rhythm, wood grain, wind, or a skull in a field. These are textual motifs, not reconstructed eyewitness scenes.
