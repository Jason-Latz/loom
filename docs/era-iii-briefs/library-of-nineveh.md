# Reconnaissance packet: library-of-nineveh

## Node contract

- Graph node: **The Library at Nineveh**, c. 660 BCE, `swasia`, ideas and power.
- Graph facts: Ashurbanipal’s palace collections at Nineveh gathered roughly 30,000 tablets and fragments covering omens, rituals, lexical lists, medicine, laments, literature, and the fullest surviving Epic of Gilgamesh. Collection was administrative and royal policy, not private curiosity alone.
- Outgoing targets: `israel-one-god`, because Assyria conquered the northern kingdom and besieged Jerusalem; `star-diaries`, because Nineveh’s scholarly collections belong to the Mesopotamian tradition of systematic celestial observation; `house-of-wisdom`, because later caliphal institutions also linked knowledge collection with rule.
- Seed conflict: “library” is a useful modern label, but the Nineveh collections were palace libraries, archives, scholarly repositories, and copied or requisitioned tablets. Do not import a modern public-library model.

## Chronology

- Ashurbanipal ruled 668 to c. 627 BCE, with some museum records using 668 to 631 BCE for the effective imperial period. Use the regnal range cautiously and distinguish reign from collection activity.
- Nineveh fell in 612 BCE. The burning and collapse of the city damaged and sometimes baked clay tablets, helping preserve them, but not every tablet came from the same event or room.
- The collection predates Alexandria’s famous library by roughly two centuries. Do not claim direct institutional descent from Nineveh to Alexandria.
- The Epic of Gilgamesh tablets in the library preserve earlier Babylonian material; the stories are much older than Ashurbanipal’s reign.
- Excavations began in the nineteenth century, long after the ancient collection. Modern “30,000” figures may count tablets, fragments, and the wider Nineveh corpus differently.

## Debates and traps

- Do not call Nineveh the “first library” without defining library and acknowledging earlier temple, palace, and private collections.
- “Ashurbanipal collected all knowledge” is a royal ideological aim, not proof that the surviving corpus was complete or encyclopedic in the modern sense.
- The tablets were not books in a uniform format. Genre, language, colophon, provenance, and function vary.
- The fire that preserved clay is contingent. It also destroyed organic writing boards, rooms, and context; preservation is not the same as completeness.
- Do not say Ashurbanipal personally wrote every text. He presents himself as literate and learned, while professional scribes copied, edited, and organized material.
- The Gilgamesh text is not an Assyrian invention. Nineveh preserves a late version of a much older Mesopotamian literary tradition.
- Avoid a simple “knowledge versus empire” moral. Collection could preserve learning and enact domination through requisition, surveillance, omen interpretation, and control of scribal labor.

## Scene evidence

- Real person: Ashurbanipal, a king who claims literacy and scholarly competence in royal inscriptions and library colophons.
- Real objects: clay tablets, colophons, lexical lists, omen series, ritual tablets, medical texts, laments, epics, and administrative documents.
- Real place: the palace and mound complex at Kouyunjik, ancient Nineveh, in modern northern Iraq.
- Material details: wet clay impressed with a stylus, tablet edges, colophon lines, shelving or storage contexts, fire-baked fragments, and labor by specialist scribes.
- Real text episode: a royal demand for tablets or copies from Babylonia, but the exact wording and tablet should be chosen from the British Museum or ORACC record rather than invented.
- Safe sensory evidence: clay dust, stylus pressure, stacks of tablets, and the heat of the destruction layer are evidence categories. Do not claim a specific room’s smell or sound without an archaeological report.
