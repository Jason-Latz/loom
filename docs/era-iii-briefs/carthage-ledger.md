## 1. Node contract

- ID: `carthage-ledger`. Title: Carthage, the Merchant Republic. Era III, sort -505, date field `c. 500 BCE`, region `africa`, thread `wealth`.
- Hook mechanism: a North African city joined fertile hinterland, tribute, maritime exchange, Iberian metals, grain, craft, and a constitution later examined by Aristotle.
- Summary mechanism: Phoenician foundation in the late ninth century BCE; political development through magistrates, councils, assemblies, and wealthy families; agricultural and imperial power alongside commerce; Hanno's voyage survives in Greek translation.
- Outgoing targets:
  - `roman-republic-breaks`, type `collides`: Punic wars, captives, provinces, and wealth form one possible background to Roman elite competition; the peasant-displacement claim remains debated.
  - `golden-sahara`, type `echoes`: Herodotus's Atlantic gold story is an ancient literary report; it is not proof of a continuous Carthaginian trans-Saharan route.
- Core architecture: the node corrects the “merchant city only” frame. Carthage's wealth rested on land, labor, tribute, production, ports, and political institutions together.

## 2. Chronology

- Late ninth century BCE: conventional foundation horizon for Carthage, with exact foundation traditions and the Dido story treated as legend rather than date evidence.
- Sixth century BCE: UNESCO places Carthage's expansion into a major western Mediterranean power from this period; this overlaps the node's c. 500 BCE sort date.
- Hanno's voyage: generally placed in the sixth or fifth century BCE; the surviving text is a Greek translation or adaptation of a lost Punic account, so exact date and route remain uncertain.
- c. 500 BCE: the graph node is not the founding moment; it is a political and economic maturity snapshot.
- Aristotle: 384 to 322 BCE, at least a century after the node's date. His *Politics* is an important later description, not a contemporary constitutional record.
- Fourth century BCE: excavations indicate substantial changes to the circular harbor and ship sheds; do not project the later harbor form back onto the ninth-century settlement.
- 264 to 146 BCE: Punic Wars, culminating in Rome's destruction of Carthage in 146 BCE. These wars are later than the node but relevant to the outgoing Roman collision.

## 4. Debates and traps

- **Merchant republic trap.** “Merchant” is a useful thread label, not a complete constitutional category. Aristotle's evidence points to wealth and office; archaeology points to land, production, ports, and labor.
- **Republic trap.** Carthage had magistrates, councils, assemblies, and elite families, but the modern word republic can hide noncitizen labor, empire, slavery, and oligarchic power. Use it as a cautious comparative label.
- **Aristotle trap.** His fourth-century BCE description postdates the node and uses Greek constitutional categories. Avoid treating suffetes, the council, and the assembly as static institutions across all Punic history.
- **Hanno trap.** The *Periplus* is not an intact Punic logbook. It survives in Greek, may be abridged, and contains place-name and ethnographic uncertainties.
- **Gold trap.** Herodotus's gold-barter story is a literary report about exchange on the Atlantic shore, not proof that Carthage possessed a direct gold route later used by camel caravans.
- **Tophet trap.** Do not use a contested child-sacrifice claim as the dramatic center of a lesson about economy and constitutional life. If mentioned, show the disagreement and distinguish cremation, burial, ritual dedication, and cause of death.
- **Port dating trap.** The famous circular harbor and stone ship sheds are later than the node's c. 500 BCE point and may not describe the original port.
- **Roman-propaganda trap.** Carthage's enemies supplied much of the surviving narrative. Counterbalance Greek and Roman literary sources with archaeology, epigraphy, and Punic material evidence.
- **Africa framing trap.** Carthage was a North African polity with a Mediterranean reach, not a European commercial appendage. Keep the fertile African hinterland and Punic population visible.

- Named people: Hanno as an attributed expedition leader; Aristotle as later analyst; Mago as agronomist in later tradition, not a securely documented person for a 500 BCE scene.
- Sensory facts: harbor water, stone or timber ship infrastructure, amphora transport, North African grain and cultivated land can be used only when anchored to the chosen source. A named trader's bargain, ledger entry, or council debate is reconstruction.
- No surviving Punic narrative archive gives a private Carthaginian voice for the node. Treat absence of text as an architectural constraint, not an invitation to invent dialogue.
