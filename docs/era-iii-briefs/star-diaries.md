- Hook mechanism: repeated sky observation was kept beside prices, weather, and river levels across many generations.
- Summary mechanism: the record runs from at least 652 BCE; surviving tablets are only a fraction; scribes extracted period relations and predictive arithmetic for eclipses and planets; Greek and later Islamic astronomy received parts of this inheritance.
- Outgoing targets:
  - `ionian-awakening`, type `echoes`: use adjacency to compare older Babylonian observation with a newer Greek argumentative register; do not assert that Thales predicted the eclipse by using a Babylonian tablet.
  - `new-eyes-on-the-sky`, type `enables`: later transmission through Ptolemy into early modern astronomy; distinguish documented transmission from a continuous unbroken chain.
- Core architecture: the diary is a hybrid instrument, not a modern observatory log. It joins observation, divination, calendrical administration, numerical procedure, local environmental monitoring, and political record keeping.

## 2. Chronology

- 652 BCE: earliest diary date in the Sachs and Hunger corpus; this is roughly 67 years before the traditional 585 BCE date for Thales's eclipse.
- 625 to 539 BCE: Neo-Babylonian period; Cyrus takes Babylon in 539 BCE. Do not treat the whole seven-century program as an Achaemenid invention.
- c. 600 BCE: the Goal-Year method is attested in the scholarly tradition; the diary record and procedure texts are related but not identical genres.
- 585 BCE: Herodotus's story of Thales predicting an eclipse belongs to a later Greek literary tradition. The eclipse date is secure enough astronomically, the method and advance warning are not.
- 331 to 323 BCE: Alexander's conquest and death are recorded in Babylonian astronomical material, but individual tablets and copies need separate dating.
- c. second century BCE: Hipparchus's observations and mathematical work postdate the first diaries by several centuries and overlap the late diary period only at its margins. Ptolemy is second century CE, not a contemporary of the diaries.
- 61 BCE: the diary series' conventional endpoint in the surviving corpus. “Seven centuries” describes the program's chronological span, not uninterrupted survival of every year.

## 4. Debates and traps

- **Observation versus omen.** Diaries record celestial appearances and terrestrial conditions in the same scholarly world as omen interpretation. Do not use “science replaced astrology.” The categories were not yet separate in the modern sense.
- **Diary versus procedure text.** A diary reports an observation on a date; an ephemeris or procedure text calculates recurrent values. Brack-Bernsen and Ossendrijver address procedures, not proof that every diarist performed the calculations personally.
- **The seven-century trap.** The corpus is serial but fragmentary. Only a small portion of the original tablets survives, and Volume IV includes undated material. The claim should be “a program persisted across roughly seven centuries,” not “a single continuous logbook.”
- **Thales trap.** The 585 BCE eclipse is a story transmitted by Herodotus and later writers. Modern astronomical back-calculation does not identify Thales's method. The fact that Babylonian diaries predate it proves proximity and opportunity, not borrowing.
- **Greek exceptionalism trap.** Hipparchus and Ptolemy transformed inherited materials, but the evidence supports a mixed Babylonian and Greek history, not a Greek invention ex nihilo and not Babylonian ownership of all later Greek theory.
- **Prediction trap.** The Saros is a period relation useful for lunar eclipses, but visibility, timing, and local conditions matter. Avoid “they could predict every eclipse.”
- **Sexagesimal trap.** Base 60 is a positional computational system, not evidence that Babylonians had the same mathematical concepts or purposes as modern mathematics.
- **Weather and prices trap.** The combination of sky, water, weather, and commodities is evidence of scholarly administration and patterned observation. It is not proof that the diaries were a neutral economic series or that each entry was state policy.

## 5. Scene evidence

- Real object: baked clay tablet, Babylon provenance, cuneiform wedges, damaged edges, museum number 34987.
- Writing materials and bodily actions available from the corpus: clay tablet, stylus impressions, repeated date formulae, columns of observations, gaps where tablets are broken.
- Record categories: lunar phase and visibility, planetary first and last appearances, stations, conjunctions, eclipses, weather, river level, commodity values, political events.
- Mathematical objects: sexagesimal place values; Saros of 223 lunar months; zigzag functions; a tablet procedure using a trapezoid-like area for Jupiter's displacement.
- Place evidence: Babylon as the principal archive location in the cited material; Euphrates level as a recurring local measurement.
- Human evidence: named scholars Sachs, Hunger, Brack-Bernsen, Ossendrijver, Hayakawa, and Rochberg are modern investigators. No ancient diary author is securely named in the candidate sources.
- Sensory facts that are safe as object evidence: clay surface, wedge-shaped impressions, broken tablet edges, the physical compression of a long record into a small tablet. Any specific night, hand, room, weather, or private motive is reconstruction and must be labeled as such.
