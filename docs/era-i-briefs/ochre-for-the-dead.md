## 1. Graph target

- Node id: `ochre-for-the-dead`. Title: Ochre for the Dead. Era 1 (The Long Dawn). Date field `c. 120,000 to 90,000 BCE`, sort -110000, region `swasia`, x 60. Threads: `ideas` only.
- Global index 2. Only two nodes precede it in the main sequence: `first-of-our-kind` (Jebel Irhoud, c. 300,000 BCE) and `the-tended-fire` (Qesem, c. 300,000 BCE). **Neither has a written lesson yet.** There are no incoming edges.
- Outgoing edges (three, all `echoes`):
  - `tribe-of-strangers` ("Furnished burial preserves a shared mortuary practice that could bind survivors, without revealing a shared doctrine about the unseen."). **Demands:** the lesson must set up burial as a *practice held in common* by a group, and must stop short of doctrine. Give the reader the idea that a repeated shared act can bind people without anyone agreeing on what it means.
  - `paint-in-the-dark` ("A painted cave and a furnished grave give physical bodies to the same early convictions about meaning and death."). **Demands:** the lesson must establish that people were spending real effort on objects with no survival payoff, so the painted caves 50,000-plus years later land as the same impulse at higher power, not as a separate invention.
  - `gods-of-the-city` ("Later temples also materialized relationships with the unseen, but no direct institutional lineage connects them to Paleolithic graves."). **Demands:** the lesson must explicitly refuse the ladder. Do not let a Qafzeh grave become the seed of Ur. The edge label already does the refusing; the prose has to match it.
- Note: `spark-of-mind` (written, index 3, c. 100,000 BCE, Blombos) sits **immediately after** this node and its summary already claims "burying the dead with care" as part of its own package. See Node-data flags §8.

**Job in the atlas.** This is the atlas's first node about a thought rather than a body or a technique, and it is the only preliterate node that can carry the ideas thread on its own terms. Its job is to install a discipline the reader will need for the next three hundred and sixty nodes: how to read a mind from a hole in the ground, and where the reading has to stop. `first-of-our-kind` gives the reader a species; `the-tended-fire` gives it a hearth; this node gives it the first act we can point at and say a human did that for a reason no animal has. It also has to do a second, harder job that `spark-of-mind` will not do: show that the ratchet slips. The Qafzeh ochre record stops dead after layer XVII, and pigment then all but disappears from the Levant for tens of thousands of years. Era I otherwise reads as accumulation; this node is where the reader learns that meaning can be lost.

## 2. Preferred scene

**Qafzeh Cave, Lower Galilee, roughly 92,000 years ago: the burial of Qafzeh 11, a twelve to thirteen year old with a healed hole in the front of the skull, laid in a dug pit with two red deer antlers across the chest and touching the palms of the hands.**

This is the strongest human-scale scene in the whole of Era I because the biography and the funeral are both excavated, not inferred.

**Documentation ledger.**

*Attested (Coqueugniot, Dutour, Arensburg, Duday, Vandermeersch and Tillier, PLOS ONE 9(7): e102822, 2014, open access; Vandermeersch's original 1970 report is the primary excavation record):*
- The individual was circa 12 to 13 years old at death, aged by dental maturation. Sex not determined; the paper writes "his/her". Use a sex-neutral child.
- A depressed fracture of the right frontal squama, quadrangular, measured at 29.7 x 23.4 x 26.5 x 11.7 mm, with fracture lines radiating star-like from the impact point. Blunt force.
- Bone remodelling around the lesion proves the injury happened well before death; the paper says the healing response "had probably not undergone its complete trajectory before the death."
- Endocranial volume is only 81 to 86 percent of what the dental age predicts, from which the authors infer the trauma occurred around age six and retarded brain growth. So: injured at about six, died at about twelve or thirteen, roughly six or seven years of living in between.
- The damaged region covers Brodmann areas 6 and 8 and possibly the orbital part of area 44: movement, task rules, coping with uncertainty, visual attention and eye movement, and social communication. The authors conclude the child probably had personality changes and neurological and psychological difficulties.
- The corpse "was originally lying in a pit on its back, the head turned to the right with upper limbs flexed", hands together near the face, oriented west.
- "Two deer antlers were lying on the upper part of the adolescent's chest, near his/her face and they were in close contact with the palmar side of the hand bones." The paper says this arrangement "attested a funerary offering and not an accidental incorporation." Figure 5's caption calls them **red deer** antlers.
- Recovered at the bottom of the Mousterian sequence in front of the cave entrance; circa 90,000 to 100,000 years before present, marine isotope stage 5b to 5c.

*Reconstruction, and must be labelled as such in `storyContext`:* any name, any face, any weather, any dialogue, any mourner, who dug, who carried the antlers, what anyone felt, whether the child was cared for tenderly or grudgingly, whether the antlers were a gift or an instrument. The authors' "social care" reading is an inference, a good one, but an inference.

*Real named person available:* **no.** No name survives from anywhere on Earth for another eighty-odd thousand years. Say so; it is a fact worth a sentence.

*Quantities to check before printing:* the fracture dimensions (four figures, easy to garble); the 81 to 86 percent endocranial figure; the age at injury (an inference, not a measurement); the layer number (see Trap 8); the species of deer (see Trap 1).

**Why this scene.** It dramatizes the node's mechanism instead of decorating it. A group carried a damaged child for years, then, at the end, did the one thing that costs effort and returns nothing: dug a hole, arranged the body, and put something in it. The reader can feel the arithmetic of that without being told.

### Alternate 1: the ochre corner (safest, no invented person)

The processing area on the terrace. Attested from Hovers, Ilani, Bar-Yosef and Vandermeersch, *Current Anthropology* 44(4), 2003, 491-522: seventy-one ochre pieces studied and a minimum of eighty-four recovered; thirty-eight of the sixty-eight precisely provenanced pieces came from two adjacent one-metre squares (C10 and C11) and fifty from four square metres; a few large lumps and many small chips in every layer, meaning the big pieces were the sources and the small ones the debris; lump #6 carries deep intersecting grooves cut with the tip of a stone flake and shallower striations that postdate them, so it was worked in at least two separate sittings; a Levallois core from layer XIX so heavily stained inside one negative scar that the authors suggest it was recycled into a pigment cup; other tools stained only on the tips and lateral edges, exactly where scraping ochre would stain them. Everything here is excavated. Nobody has to be invented. Weaker on human pressure, stronger on candour. Hold in reserve, or use as the second movement.

### Alternate 2: the Qafzeh 8 cluster

In layer XVII, within less than a metre of one another in square B16: the burial of Qafzeh 8, the intensively scraped ochre lump #6, and an engraved cortical flake (Hovers, Vandermeersch and Bar-Yosef, *Rock Art Research* 14(2), 1997, 79-87 - metadata UNVERIFIED beyond a search listing, confirm before citing). Three kinds of not-food inside a square metre. The liability: the association is spatial, and the prose will strain toward "grave goods" when the paper only claims proximity.

### Scene to AVOID

**The Shanidar "flower burial."** It is the most beautiful image in Paleolithic archaeology and it has now been taken apart twice: Sommer (1999) proposed that Persian jirds, rodents attested in the cave sediments, dragged the flower heads in; Hunt, Pomeroy, Reynolds, Tilby and Barker (*Journal of Archaeological Science* 159, 2023, 105822) argue solitary burrowing bees, which still nest in the cave floor sand, are the better explanation for the pollen clumps. Opening on it would spend the reader's trust on something the field has retracted. Use it inside significance as an object lesson in how a picture outlives its evidence, never as theatre.

**Also avoid:** opening on Neanderthals. The node is about a Levantine practice that appears across Homo groups; leading with Neanderthals invites the "even they could do it" condescension the summary is trying to prevent.

## 3. Story movement

Six paragraphs. One place, one funeral, told mostly forward.

1. **The pit, being dug.** Open inside the physical labour: hard cave-terrace sediment, a stone flake, hands. The first sentence should be a person making a hole that will hold nothing useful.
2. **The child.** Move to the body. The scar in the forehead, healed, six or seven years old. Establish that this person was known, was difficult, was kept.
3. **The arrangement.** Body on its back, head turned right, arms folded, hands together near the face, oriented west. This is the beat where the reader realizes someone is *composing* a body. Slow down here; the specificity is the whole trick.
4. **The antlers.** Two of them, cut from a red deer, laid across the chest so they touch the palms. Do not explain them. The unexplainability is the point.
5. **The wider cave.** Widen to the terrace: hearths, the corner where ochre was ground, the tool with red on its tip, ten sea shells carried up from a coast thirty-five to forty kilometres away, holes in them worn by surf and by string. A place where people were doing several things that do not feed anyone.
6. **The pivot.** Close on the covering of the pit and the question underneath it. The reader should arrive at: *we can prove they did this on purpose, and we can prove nothing at all about why.* Do not answer it in the story.

**Governing image:** the pit. The only technology in the lesson that produces nothing. Every other invention in Era I returns calories, warmth, or a sharper edge; this one returns a hole. Let the image come back once at the close of significance, when the reader learns that the practice stops and the holes stop being dug.

**Words and framings to refuse.** No "mysterious", no "vanished", no "dawn of religion", no "the first spark of the soul", no "primitive". Do not write "even these early people" or "already". Do not call the antlers "the world's first grave goods" (Trap 6). Do not stage the Levant as a corridor, doorway, gateway, or stepping stone to Europe; it is a Mediterranean woodland where at least three kinds of human being overlapped, and it is the subject, not the hallway. Do not let the word "ritual" do work the evidence has not paid for; Hovers and colleagues are careful, and so should the lesson be. Avoid "Cro-Magnon" entirely, and avoid "Proto-Cro-Magnon", which is a live phrase in the older literature (Howell 1957) and encodes exactly the teleology the atlas rejects.

## 4. Significance spine

Five movements. Threads to tie in words: **ideas** (the node's own pigment, and the spine of the whole lesson) and **craft** (the ochre operational sequence: locating outcrops, selecting for red hue against cheaper alternatives, scraping with a flake tip, grinding, recycling a Levallois core into a pigment receptacle, and hauling shells thirty-five to forty kilometres inland). Do not reach for power or wealth; there is nothing here to hang them on, and forcing them would be the failure mode.

**1. How we know, and how thin it is.** Qafzeh's lower layers (XXIV to XVII) date by thermoluminescence and electron spin resonance to roughly 100,000 to 90,000 years ago, with an average TL date of 92,000 plus or minus 5,000 (Schwarcz et al. 1988; Valladas et al. 1988, both cited in Hovers et al. 2003). Skhul, on Mount Carmel, runs older and wider: the layer B deposit that produced ten human fossils is placed at roughly 100,000 to 135,000 years ago (Grün, Stringer, McDermott, Nathan, Porat, Robertson, Taylor, Mortimer, Eggins and McCulloch, *Journal of Human Evolution* 49(3), 2005, 316-334; and as reported in Vanhaeren et al. 2006 - the paper itself was not fetchable, see §7). Qafzeh produced sixteen individuals, seven adults and nine juveniles. Almost all the burials come from layer XVII; Hovers and colleagues count five there. Say the ranges. Say that TL and ESR are not calendars: they carry five-figure error bars, and at this depth "contemporary" can mean five thousand years apart.

**2. The mechanism: why there, why then, what it cost.** The Levantine Middle Palaeolithic is the one window where the evidence is dense enough to catch the practice. Qafzeh's lower layers hold a package that appears and disappears together: hearths, ochre, marine shells, and burials. Every element vanishes above layer XVII. The ochre itself is the best-documented decision. Sources sit close: veins of iron oxide 2.5 km west, Lower Cretaceous exposures at Mt Devora 4 km east, ferruginous oolitic sandstone at Mt Tavor about 8 km east, with more distant candidates at 'Ein el Assad (about 30 km), Wadi Malikh (about 40 km) and Ramim Ridge (about 60 km). The people did not take the nearest or the easiest. They walked past ubiquitous yellow goethite, which is softer, equally available and equally good for tanning or hafting, and repeatedly brought back red hematitic ochre from a restricted set of beds. Hovers and colleagues argue that cost-effectiveness was not the criterion and that the red hue was. That is the mechanism: a preference that costs something and returns nothing measurable is the signature of meaning. **Craft belongs here in words.** So do the shells: ten Glycymeris valves, carried from the Mediterranean shore thirty-five to forty kilometres off, of inedible species, several worn at the umbonal hole in a way consistent with hanging on a string, a few carrying red ochre stains (Bar-Yosef Mayer, Vandermeersch and Bar-Yosef, *Journal of Human Evolution* 56, 2009, 307-314).

**3. The named debate (use this one, and give it real space).** *Is Middle Palaeolithic burial intentional at all?* Robert H. Gargett argued no, or at least not proven: "Middle Palaeolithic burial is not a dead issue: the view from Qafzeh, Saint-Césaire, Kebara, Amud, and Dederiyeh", *Journal of Human Evolution* 37(1), 1999, 27-90, holding that most claimed burials are fragmentary, incomplete and disarticulated, and that natural processes explain them as well as intention does. Against him: Vandermeersch, Tillier, Belfer-Cohen and Hovers, who point to articulated skeletons in cut pits with arranged limbs. The argument is still running in Europe with better tools: William Rendu and thirteen colleagues, "Evidence supporting an intentional Neandertal burial at La Chapelle-aux-Saints", *PNAS* 111(1), 2014, 81-86, reported a twelve-year re-excavation showing an anthropogenic pit and taphonomic signs of rapid covering; Harold L. Dibble, Vera Aldeias, Paul Goldberg, Shannon P. McPherron, Dennis Sandgathe and Teresa E. Steele answered in *Journal of Archaeological Science* 53, 2015, 649-657, arguing the depression is not demonstrably dug and the case is unproven. **No verdict.** The honest position is that a few burials are as close to certain as this evidence gets, most are arguable, and the difference matters because it decides whether we are looking at a practice or at a handful of accidents. A second debate is available if a second is wanted: Hovers et al. read the Qafzeh ochre as symbolic, while Lyn Wadley, "Putting ochre to the test: replication studies of adhesives that may have been used for hafting tools in the Middle Stone Age", *Journal of Human Evolution* 49(5), 2005, 587-601, showed experimentally that ochre is a genuinely useful loading agent in hafting glue, which keeps a functional reading alive wherever ochre turns up near stone tools.

**4. The correction that reframes the node: Tinshemet, 2025.** Yossi Zaidner and thirty-two co-authors, "Evidence from Tinshemet Cave in Israel suggests behavioural uniformity across Homo groups in the Levantine mid-Middle Palaeolithic circa 130,000 to 80,000 years ago", *Nature Human Behaviour* 9(5), 2025, 886-901 (published 11 March 2025). Articulated Homo remains dated to about 100,000 years ago, in association with rich ochre, fauna and stone tools; more than 7,000 fragments of ochre nodules in the deposit; at least five individuals with at least two read as intentional burials; the authors report ochre used **in burial contexts**, which Qafzeh does not directly show. Their argument is that a single behavioural set - the same lithic technology, heavy reliance on large game, intentional burial, ochre - was shared across distinct Homo populations, and that the sharing is the product of intensified interaction and admixture. This is the anti-teleology payload of the whole node: burial is not a modern-human trait that Neanderthals imitated, and not a Neanderthal habit that we inherited. It looks like something a region did. John Hawks, reading the same paper, is sceptical of the specific claim about flexed-on-the-left body posture (he counts eight cases out of eleven once the omitted ones are added back, and notes mechanical reasons a body ends up flexed) while agreeing that burial itself is the real signal. Give the reader both.

**5. The uncertainty, and the landing.** Three honest ranges to print. (a) Dating: the whole Skhul-Qafzeh window is 135,000 to 90,000 years ago depending on site, method and specimen. (b) Meaning: nothing in the ground distinguishes grief from hygiene from fear of scavengers from a belief about what comes next. Even the minimalist reading Hovers quotes from Paul Mellars claims only "strong social or emotional bonds", not doctrine. (c) Descent: the Skhul and Qafzeh people are widely read as an early expansion out of Africa that left little or no genetic contribution to living people. Then land it here: **the practice stops.** Ochre use at Qafzeh ends after layer XVII. Hovers and colleagues state that Qafzeh is the only Levantine Middle Palaeolithic site that has yielded hematitic ochre at all, that there is no systematic pigment use in the Late Levantine Mousterian (80,000 to 45,000 years ago), only sporadic and largely utilitarian occurrences through the Upper and early Epi-Palaeolithic (45,000 to 12,500), and that quantities rise again only in the Natufian, around 12,700 to 10,500 years ago (uncalibrated). Seventy thousand years of near-silence between one people who ground red stone for their dead and another who did it again. That is the sentence the modern world still carries: every funeral held anywhere today is a link in a chain that has been broken, and re-forged, more than once. The ideas thread does not run continuously from this grave to any temple, church, or crematorium; the graph's own edge to `gods-of-the-city` says so. What runs continuously is the capacity, not the custom.

## 5. Factual traps

1. **Trap:** the antlers on Qafzeh 11's chest are fallow deer antlers (as the node's own hook says). **Truth:** the peer-reviewed 3D reappraisal, co-authored by Bernard Vandermeersch, who excavated the burial, captions them **red deer** antlers. Qafzeh's faunal assemblage contains both red deer and fallow deer in quantity, which is exactly why the confusion propagates; secondary web sources give *Dama dama*, *Dama mesopotamica* and *Cervus elaphus* interchangeably and none of them cite a faunal identification. **Source:** Coqueugniot, Dutour, Arensburg, Duday, Vandermeersch and Tillier, *PLOS ONE* 9(7): e102822, 2014, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0102822, Figure 5 caption. **Action:** write "red deer" or, safest, "deer". Do not write "fallow".

2. **Trap:** the Qafzeh dead were sprinkled or painted with red ochre. **Truth:** no ochre has been reported directly on the Qafzeh skeletons. The ochre-and-burial relationship is stratigraphic and spatial: both occur only in the lower layers, ochre clusters in a processing area a few metres away, and layer XVII has both the most burials and the heaviest ochre lumps. Hovers and colleagues call this an early, possibly the earliest, "prototype" of ochre-associated mortuary behaviour, which is a deliberately hedged phrase. The dark stains that *are* on the Qafzeh bones are natural dendritic manganese, not pigment. **Source:** Hovers et al. 2003, 491-522; Bar-Yosef Mayer et al. 2009, 307-314. **Action:** the title "Ochre for the Dead" is a licence the lesson must pay for by saying plainly that the ochre was near the graves, not on the bodies. Tinshemet (2025) is where ochre in burial contexts is directly reported; keep the two sites distinct.

3. **Trap:** the shells were pierced, and were strung as beads and buried with the dead (the node summary says "pierced shells"). **Truth:** the ten Glycymeris valves from Qafzeh are **naturally** perforated at the umbo by surf abrasion, and Bar-Yosef Mayer and colleagues state there is no indication they were directly associated with any of the burials; they come from levels below most of the skeletons. Several show wear consistent with a string running through the natural hole, and a few carry ochre stains, which is what makes them interesting. At Skhul the Nassarius gibbosulus perforations are likewise read as natural. **Source:** Bar-Yosef Mayer, Vandermeersch and Bar-Yosef, *JHE* 56, 2009, 307-314; Vanhaeren, d'Errico, Stringer, James, Todd and Mienis, "Middle Paleolithic Shell Beads in Israel and Algeria", *Science* 312(5781), 2006, 1785-1788. **Action:** write "shells with holes the sea had made" and let the human act be the carrying and the stringing, which is stronger anyway.

4. **Trap:** the ochre was hauled from far away, showing long-distance procurement. **Truth:** the opposite is the interesting finding. Candidate sources sit 2.5 to 8 km from the cave, and Hovers and colleagues explicitly conclude that the size and mass distributions are "consistent with short transport distances, which did not impose heavy costs". The choosiness, not the distance, is the evidence. The long carry belongs to the **shells**, thirty-five to forty kilometres from the Mediterranean (the paper's abstract says "some 35 km", its discussion says "about 40 km"; use "thirty-five to forty" or "roughly forty"). **Source:** Hovers et al. 2003; Bar-Yosef Mayer et al. 2009.

5. **Trap:** humans invented burial in Africa and carried it out with them. **Truth:** the oldest known human burial in Africa is far younger than the Levantine ones. Mtoto, a two and a half to three year old at Panga ya Saidi in coastal Kenya, was buried in a dug pit, flexed on the right side with knees to the chest and possibly a perishable head support, about 78,000 years ago (María Martinón-Torres, Francesco d'Errico, Elena Santos and forty others, "Earliest known human burial in Africa", *Nature* 593(7857), 2021, 95-100). Qafzeh is roughly 92,000. That is about fourteen thousand years earlier, in Asia. Preservation and excavation history explain some of the gap, and the lesson should say so, but the naive Africa-first ordering is simply not what the record shows. **Action:** this is the node's best anti-teleology beat. Use it.

6. **Trap:** the antlers are "the oldest grave goods in the world", and the Skhul boar mandible is a second confirmed case. **Truth:** the antlers are the only unambiguous grave good at Qafzeh (Hovers et al. call it "one instance"). Skhul 5 was found with a wild boar mandible between the left forearm and the ribcage, reported by the Mount Carmel excavators, but its intentionality is exactly what Gargett and others contest, and the excavation is a 1930s one. Superlatives here are fragile and turn over. **Source:** Hovers et al. 2003; Garrod and Bate 1937 / McCown and Keith 1939 as reported at second hand (UNVERIFIED: the original Skhul 5 field description was not fetched in this pass; do not quote it).

7. **Trap:** the Shanidar Neanderthal was buried on a bed of flowers. **Truth:** the pollen clumps under Shanidar 4 are now better explained by animals. Sommer (1999) argued for Persian jirds hoarding flower heads; Chris O. Hunt, Emma Pomeroy, Tim Reynolds, Emily Tilby and Graeme Barker, "Shanidar et ses fleurs? Reflections on the palynology of the Neanderthal 'Flower Burial' hypothesis", *Journal of Archaeological Science* 159, 2023, 105822, make the case for solitary bees that still nest in the cave floor. Note carefully: this dismantles the *flowers*, not the *burial*. Emma Pomeroy, Paul Bennett, Chris O. Hunt, Tim Reynolds, Lucy Farr, Marine Frouin, James Holman, Ross Lane, Charles French and Graeme Barker, "New Neanderthal remains associated with the 'flower burial' at Shanidar Cave", *Antiquity* 94(373), 2020, 11-26, report a new articulated individual, Shanidar Z, provisionally OSL-dated to between 70,000 and 60,000 years ago, whose stratigraphy suggests intentional burial in a cut feature.

8. **Trap:** Qafzeh 11 came from layer XXIII (or XXII, or XVII) - pick one and be confident. **Truth:** the published sources disagree. Coqueugniot et al. 2014 say layer XXIII, at the bottom of the Mousterian sequence. Bar-Yosef Mayer et al. 2009 say "the single burial of H. 11 that was found in layer XXII". Hovers et al. 2003 say layer XVII yielded five burials, the largest number, and that Qafzeh 13 was re-assigned to XVII in 1997. **Action:** do not print a layer number for Qafzeh 11. Say "near the bottom of the Mousterian sequence" and move on.

9. **Trap:** Qafzeh 9 was an adult woman, buried with a child. **Truth:** the double burial of Qafzeh 9 and Qafzeh 10 is real, but the adult's identity has been revised: Dany Coutinho-Nogueira, Hélène Coqueugniot and Anne-Marie Tillier, "Qafzeh 9 Early Modern Human from Southwest Asia: age at death and sex estimation re-assessed", *HOMO* 72(4), 2021, 293-305, report a young age at death, before full dental and skeletal maturity, and support a **male** sex assignment. **Action:** if the double burial is mentioned at all, say "a young adult and a child" and skip the sex.

10. **Trap:** burial proves belief in an afterlife, or the invention of religion. **Truth:** it proves that a body was treated as something other than refuse. Hovers and colleagues quote Paul Mellars's minimalist reading, that deliberate burial implies "some kind of strong social or emotional bonds" dictating that the remains of kin be protected, and even that is an inference. The graph's summary already gets this right; the prose must not undo it. There is no material test that separates grief, hygiene, scavenger avoidance and cosmology.

11. **Trap:** Homo naledi buried its dead 250,000 years ago, so burial is far older and not ours. **Truth:** the 2023 Rising Star claims went out as unreviewed preprints alongside a documentary; all four eLife reviewers found the evidence inadequate, citing missing data on whether the bones were placed rather than deposited by natural processes, and independent attempts to reproduce the sediment-mixing results found no support. A revised version has since been resubmitted and remains contested. **Action:** if you mention it at all, mention it as a live and unsettled claim, not a fact, and do not build a paragraph on it.

12. **Trap:** the Qafzeh people were modern humans and the Levant was the doorway through which our ancestors entered Eurasia. **Truth:** two problems. First, they made Mousterian tools essentially indistinguishable from those of European Neanderthals, which is why McCown and Keith originally named them *Palaeoanthropus palestinensis* and later writers called them "Proto-Cro-Magnons"; toolkits do not sort species here. Second, this population is widely read as an early expansion that left little or no genetic contribution to living non-Africans, and the Levant of this period held at least three kinds of human being, including the archaic-featured Nesher Ramla Homo dated to 140,000 to 120,000 years ago (Hershkovitz, May, Sarig and colleagues, *Science* 372(6549), 2021, 1424-1428, whose taxonomic reading has itself drawn a published Comment in *Science*). **Action:** frame the Levant as a crowded place in its own right, not as a corridor to somewhere better. Flag the "left no genetic contribution" claim as the field's majority reading rather than settled fact; it was confirmed here only from synthesis-level sources, not a genetics paper (UNVERIFIED at primary level).

13. **Trap:** ochre is obviously symbolic. **Truth:** ochre has real jobs. Wadley's replication studies (2005) show it works as a loading agent in hafting adhesive, and hide tanning, sun protection and insect repellence have all been proposed. Hovers and colleagues do not deny this; they argue against it *at Qafzeh specifically*, on the grounds that tanning and hafting do not care what colour the ochre is, while the Qafzeh assemblage is strongly and repeatedly skewed to red. That is a site-specific argument, not a general one. Do not generalize it.

14. **Trap:** the red ochre was made red by roasting yellow ochre in a hearth. **Truth:** Hovers and colleagues considered this and argued against it for Qafzeh: yellow goethite is almost absent from the assemblage, and goethite with the right sandy matrix would have come from the same Lower Cretaceous beds that already contained red hematitic ochre, so heating would have been pointless work. Francesco d'Errico's comment in the same issue notes that some goethite elsewhere may have been heated, and that turning yellow to red is a controlled procedure rather than roasting, since red goes black above about 450 degrees while a hardwood hearth reaches around 1,000. **Action:** if heating is mentioned, mention it as a considered and rejected alternative, not as the method.

15. **Trap:** Skhul and Qafzeh are the same site, the same people, the same moment. **Truth:** Skhul is a rock shelter on Mount Carmel excavated in the 1929 to 1934 Mount Carmel campaign; Qafzeh is a cave near Nazareth in the Lower Galilee, dug by Neuville and Stekelis in 1933 to 1934 and by Vandermeersch from 1965 to 1979. Skhul's layer B runs 135,000 to 100,000; Qafzeh's lower layers 100,000 to 90,000. They are neighbours in a chapter heading, not in a week. The straight-line distance between them is UNVERIFIED here; do not print a figure.

## 6. Callback design

**Hard constraint.** The gate demands 1 recall, 2 why, 2 callback. Only two earlier nodes exist, and **neither has a written lesson file.** Both callbacks must therefore be grounded strictly in the node summaries quoted in the packet. Do not invent detail from those lessons; ask questions the summaries alone can answer. Read no lesson files for these two; there are none.

**`first-of-our-kind`** (Jebel Irhoud, c. 300,000 BCE; summary: modern features accumulated in a mosaic across populations scattered over the whole continent, the face first and the braincase much later; Africa was the workshop, not a point on a map). *The genuine comparison:* that node dates the arrival of the **body**; this one dates the first arrival of a **decision** we recognise. Do the arithmetic on the page: roughly 315,000 minus about 92,000 is about 223,000 years, more than twice the span from this grave to the present. And the twist that makes it a comparison rather than a timeline: Jebel Irhoud says the anatomy came together piecemeal and did not switch on all at once, and the Levant says the same about behaviour. The people at Qafzeh made the same Mousterian tools their Neanderthal neighbours made, so neither the skeleton nor the toolkit sorts them cleanly, and neither does the burial. Mosaic in the bones, mosaic in the behaviour.

**`the-tended-fire`** (Qesem, c. 300,000 BCE; summary: a hearth with ash returned to the same spot for millennia; cooking makes food safer and easier to digest; the hearth became a social centre that stretched the day past sundown). *The genuine comparison:* both nodes are about a **place made permanent by repetition**, and they split on payoff. The hearth's return is measurable in calories and hours of usable daylight; the grave's return is nothing you can count. The comparison is sharpened by a real finding rather than a rhetorical parallel: at Qafzeh, hearths, ochre, shells and burials occur together in the lower layers and all disappear together above layer XVII, which is why Hovers and colleagues treat them as one behavioural package. Fire and burial are not just two nice ideas side by side; at this site they enter and leave the record hand in hand. That is a comparison with teeth.

**Recommended pair:** both, since both are required and both are strong. Sequence them `first-of-our-kind` first (body versus decision, the long arithmetic) then `the-tended-fire` (two kinds of permanence, one of which pays). That order also lets the five questions run as one arc: recall the antlers, then why-red-ochre, then why-does-burial-count-as-evidence, then the two callbacks widening outward.

**Alternates, ranked, if one of the above proves unusable:** there are none. Do not reach forward to `spark-of-mind` or `paint-in-the-dark`; both are later in the main sequence and the gate will reject them. If a callback genuinely cannot be built, the forge spec permits 1 callback plus 3 why for very early nodes, but the packet's `questionMix` demands 2 callbacks, so treat that escape hatch as closed unless the gate says otherwise.

**Suggested `threadsOut`:** mirror all three graph edges (`tribe-of-strangers`, `paint-in-the-dark`, `gods-of-the-city`) with fresh why-sentences, and consider adding `spark-of-mind` as a fourth. The graph does not draw that wire, but the lesson earns it: Blombos at about 100,000 years ago is contemporary with Qafzeh's lower layers, and the two sites are the same argument in two materials, a continent apart. If you add it, make the why-sentence carry the contemporaneity explicitly so the reader does not read the sequence as Africa-then-Asia.

## 8. Node-data flags

Precise problems in the committed graph. Do not edit `data/eras/01-long-dawn.js`; report them.

1. **The hook says "fallow deer antlers".** The 2014 reappraisal, co-authored by the burial's excavator, captions them red deer antlers, and no fetched source supports *Dama*. Recommend "deer antlers" (safe) or "red deer antlers" (supported). This is the single clearest factual defect in the node.

2. **The summary says burials came "sometimes with ochre, animal remains, or pierced shells".** Two problems in one clause. (a) *Pierced*: the Qafzeh Glycymeris and the Skhul Nassarius are naturally perforated; nobody drilled them. (b) *With burials*: Bar-Yosef Mayer and colleagues state explicitly that the Qafzeh shells show no association with any burial and come from levels below most of the skeletons, and no ochre has been reported on the Qafzeh bones. Recommend rewording to something like "sometimes with animal remains, in layers that also held worked ochre and sea shells carried in from the coast", which is both true and no less vivid. The Tinshemet 2025 report does support ochre in burial contexts, so if Codex wants the ochre-in-grave claim it should be sourced to Tinshemet, not Qafzeh.

3. **"More than 90,000 years ago" undersells the range and mismatches the date field.** The node's date field reads `c. 120,000 to 90,000 BCE`, but Skhul's layer B runs to about 135,000 years ago, older than the field's upper bound. The honest window for the node's combined subject is roughly 135,000 to 90,000 years ago. Minor, but the lesson will otherwise inherit an inconsistency it has to paper over.

4. **BCE at this depth is awkward.** Every source in this field counts in years before present. At 92,000 the difference is negligible arithmetically but real stylistically, and the reader is better served by "about ninety-two thousand years ago" than by "90,000 BCE". Recommend the map label stays as is and the prose uses "years ago" throughout, with one sentence early on that reconciles them.

5. **Threads: `ideas` only.** Defensible, and the node is the era's designated ideas carrier. But the evidence base is heavily a craft record: source selection against cost, two-stage scraping, grinding, a Levallois core recycled as a pigment cup, stained tool tips, and forty kilometres of shell transport. Codex decision whether to add `craft`. If the thread stays single, the lesson should still name craft in words, per the forge spec's two-thread rule.

6. **Missing wire to `spark-of-mind`, which is claiming this node's territory.** `spark-of-mind`'s committed summary lists "burying the dead with care" among its own achievements, and its lesson text says symbolic artefacts are the best evidence for language. That node sits at index 3, immediately after this one, and Blombos (about 100,000 years ago) is contemporary with Qafzeh's lower layers rather than later. There is no edge in either direction. Two risks: reader whiplash (two adjacent nodes both claiming the first symbolic behaviour) and an implied Africa-then-Asia sequence the dates do not support. Recommend Codex either add an `echoes` edge between them or trim the burial clause from `spark-of-mind`'s summary. Not a defect in this lesson, but the lesson must be written knowing it.

7. **The `gods-of-the-city` edge label is exemplary and should be protected.** "no direct institutional lineage connects them to Paleolithic graves" is exactly the guard rail this node needs. Whoever reviews the lesson should check that the prose honours it and does not quietly build the ladder the edge forbids.
