# Lesson brief: wolf-at-the-fire

Research and architecture scaffolding for Fable. Not draft prose. Every claim below carries its evidentiary status. Where this brief and a source disagree, follow the source and say so in the completion report. Two things about this node changed in March 2026 and the brief is built around them: the genetic record of domestic dogs was pushed back by two companion papers in *Nature*, and a canid that morphologists had read as a small dog turned out, under DNA, to be a wolf.

*Second research pass, 29 July 2026.* Both 2026 *Nature* abstracts were re-read verbatim at Crossref and one claim in the first pass was found overstated: Pınarbaşı is not described by its own authors as the oldest confirmed dog (see the correction under Alternate 1 in section 2, and Trap 17). Five verified additions were made, each aimed at the same weakness, which is that the first pass left the lesson standing almost entirely on European ground: Shubayqa 6 in Jordan with its digested-bone sequence, Jōmon Japan, the Erralla claim from the Basque Country, the dingo's late arrival in Australia, and the post-contact erasure of American dogs. Traps 15 to 19 are new, Trap 7 now names Serpell 2021 as the open-access anchor for the domestication debate, and Trap 14 now carries a hard date for Neanderthal disappearance instead of a rounded one. The `deeper` recommendation has been revised. Where a claim below says a paper was read in full, it was; where it says metadata or abstract only, treat it as such.

## 1. Graph target

- Node id: `wolf-at-the-fire`. Title: The Wolf at the Fire. Era 1 (The Long Dawn), date field `c. 20,000 to 14,000 BCE`, region `world`, globalIndex 15. Threads: **craft**, **wealth**.
- Question mix the gate will demand: 1 recall, 2 why, 2 callback. (Not the "1 callback + 3 why" early-node allowance; five written predecessors exist.)
- Outgoing edges (three), and what each demands this lesson set up:
  1. `into-the-last-continents` ("Domesticated dogs accompanied later human migrations into the Americas, the only tamed animal known to have arrived alongside people."). **Demands:** that the lesson establish dogs as portable across a continental frontier, and that it do the arithmetic honestly, because that node's own headline is the White Sands footprints at roughly 23,000 to 21,000 years ago and the best current estimate has dogs entering the Americas only from about 15,000 years ago. The lesson must set up "later," not "first."
  2. `trap-of-seeds` ("Taming the wolf rehearsed the domestication of plants and herds that settled farming would soon demand."). **Demands:** the lesson must name what domestication *is* as a process, so farming reads as the same move applied to grasses and herds. It must also mark the difference: this one happened without storage, without sedentism, without a stable climate, and thousands of years earlier.
  3. `horse-and-wheel` ("The partnership that began with the wolf reached a further pitch when humans later bred and harnessed the horse."). **Demands:** the lesson establish selective breeding for *task* (the Zhokhov sled and hunting dogs are the earliest hard evidence) so that trained horse teams read as a later chapter of the same craft, not a fresh invention.
- Incoming edges: **none**. The node is a source. Nothing later depends on it having been read, so all backward reach has to come from `threadsOut` and the two callback questions.

**This node's job in the atlas.** Era I is a run of nodes about what our species did to itself: minds, symbols, boats, needles, paint, an economy of enforced sharing. This is the first node where the subject is what our species did to something else. It is the atlas's only pre-agricultural domestication and the only one carried out by mobile foragers inside an unstable Ice Age climate, which makes it the exception that sets up the rule `trap-of-seeds` will state. It also opens the biology-and-companionship strand the graph otherwise never touches. Structurally it is a fork: forward into the Americas, forward into farming, forward into the horse. Written well, it is the moment the atlas stops being a story about humans alone.

## 2. Preferred scene

**A Rhineland winter, roughly 14,200 years ago (about 12,270 BCE): six weeks of nursing a puppy through distemper, ending at the grave.**

Not the burial. The *nursing*. The burial is the last beat.

The documented object is the Bonn-Oberkassel grave, found on 18 February 1914 by workers in Peter Uhrmacher's basalt quarry at Oberkassel, now a suburb of Bonn, and reanalysed by Luc Janssens and colleagues in the *Journal of Archaeological Science* in 2018. What that paper attests: a double human burial with dog remains, all under flat basalt blocks about 20 cm thick, in an area roughly 3 m across, the whole thing liberally dusted with red hematite powder that is foreign to the site and found nowhere else in the quarry. The dog was a late juvenile of about 27 to 28 weeks. Its teeth carry a horizontal enamel hypoplasia line laid down at the 19-week developmental stage, plus two further lines on the canine at weeks 21 and 23, together with severe periodontal disease and an atypical abrasion on the caudal aspect of the mandibular canine. Canine distemper (a morbillivirus) runs a roughly three-week course; 80 to 90 percent of dogs die in the second week. Three bouts between 19 and 23 weeks, survived. The authors' conclusion, in their own frame: survival "without intensive human assistance would have been unlikely," and the care required would have consisted of keeping the dog warm and clean of diarrhoea, urine, vomit and saliva, and certainly giving water and possibly food. During the illness "the puppy was clearly too sick to be of any practical use to humans."

Second documented object for the same hand: **the other tooth.** Re-examination of the assemblage found a right upper molar belonging to a second, older and smaller dog, which the authors add to the list of grave goods. It is the only trace of an animal that died before the puppy. This makes Bonn-Oberkassel the only known dog burial containing two dogs.

### Documentation ledger

- **Attested (Janssens et al. 2018 unless noted):** the 1914 quarry discovery and the damage extraction did to the grave; the date, 14,223 ± 58 cal BP, with the paper's own calibrated weighted mean at 12,290 to 12,050 cal BC (2σ); four radiocarbon dates on the dog and two on the woman, statistically indistinguishable, hence one burial event; two humans, a man of about 40 and a parous woman of about 25, genetically close relatives (Mittnik and Krause 2015, cited within); both with significant oral-cavity disease; the man with a healed oblique distal ulnar fracture; the dog at about 27 to 28 weeks with full permanent dentition and open growth plates; enamel hypoplasia at 19, 21 and 23 weeks; severe periodontal disease; the second dog's upper molar; grave goods of a bone pin, a flat elk-antler sculpture of a large ungulate head (most likely an elk), a modified bear baculum, and a modified red deer incisor; the basalt slabs and hematite powder; mtDNA placing the dog in the C clade; and the environmental setting, the early part of the Meiendorf Interstadial (GI-1e), with the mammoth steppe giving way to temperate woodland.
- **Estimated, not measured:** shoulder height about 0.45 m and mass about 15 kg. Both are derived from a single measurement, the smallest humeral diameter of 11.7 mm, run through published conversion formulae. Write these as estimates or omit the numbers.
- **Reconstruction (the writer must own it in `storyContext`):** every name; who did the nursing; the season; the weather; whether the humans understood what they were doing; the dog's colour, coat, behaviour; the emotional content. Also, explicitly, **how the dog died.** The paper says flatly that we cannot know whether it was killed to accompany the humans, died of its earlier illnesses, or died otherwise at the same time. Do not resolve that.
- **Real named person available:** **no.** There is no writing, no name, nothing. Everything human in the scene is composite. `storyContext` must say so, in the manner of `spark-of-mind` ("a composite, assembled from...").
- **Quantities to check before printing:** the date (see Trap 2, the BP-versus-BCE problem, which bites this node harder than any other in Era I); the human ages (see Trap 4, sources conflict); the 15 kg and 0.45 m (estimates); any claim about how many Magdalenian dogs are known (the paper says nine including the second Bonn-Oberkassel animal, eight described over the previous century).

**Why this scene.** It dramatises the node's actual mechanism rather than the mechanism people assume. The assumed mechanism is utility: wolves earned their place by working. This grave is the counter-case, and it is the best-documented human-dog interaction in the Palaeolithic record. Somebody spent six weeks on an animal that could not hunt, could not guard, could not pull, and was mostly leaking. The atlas's craft thread and wealth thread both run through it, and the story ends on a question the significance section then answers.

### Alternate 1 (currently the oldest, but thinly documented in open sources)

**Pınarbaşı, central Anatolia, about 15,800 years ago.** Reported by Marsh, Scarsbrook, Yüncü and colleagues in *Nature* on 25 March 2026. Douglas Baird (University of Liverpool), who excavated, is quoted saying the puppies "like humans, were carefully buried in the rock shelter near human burial, thereby receiving ritualized treatment." **Liability:** the paper is paywalled and I could not open it; the burial arrangement, the number of puppies, and the excavation context are all UNVERIFIED at primary level, coming from institutional press coverage only. Use Pınarbaşı as a fact in significance, not as a scene, unless the writer can open the paper.

**Correction to the phrasing everyone will reach for, verified against the abstract in this pass.** Marsh et al. do *not* say Pınarbaşı is the oldest confirmed dog. Their abstract calls it "canid remains at Pınarbaşı in Türkiye (15,800 years ago)" and lands its actual claim one step later: "a genetically homogeneous dog population was already widely distributed across Europe and Anatolia during the Late Upper Palaeolithic (by at least 14,300 years ago)." The companion paper is more explicit still: Bergström et al. write that "the oldest dog data that we recovered are from a 14,200-year-old dog from the Kesslerloch site in Switzerland." So the defensible sentence is **dogs genetically confirmed and already spread from Britain to Anatolia by at least 14,300 years ago**, with Pınarbaşı at 15,800 as the oldest canid the pipeline reached, not a certified dog. Popular coverage has collapsed the two. Do not follow it. (Marsh et al. also give the previous state of play, which is worth a clause of its own: before these papers "the earliest unequivocal genetic evidence" for dogs came from Mesolithic contexts about 10,900 years ago, so the record moved back roughly three and a half thousand years in one morning.)

### Alternate 2 (safest, no invented people at all)

**Open inside the 2026 identification.** A canid from Belgium, about 13,700 years old, small, bearing human modification, read for years as a probable dog, goes into a hybridisation-capture pipeline and comes back a wolf. Nothing in the scene is invented and the lesson never puts words in a Palaeolithic mouth. It also opens on the honest epistemological centre of the whole node: for a century we have been sorting dogs from wolves by the shape of their skulls, and we were wrong often enough to matter. **Liability:** cold, and it spends the emotional charge of the subject on method. Hold in reserve. (Note: I could not confirm at primary level which Belgian site this specimen comes from; a secondary summary names Goyet, which risks confusion with the famous 36,000-year-old Goyet skull. Do not name the site without checking.)

### Scene to AVOID

**The bold wolf at the edge of the firelight, tossed a bone.** Three reasons. First, it is the single most-told version of this story and the reader has already seen it. Second, it is undocumented: there is no site, no layer, no bone that shows it. Third, and worst, it silently commits the lesson to Coppinger's dump-scavenging model, which requires middens, which require sedentism, which Late Pleistocene mobile foragers largely did not have (see Trap 7). The node's own hook already recites this scene, which is exactly why the lesson should not.

Also avoid, as a cold open, **the Chauvet "boy and his dog" footprints.** See Trap 10. The atlas has already used Chauvet for `paint-in-the-dark`, and that lesson correctly hedges the tracks as "a wolf or a large dog."

## 3. Story movement

Six or seven paragraphs. One camp, one animal, one season, and a grave at the end.

1. **The sick puppy, close up and physical.** Nineteen weeks old, second week of the disease, the week that kills four dogs in five. Fever, the discharge, the mess. A person cleaning it. Concrete and unsentimental; the labour is the point and the labour is disgusting. Do not narrate feelings yet.
2. **Why it is absurd.** Widen just enough to show what else that person could be doing in a Rhineland winter at the tail of the Ice Age. The animal is a net loss. Nobody in the camp can say what it is for.
3. **Relapse, and relapse again.** Weeks 21 and 23. Two more lines in the enamel. The care does not stop. This is where the reader should start to feel that something has changed in what a human being is willing to spend effort on.
4. **The older dog.** Introduce the other tooth: this puppy is not the first. There was an animal before it, smaller and older, of which one molar survives. The relationship is already inherited. Let the reader feel that we have walked in halfway through.
5. **What the dog was good for, honestly.** Sentry, tracker, hunting partner, warmth, waste disposal, an ear that works while you sleep. Give the utilitarian case its full weight here, because the story has just spent three paragraphs undermining it. Do not resolve the tension.
6. **The grave.** The 20 cm basalt slabs, the red hematite dusted over everything, the bone pin, the elk-antler carving, the bear baculum, the red deer incisor, the older dog's tooth. Two people and an animal in one hole, dated to the same moment. Do not say why. Say what.
7. **The pivot.** End on the fact that we cannot tell, from these bones, whether the dog was killed to go with them or simply died. That unresolvable gap is the hinge into significance: whatever we were doing by this point, it was no longer a transaction.

**Governing image: the tooth.** One molar is all that is left of the animal that came before, and the entire science of dog origins is exactly this, an enormous inference resting on a handful of teeth and, since 2026, on the DNA inside them. Let the image return once in the middle (the older dog) and once at the close of significance (the 2026 papers reading ancestry out of teeth from Anatolia to Somerset).

**Words and framings to avoid.** "Mysterious." "Vanished." "Man's best friend" (unearned and modern). "Wolves chose us" (a slogan, not a finding). Any sentence in which the Ice Age is "primitive." Any sentence that makes domestication a European event: the genetic evidence points east, and the oldest confirmed dog is Anatolian. Do not write "the first pet" as a settled claim; it is Janssens et al.'s hypothesis and should be attributed. Do not write that the dog "hunted with" these people (the hook does; see Trap 3).

## 4. Significance spine

Five movements. Threads the lesson must tie to **in words: craft and wealth.** Craft, because domestication is the first technology whose material is alive and whose product is a lineage rather than an object; the technique is not knapping but selection, and it is transmitted the same way a paint recipe is. Wealth, because the dog is the first thing a forager can hold that reproduces itself, follows one person rather than the band, and can be given away entire; this is where the atlas should say plainly that we do not know whether Palaeolithic foragers experienced dogs as property, and that Losey's Baikal work suggests some were treated as persons instead. A third thread, ideas, may flicker at the close.

1. **How we know, and how recently it changed.** The morphological era: for a century, dogs were sorted from wolves by skull shape, snout length, palate width, tooth crowding. The genetic era arrived in force on 25 March 2026, when two companion papers in *Nature* (Bergström et al., 651:986-994; Marsh, Scarsbrook, Yüncü et al., 651:995-1003) applied a genome-wide capture approach, enriching endogenous DNA tenfold to a hundredfold, to 216 canid remains, 181 of them from Palaeolithic and Mesolithic Europe, and could distinguish dog from wolf ancestry for 141 of them. Before those papers, the earliest unequivocal genetic evidence for dogs came from Mesolithic contexts about 10,900 years ago. After them, Bergström's oldest confirmed dog is Kesslerloch in Switzerland at 14,200 years, Marsh's material adds Gough's Cave in Britain at 14,300 and canid remains from Pınarbaşı in Türkiye at 15,800, and the joint claim is that a **genetically homogeneous dog population was already spread from Britain to Anatolia by at least 14,300 years ago**. Write that sentence and not the shorter one; see the correction under Alternate 1 in section 2. Two further findings from the same morning are worth the reader's time. Bergström's Kesslerloch dog already shares ancestry with later worldwide dogs, which is inconsistent with the idea that European Upper Palaeolithic dogs came from a wholly separate domestication; and it already resembles later European dogs more than Asian ones, so dog diversification had begun well before 14,200 years ago. Marsh's inference is the more startling one for this atlas: a single homogeneous dog population shared across Magdalenian, Epigravettian and Anatolian hunter-gatherers, culturally and genetically distinct human groups, implies **dogs were being exchanged between peoples**, which makes the dog an early item of long-distance traffic and is the cleanest possible earning of this node's `wealth` pigment. Say plainly that this is a moving field and that the lesson is written in mid-2026. Note also, for candour, one claim the new work does not corroborate: an almost complete humerus from Erralla cave in the Basque Country, directly dated to 17,410 to 17,096 cal BP, was published in 2022 as a domestic dog on morphological and mitochondrial grounds, and its own authors phrased it cautiously as "one of the earliest domesticated dogs in Europe." It is older than anything the 2026 nuclear work confirms and it sits inside this node's committed date range, which the confirmed dogs do not. Mention it as an outstanding claim, not as a fact.

2. **Mechanism: why there, why then, what it caused.** Why then: dogs are the only animal domesticated by mobile hunter-gatherers, before farming, before pottery, before villages, and inside the volatile Pleistocene rather than the calm Holocene. That is the fact `world-the-ice-made` and `trap-of-seeds` need. Why it was even possible: Lahtinen, Clinnick, Mannermaa, Salonen and Viranta (*Scientific Reports* 11:7, 2021) point out that humans cannot draw much more than about 20 percent of energy from protein without harm, while wolves thrive on lean meat for months. In deep winter, when game is lean and fat is scarce, a hunting band has surplus protein it physically cannot use and a competitor that can. Feeding the competitor costs nothing. Note that all Pleistocene sites with dog or incipient-dog remains lie in subarctic or arctic-analogue environments, which is the observation their model is built to explain. Present this as one live hypothesis, not the answer. What it caused: a division of sensory labour. Dogs hear and smell at night; humans plan and throw. Karen Lupo (*Journal of Anthropological Archaeology* 47, 2017, 139-151) is the necessary corrective here, showing from the ethnographic record that hunting-productivity gains from dogs are highly variable and largest when the dog is a *novel* predator the prey has not learned to counter. Dogs are not automatically useful; they are useful in particular ecologies. Two cases make that concrete, and neither is European, which matters for a node whose evidence base otherwise skews hard toward the Rhine and the Ardèche.

*Japan.* Angela Perri's study of Jōmon Japan (*Antiquity* 90(353), 2016, 1166-1180) is the cleanest positive case anywhere: dog burials there cluster tightly with the hunting of forest ungulates, sika deer and wild boar, in exactly the closed, broken terrain where an animal that tracks and holds wounded game earns its food. Dogs as environmental adaptation, not as universal upgrade. (Secondary summaries give the burial count as "more than 110"; verify against the paper or write "more than a hundred" unattributed.)

*Jordan.* At Shubayqa 6, in the Harrat al-Sham or Black Desert of northeast Jordan, Lisa Yeomans, Louise Martin and Tobias Richter (*Journal of Anthropological Archaeology* 53, 2019, 161-173) found dogs arriving alongside a shift toward *smaller* prey, hare and fox, and argued that new hunting methods, not resource depletion, may account for part of what is usually called the broad-spectrum revolution. Their proxy is the best single number in this brief because the dogs wrote it themselves: the proportion of small ungulate bone bearing carnivore digestion marks rises from 0.8, 0.5 and 7.5 percent in the Natufian levels at nearby Shubayqa 1 to 22.3 and 22.8 percent in the Early and Late Pre-Pottery Neolithic A at Shubayqa 6. Roughly a fifth of the small bones on the site had been through a dog. Animals living inside the settlement, eating the rubbish, and leaving their record in the rubbish they did not finish. If the lesson wants one image of what daily co-residence actually looked like, this is it, and it is a thousand miles from any campfire in the Ardèche.

By about 9,000 years ago at Zhokhov Island in the Siberian Arctic, dogs of two distinct size classes appear alongside sled parts, which Pitulko and Kasparov read as deliberate breeding for task. That is the craft thread arriving at specification, and it is the earliest evidence anywhere in the archaeological record for deliberately breeding an animal, thousands of years before any comparable case for another species. (**UNVERIFIED at the level of the numbers:** the specific withers heights of roughly 60 to 70 cm and the malamute/husky comparison come from secondary summaries only. Confirm or drop the measurements; the two-size-classes claim and the sledge parts are safe.)

3. **The named live debate (use this one): are there Palaeolithic dogs before the Magdalenian?** Mietje Germonpré (Royal Belgian Institute of Natural Sciences) and colleagues have argued since 2009 that large canid skulls from Goyet in Belgium and Eliseevichi in Russia, and later from Předmostí in the Czech Republic, are "palaeodogs," short-snouted and wide-palated, pushing domestication far back into the Upper Palaeolithic. Against them: Abby Grace Drake, Michael Coquerelle and Guillaume Colombeau (*Scientific Reports* 5:8299, 2015) ran 3D geometric morphometrics and put Goyet and Eliseevichi squarely inside wolf morphospace, lacking the cranial flexion that defines dog skulls; Darcy Morey argued the same case in 2014 and Germonpré's group replied in 2015. In March 2026 the DNA arrived and reclassified a small, human-modified Belgian canid of about 13,700 years as a wolf. **No verdict.** Germonpré's position is not simply refuted: morphology could be registering a real process, incipient domestication in populations whose lineages left no descendants and therefore no signal in an ancestry analysis of later dogs. The honest statement is that a dog-shaped skull is no longer sufficient evidence that an animal was a dog, and that this changes what can be claimed, not what happened. **Secondary debates if a second is wanted:** Perri, Feuerborn, Frantz, Larson, Malhi, Meltzer and Witt (*PNAS* 118(6), 2021) argue domestication in Siberia by about 23,000 years ago (95% CI 26,000 to 19,700), against Bergström et al.'s 2022 finding (*Nature* 607:313-320) that dogs derive mainly from an eastern Eurasian wolf source but that Near Eastern and African dogs draw 20 to 60 percent from a distinct western Eurasian wolf-related population, which is either a second domestication or later admixture, and that **none** of 72 ancient wolf genomes is a direct match for either ancestry. Also live: whether the "domestication syndrome" exists at all (movement 5).

4. **Honest uncertainty ranges, with the arithmetic on the page.** Genetic estimates for domestication cluster in the range roughly 23,000 to 15,000 years ago and the field does not agree on a figure or a place. The oldest bones with confirmed dog DNA are about 15,800 years old. The oldest dog buried with humans is about 14,200 years old. The gap between the genetic inference and the physical evidence is thousands of years wide and the lesson should say so rather than smooth it. Put these subtractions in the prose where they land: from the Bonn-Oberkassel burial (about 14,200 years ago) to the first farming villages of `trap-of-seeds` (about 9500 BCE, that is about 11,450 years ago) is roughly **2,750 years**; take Perri et al.'s 23,000-year estimate instead and the dog precedes agriculture by roughly **11,550 years**. From the burial to the Sintashta chariot teams of `horse-and-wheel` (about 2000 BCE) is roughly **10,200 years**. And for the `into-the-last-continents` edge: the White Sands footprints are about 23,000 to 21,000 years old, dogs are inferred into the Americas from about 15,000 years ago, and the oldest directly dated dogs in the hemisphere are the Koster and Stilwell II burials in Illinois at 10,190 to 9,630 cal BP (Perri et al., *American Antiquity* 84(1), 2019, 68-87). If both the footprints and the dog date hold, the first people in the Americas arrived **without dogs, by six to eight thousand years.**

5. **What the modern world still carries, and the trap in that sentence.** Two threads to close on, and they should be braided rather than sequential. (a) The relationship left marks in both species' biology. Nagasawa and colleagues (*Science* 348:333-336, 2015) showed a mutual oxytocin rise in dogs and owners during eye contact, a loop otherwise seen between human mothers and infants. Salomons and colleagues (*Current Biology* 31(14), 2021) found that dog puppies read human gestures and seek human eye contact far more than wolf puppies raised with more human exposure, on the same non-social cognitive baseline; Hansen Wheat and colleagues have contested the rearing controls and the authors have replied, so present it as a live result. (b) The domestication-syndrome story that everyone knows is in trouble. Belyaev's Russian farm-fox experiment has been taught for sixty years as proof that selecting for tameness alone produces floppy ears, curly tails and piebald coats; Kathryn Lord, Greger Larson, Raymond Coppinger and Elinor Karlsson (*Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 35(2), 2020, 125-136) showed the foxes came from eastern Canadian fur farms already carrying most of those traits, and that the syndrome is not unambiguously supported across species. There is a published Comment defending the concept. The argument is open. Land the lesson on the wealth thread and the honest scale: on the standard estimates there are roughly a billion dogs alive, and the large majority of them are not pets but free-ranging village dogs, self-feeding, self-breeding, living beside people rather than with them (Raymond and Lorna Coppinger, *What Is a Dog?*, University of Chicago Press, 2016). The household dog on a sofa is a recent and minority arrangement. What is not recent is the arrangement itself: one species that has spent fifteen thousand years or more inside the edge of another's camp, and a technique, selection, that we would eventually turn on wheat, cattle, horses, and ourselves.

(c) **The anti-teleology beat, and it should be the last one.** The easy close is that dogs went wherever we went, and it is false in both directions. Australia is the counterexample and it is a large one: people were on the continent by at least 50,000 years ago and possibly 65,000, and the oldest firmly dated dingo remains, from Madura Cave on the Nullarbor Plain, are 3,450 ± 95 BP, with the authors' modelled arrival between about 3,348 and 3,081 years ago (Balme, O'Connor and Fallon, *Scientific Reports* 8, 2018). Tens of thousands of years of human life on a continent with no dog in it. And the partnership can be unmade: the pre-contact dogs of the Americas, which had travelled with people for at least nine thousand years, were almost entirely replaced after European arrival, and Ní Leathlobhair and colleagues (*Science* 361(6397), 2018, 81-85) report that the closest detectable surviving relative of that whole lineage is the canine transmissible venereal tumour, a contagious cancer descended from a single dog. An entire population of animals survives mainly as a disease. Meanwhile the making goes on: the most recent large study (Zhang, Scarsbrook, Li et al., *Science* 390(6774), 2025, 735-740) reads 73 ancient dog genomes across nearly ten thousand years of eastern Eurasia and finds dog ancestry shifting in step with dispersals of hunter-gatherers, farmers and pastoralists, with dogs traded between populations wherever a working trait was worth having. Fifteen thousand years in, this is still not a settled arrangement. It is a relationship that has been negotiated, broken and renegotiated on every continent, and the negotiating is what the reader should carry out of the lesson, not the sofa.

## 5. Factual traps

The single highest-value section. Each is a confident-sounding thing a writer will otherwise reach for.

1. **Trap:** Bonn-Oberkassel is the oldest known dog.
   **Truth:** it is the oldest known dog *buried with humans*, and among a small set of genetically confirmed Late Palaeolithic dogs. As of 25 March 2026 the oldest genetically confirmed domestic dog is from Pınarbaşı, Türkiye, about 15,800 years ago; Gough's Cave (England) is about 14,300; Kesslerloch (Switzerland) about 14,200. Janssens et al. themselves claim only "the oldest known [domestic dog burial]" and "some of the oldest undisputed evidence for domestic dogs."
   **Source:** Marsh, Scarsbrook, Yüncü et al., "Dogs were widely distributed across western Eurasia during the Palaeolithic," *Nature* 651(8107), 2026, 995-1003, DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10170-x (metadata verified at Crossref); Bergström et al., "Genomic history of early dogs in Europe," *Nature* 651(8107), 2026, 986-994, DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10112-7 (metadata verified at Crossref); Janssens et al. 2018 (full text read).

2. **Trap:** treating "14,000 years ago" and "14,000 BCE" as the same thing. This node is the worst offender in Era I.
   **Truth:** the Bonn-Oberkassel burial is 14,223 ± 58 **cal BP**, which is about **12,270 BCE**; the paper's own calibrated weighted mean is 12,290 to 12,050 cal BC (2σ). The node's date field reads `c. 20,000 to 14,000 BCE`, and 14,000 BCE is 15,950 years ago, so on a strict reading **the node's headline evidence falls outside the node's own date range.** Pınarbaşı at about 15,800 years ago is about 13,775 BCE, also outside. The graph packet already flags the era's BP/BCE convention; this node is where it becomes visible. Fable should keep every date in the lesson in one convention, state it once, and prefer "years ago" throughout rather than converting.
   **Source:** Janssens, Giemsch, Schmitz, Street, Van Dongen and Crombé, "A new look at an old dog: Bonn-Oberkassel reconsidered," *Journal of Archaeological Science* 92, 2018, 126-138, DOI 10.1016/j.jas.2018.01.004, section 2.1 and abstract.

3. **Trap:** the Bonn-Oberkassel dog was a hunting partner, as the node hook says ("buried in the same grave as the people it hunted with").
   **Truth:** the animal died at about 27 to 28 weeks after six weeks or more of grave illness between weeks 19 and 23. The paper states it "likely was untrained as a result, and thus had no obvious utilitarian value," and that during the illness there "was no materialistic benefit" and the benefit ran the other way, humans being useful to the dog. Nothing in the assemblage shows this dog hunting. The hook is not supported by the paper it rests on. Report; do not repeat.
   **Source:** Janssens et al. 2018, discussion and conclusion.

4. **Trap:** printing the wrong ages for the two humans, or the wrong date range for the grave.
   **Truth:** sources conflict. Janssens et al. 2018 give a man of about 40 and a parous woman of about 25, and 14,223 ± 58 cal BP. The LVR-Landesmuseum's own outreach material and the German Wikipedia article give "approximately a 50-year-old man" and a 20 to 25-year-old woman, with a range of 13,300 to 14,000 years across different radiocarbon determinations. Use the 2018 peer-reviewed figures, write "about," and do not let a museum caption override the paper.
   **Source:** Janssens et al. 2018 (read in full) against LVR-LandesMuseum Bonn, Projekt Oberkassel (fetched) and de.wikipedia "Doppelgrab von Oberkassel" (fetched via the English article).

5. **Trap:** dogs were domesticated once, at a known time, in a known place.
   **Truth:** none of the three is settled. Perri et al. 2021 argue Siberia by about 23,000 years ago (95% CI 26,000 to 19,700) from the correlated divergence of human and dog lineages, and explicitly call for genetic analysis of putative Siberian dog remains "to fully test" it. Bergström et al. 2022 analysed 72 ancient wolf genomes spanning 100,000 years and found dogs closer to eastern than western Eurasian ancient wolves, with Near Eastern and African dogs deriving 20 to 60 percent of ancestry from a distinct western Eurasian wolf-related source that is "either an independent domestication process or admixture from local wolves," and stated that **no** analysed ancient wolf genome is a direct match for either dog ancestry. Write the uncertainty; it is more interesting than a false answer.
   **Source:** Perri, Feuerborn, Frantz, Larson, Malhi, Meltzer and Witt, *PNAS* 118(6):e2010083118, 2021, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2010083118 (open access, abstract and caveats read); Bergström, Stanton, Taron, Frantz, Sinding et al., *Nature* 607(7918), 2022, 313-320, DOI 10.1038/s41586-022-04824-9 (abstract read verbatim).

6. **Trap:** the Goyet skull proves dogs existed 36,000 years ago.
   **Truth:** contested, and the "36,000" figure is unstable. Germonpré and colleagues (2009) identified Goyet and Eliseevichi MAE 447/5298 as Palaeolithic dogs; Drake, Coquerelle and Colombeau (2015) cite those specimens at 31,680 ± 250 BP and 13,905 ± 55 BP respectively and place both inside wolf morphospace, lacking cranial flexion and muzzle concavity. Popular accounts convert or inflate the Goyet date variously. If a number must be printed, print the uncalibrated determination with its convention named, and print the dispute alongside it. Better: name the dispute and skip the number.
   **Source:** Drake, Coquerelle and Colombeau, "3D morphometric analysis of fossil canid skulls contradicts the suggested domestication of dogs during the late Paleolithic," *Scientific Reports* 5:8299, 2015, DOI 10.1038/srep08299 (read via PMC5389137); Morey, *JAS* 52, 2014, 300-307, and Germonpré et al.'s reply, *JAS* 54, 2015, 210-216 (citations verified via search, papers not opened: treat as UNVERIFIED at page level).

7. **Trap:** wolves domesticated themselves by scavenging human rubbish dumps.
   **Truth:** this is Raymond Coppinger's commensal-scavenging model, and it is a hypothesis with a load-bearing problem: a dump implies a settlement, and Late Pleistocene foragers were mobile. Melinda Zeder's commensal pathway (as against her prey and directed pathways) remains the standard framework and does not require dumps. James Serpell's 2021 review is the sharpest published statement of the objection and is the one to cite, because it is open access, single-authored, and lays out three distinct problems rather than one: Last Glacial Maximum populations were too small, too dispersed and too nomadic to keep such a niche reliably stocked; forager practice commonly carries ritual prohibitions against wasting the remains of hunted animals, which limits the carrion on offer; and habituation is not socialization, so a habituated but unsocialized wolf near children is a hazard rather than a tenant. Serpell's alternative is cross-species adoption, people taking pups from dens and hand-rearing them inside a narrow developmental window, documented ethnographically for both wolves and dingoes, for which the technical anchor is Kathryn Lord's comparison of wolf and dog sensory development (*Ethology* 119(2), 2013, 110-120). His single best exhibit is the Bonn-Oberkassel puppy, which he calls "the earliest known evidence for a purely emotion-driven human-dog interaction," and that is one reason this brief opens the story there. Serpell is also the right source for the field's honest range, in his own words: domestication "probably occurred somewhere between 40,000 and 15,000 years ago somewhere on the Eurasian continent, perhaps in more than one location." Lahtinen et al. 2021 offer a third framing fitted to mobile Arctic foragers: humans cannot metabolise much beyond about 20 percent of energy as protein, so in fat-poor winters hunters had surplus lean meat that was useless to them and ideal for wolves, dissolving the competition. The node summary's "tamer wolves fed on human waste" quietly picks a side. Present it as one model among several.
   **Source:** James A. Serpell, "Commensalism or Cross-Species Adoption? A Critical Review of Theories of Wolf Domestication," *Frontiers in Veterinary Science* 8:662370, 2021, DOI 10.3389/fvets.2021.662370 (fetched at the publisher; title, single author, journal, volume, article number, publication date 15 April 2021, DOI and the opening of the abstract verified verbatim); Lahtinen, Clinnick, Mannermaa, Salonen and Viranta, "Excess protein enabled dog domestication during severe Ice Age winters," *Scientific Reports* 11:7, 2021, DOI 10.1038/s41598-020-78214-4 (abstract and argument read via PMC7790815); Zeder, "Pathways to Animal Domestication," 2012 (framework verified via multiple secondary sources; the chapter itself not opened, so cite the framework, not a page).

8. **Trap:** dogs evolved to digest starch as part of becoming dogs, so they were built for human food from the start.
   **Truth:** the *AMY2B* amylase copy-number expansion is largely a farming-era adaptation, not a domestication one. Ollivier, Tresset, Bastian, Lagoutte, Axelsson and colleagues assayed 13 ancient dogs dated between 15,000 and 4,000 years before present and found 2 to 20 diploid copies, with the expansion not fixed and selection for increased copy number beginning **7,000 years ago at the latest**. Ice Age dogs were not eating porridge.
   **Source:** Ollivier et al., "*Amy2B* copy number variation reveals starch diet adaptations in ancient European dogs," *Royal Society Open Science* 3(11):160449, 2016 (findings verified via the Royal Society listing and PMC5180126; full text not opened, so do not attribute a specific page).

9. **Trap:** Belyaev's silver foxes prove that selecting for tameness alone produces floppy ears, curly tails and patchy coats.
   **Truth:** Lord, Larson, Coppinger and Karlsson showed from historical records and genetics that the experiment's founder foxes came from eastern Canadian fur farms and that most traits attributed to selection for tameness **predated the experiment**, and concluded that the weight of evidence does not unambiguously support an animal domestication syndrome. A Comment defending the concept was published in the same journal; the dispute is live. Do not narrate Belyaev as a demonstration.
   **Source:** Lord, Larson, Coppinger and Karlsson, "The History of Farm Foxes Undermines the Animal Domestication Syndrome," *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 35(2), 2020, 125-136 (open access, abstract read); "Defining the Domestication Syndrome: Comment on Lord et al. 2020," *TREE*, 2020 (listing seen, not opened: UNVERIFIED at text level).

10. **Trap:** a child walked through Chauvet beside a dog, 26,000 years ago.
    **Truth:** Michel-Alain Garcia reported canid tracks with a shortened middle digit near a child's footprints, and suggested the two might have travelled together; the contemporaneity rests on a nearby torch mark dated around 26,000 ± 400 BP, and the animal has never been shown to be a dog rather than a wolf. If it were a dog it would predate every genetically confirmed dog by more than ten thousand years, which is itself a reason for caution. I could not open a primary publication for the tracks; treat as **UNVERIFIED** and prefer to omit. The atlas's `paint-in-the-dark` lesson already handles it correctly as "a wolf or a large dog."
    **Source:** secondary accounts only. Do not print a claim here without a primary reference.

11. **Trap:** dogs came into the Americas with the first people.
    **Truth:** on the current best estimates they did not. Perri et al. 2021 place dogs entering with a dispersal beginning about 15,000 years ago; the atlas's own `into-the-last-continents` node rests on the White Sands footprints at about 23,000 to 21,000 years ago. The oldest directly dated dogs in the hemisphere are the Koster and Stilwell II animals from Illinois at 10,190 to 9,630 cal BP, which Perri et al. 2019 also identify as the earliest individual dog burials known anywhere. The forward edge's wording, "later human migrations," is correct; the lesson must not soften it into "the first."
    **Source:** Perri et al., *PNAS* 118(6), 2021 (abstract read); Perri, Widga, Lawler, Martin, Loebel, Farnsworth, Kohn and Buenger, "New Evidence of the Earliest Domestic Dogs in the Americas," *American Antiquity* 84(1), 2019, 68-87, DOI 10.1017/aaq.2018.74 (author list and abstract verified at Cambridge Core).

12. **Trap:** the Předmostí dog skull was buried with a mastodon bone in its mouth.
    **Truth:** mastodons are an American animal; the reported object is a fragment of **mammoth** bone placed between the teeth. This error is loose in secondary summaries. Note also that the Předmostí "palaeodog" identification is itself the contested Germonpré position (Trap 6), so the bone-in-mouth detail should be attributed, not asserted.
    **Source:** Germonpré, Lázničková-Galetová and Sablin, "Palaeolithic dog skulls at the Gravettian Předmostí site, the Czech Republic," *Journal of Archaeological Science*, 2012 (citation verified via publisher listings; full text not opened: UNVERIFIED at page level, and the mastodon/mammoth correction is on general grounds).

13. **Trap:** dogs are pets.
    **Truth:** on the standard estimates there are roughly a billion dogs on Earth and the large majority are free-ranging village dogs rather than household animals; Raymond and Lorna Coppinger frame it as fewer than two hundred million pets against roughly four times as many self-directed dogs. The widely repeated "75 to 85 percent free-ranging" figure is softly sourced; give it as an estimate or use the Coppingers' formulation.
    **Source:** Raymond Coppinger, Lorna Coppinger and Alan Beck, *What Is a Dog?*, University of Chicago Press, 2016 (publisher record and description verified; book not read).

14. **Trap:** dogs helped modern humans outcompete Neanderthals.
    **Truth:** this is Pat Shipman's hypothesis and it is not supported by the current chronology. Do the subtraction on the page. Higham and colleagues dated the disappearance of Neanderthals across Europe to between 41,030 and 39,260 cal BP; the oldest genetically confirmed dog is 14,200 to 14,300 years old, and even the outstanding Erralla claim is 17,410 cal BP. That is a gap of roughly twenty-two to twenty-five thousand years. The hypothesis depends entirely on the contested "palaeodog" specimens of Trap 6 being dogs. Do not use it, even as a rhetorical flourish.
    **Source:** Tom Higham, Katerina Douka, Rachel Wood, Christopher Bronk Ramsey et al. (48 authors), "The timing and spatiotemporal patterning of Neanderthal disappearance," *Nature* 512(7514), 2014, 306-309, DOI 10.1038/nature13621 (metadata verified at Crossref; full text not opened), against the chronology above (Marsh et al. 2026; Bergström et al. 2026).

15. **Trap:** dogs went wherever humans went.
    **Truth:** Australia is the counterexample. People reached Sahul by at least 50,000 years ago (the atlas's own `crossing-to-sahul` node argues 65,000 with the dispute named), and the oldest firmly dated dingo remains, from Madura Cave on the Nullarbor Plain, are 3,450 ± 95 BP, with the authors placing dingoes in southern Australia between about 3,348 and 3,081 years ago. Previously quoted figures of 5,000 to 4,000 years, and DNA-based estimates as high as 18,000, are superseded by direct dates on the bones themselves. Tens of thousands of years of human occupation of a continent with no dog on it.
    **Source:** Jane Balme, Sue O'Connor and Stewart Fallon, "New dates on dingo bones from Madura Cave provide oldest firm evidence for arrival of the species in Australia," *Scientific Reports* 8, 2018, DOI 10.1038/s41598-018-28324-x (three authors, journal, volume, year verified at Crossref; findings from the abstract, open access at PMC6053400).

16. **Trap:** the dogs that crossed into the Americas are the ancestors of American dogs today.
    **Truth:** they are almost entirely gone. Ní Leathlobhair and colleagues sequenced 71 mitochondrial and 7 nuclear genomes from ancient North American and Siberian dogs spanning about 9,000 years and found that pre-contact American dogs formed a monophyletic lineage of Siberian origin which almost completely disappeared after European arrival, leaving a minimal genetic legacy in living dogs. The closest detectable surviving relative of the entire lineage is the canine transmissible venereal tumour, a contagious cancer clone descended from a single dog that lived up to 8,000 years ago. A whole population of animals survives principally as a disease. Handle this with care and without ghoulishness; it is the node's strongest argument that the human-dog relationship is negotiated rather than progressive.
    **Source:** Máire Ní Leathlobhair, Angela R. Perri, Evan K. Irving-Pease et al. (50 authors), "The evolutionary history of dogs in the Americas," *Science* 361(6397), 2018, 81-85, DOI 10.1126/science.aao4776 (author count, journal, volume, issue, pages and year verified at Crossref; findings from the abstract, full text not opened).

17. **Trap:** treating "the oldest dog" and "the oldest confirmed dog" as the same claim, in either direction.
    **Truth:** four different superlatives are in play and popular coverage mixes them. (a) Oldest genetically confirmed dog: Kesslerloch, Switzerland, 14,200 years (Bergström et al. 2026), with Gough's Cave at 14,300 in the companion paper. (b) Oldest dog buried with humans: Bonn-Oberkassel, 14,223 ± 58 cal BP. (c) Oldest *individual* dog burials anywhere: the Koster and Stilwell II dogs in Illinois, 10,190 to 9,630 cal BP, each in its own shallow grave (Perri et al. 2019 make exactly this claim in their abstract). (d) Oldest outstanding claim not confirmed by nuclear DNA: the Erralla humerus, Basque Country, 17,410 to 17,096 cal BP, published in 2022 on morphological and mitochondrial grounds by authors who wrote only that it "represents one of the earliest domesticated dogs in Europe." Keep all four apart or the lesson will contradict itself between paragraphs.
    **Source:** Bergström et al. 2026 and Marsh et al. 2026 (Crossref abstracts read verbatim); Janssens et al. 2018 (full text); Perri et al. 2019 (Cambridge Core abstract and radiocarbon table); Montserrat Hervella, Asier San-Juan-Nó, Aloña Aldasoro-Zabala, Koro Mariezkurrena, Jesús Altuna and Concepción de-la-Rua, *Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports* 46, 2022, article 103706, DOI 10.1016/j.jasrep.2022.103706 (six authors, journal, volume, article number verified at Crossref; dates and mitochondrial finding verified via the University of the Basque Country release; **ScienceDirect returned 403 and the repository record reset, so the paper itself was not opened**).

18. **Trap:** assuming dogs meant access to bigger game.
    **Truth:** at Shubayqa 6 in northeast Jordan the arrival of dogs coincides with a shift toward *smaller* prey, hare and fox, and the excavators argue that new hunting methods, rather than resource depletion alone, deserve consideration in explaining the broad-spectrum revolution. Their measure is the share of small ungulate bone bearing carnivore digestion damage, which rises from 0.8, 0.5 and 7.5 percent in the Natufian levels at Shubayqa 1 to 22.3 and 22.8 percent in the Early and Late PPNA at Shubayqa 6. Note their own caution too: a canid bone may belong to a wolf or a jackal, and tooth-crowding criteria for identifying early dogs have themselves been challenged.
    **Source:** Lisa Yeomans, Louise Martin and Tobias Richter, "Close companions: Early evidence for dogs in northeast Jordan and the potential impact of new hunting methods," *Journal of Anthropological Archaeology* 53, 2019, 161-173, DOI 10.1016/j.jaa.2018.12.005 (full text read from the UCL Discovery open copy; percentages, Natufian span of about 14,500 to 11,600 cal BP, and the small-prey argument verified).

19. **Trap:** the Natufian burial at Ain Mallaha shows "a woman with her arm around her puppy."
    **Truth:** the reported arrangement is an adult skeleton lying on its right side with a hand resting on the thorax of a three-to-five-month-old canid. That is quite affecting enough without embellishment, and the identification of the animal as dog rather than wolf pup rests on the same contested morphological criteria as everything else in this node. **I did not open Davis and Valla's 1978 *Nature* paper**, so the posture description here is at secondary level. Verify before printing, and if it cannot be verified, describe the burial without describing the pose.
    **Source:** Simon J. M. Davis and François R. Valla, "Evidence for domestication of the dog 12,000 years ago in the Natufian of Israel," *Nature* 276, 1978, 608-610 (**UNVERIFIED at source**; cited within Yeomans, Martin and Richter 2019, which was read in full and which also notes the Middle Epipalaeolithic human-and-fox burial at 'Uyun al-Hammam as a caution against reading any interred animal as a domesticate).

## 6. Callback design

Written predecessors, all read for this brief, with the genuine comparison in each case.

- **`world-the-ice-made`** (written; read). **The strongest contrast in the whole set.** That lesson's central argument, built on Richerson, Boyd and Bettinger, is that agriculture was close to impossible in the volatile Pleistocene and close to inevitable once the Holocene stabilised: "You cannot stake your life on a harvest a year away when next year is a coin flip." The dog is the exception that proves the rule, and it proves it precisely because it is an animal and not a crop. A dog does not require you to bet on next year; it feeds itself, moves with you, and pays out daily. So the one domestication that mobile Ice Age foragers could pull off is the one that needed no storage and no stable seasons. Add the geography and the arithmetic: Enu's mammoth-bone camp in that lesson is set around fifteen thousand years ago on the Dnieper; Bonn-Oberkassel is about 14,200 years ago on the Rhine, and Janssens et al. place it in the early Meiendorf Interstadial, exactly the thaw Enu "cannot feel." Roughly eight hundred years and a thousand kilometres apart, the same warming, and in one of the two the world's first domestic animal is being nursed through distemper.

- **`firelight-economy`** (written; read). The leveling machine: arrow ownership prising prestige apart from meat, insulting the meat, hxaro debt, a coalition of the many against anyone who tries to rise. A dog is the first thing that machine has no obvious grip on. It is a self-reproducing asset; it attaches to *one* person, not to the band; it cannot be cut into rings and passed outward as a haunch can. Ask what the leveling coalition does with an animal that follows only Toma. The honest answer is that we do not know for the Palaeolithic, but the later evidence points away from simple property: Robert Losey and colleagues' Cis-Baikal work (*Journal of Anthropological Archaeology* 30, 2011, 174-189) found dogs and wolves in Middle Holocene hunter-gatherer cemeteries treated across the whole range, some casually discarded and some given mortuary care "on par with that given to the human dead." This is the callback that most directly earns the node's **wealth** thread.

- **`spark-of-mind`** (written; read). The ratchet: taught, cumulative culture that outlives its knower. Domestication is that ratchet reaching outside the human body for the first time. A paint recipe is knowledge stored in hands and mouths; a dog is knowledge stored in another species' genome, and the "recipe" gets rewritten by whoever decides which pups live. Also a clean arithmetic hook: Blombos to the earliest genetically confirmed dog is roughly eighty-five thousand years, and in all that time the ratchet only ever turned inward. Strong alternate; use if either recommended target is claimed by another Era I lesson.

- **`tribe-of-strangers`** (written; read). Dunbar's ceiling and the trust problem, solved by fictions: names, beads, totems, gossip. The dog is the odd case, a *non-human* admitted into the web of named relations, and (per Losey) sometimes buried like a person. It is also the technology that solved a problem no fiction could touch: the night. A sentry that never sleeps through a footfall is an outsourced sense, not an outsourced belief. Good alternate, slightly more oblique.

- **`great-dispersal`** (written; read). The obvious link is the Americas, but that comparison belongs in `threadsOut` to `into-the-last-continents`, not in a question. If used as a callback, the sharper angle is different: that lesson's thesis is that humans have no range because culture, not biology, does their adapting. The dog is the first time humans handed a *second* species that same trick, exporting a lineage into biomes its wolf ancestors did not occupy. Third alternate.

- **`paint-in-the-dark`** (written; read). Weakest of the written set for this node, and slightly booby-trapped by the Chauvet canid tracks (Trap 10). If used, the real comparison is the therianthrope and the Lion Man: for tens of thousands of years the only way to fuse a human and an animal was to imagine one. The dog is the first time the fusion is done in the world instead of on a wall. Use as a threadsOut line rather than a question.

**A note on where the callbacks put the reader.** All six written predecessors are anchored in Africa, the Kalahari, Wallacea, the Dnieper or the Ardèche, and this node's headline evidence is Rhineland and Anatolian. That is a lot of Europe for one lesson. The corrective belongs in the significance section rather than the callbacks: the Shubayqa 6 digested-bone sequence in the Jordanian Black Desert, the Jōmon hunting-dog burials, the Zhokhov sled dogs in the Siberian Arctic, the Koster burials in Illinois, and the dingo's late arrival in Australia are all verified, all load-bearing, and all elsewhere. Use at least two of them, and place at least one before the lesson's midpoint so the reader does not settle into a European frame and then get corrected out of it.

Unwritten peers (summary only; use strictly at summary level if forced): `the-eyed-needle` (soft, sewn technology that rarely survives; the dog is soft technology that walks, and would also leave almost nothing), `beads-and-alliances` (a dog as a gift is the highest-value object in a hxaro-like network, since it eats, breeds and can refuse), `the-tended-fire` (the second thing our species learned to keep alive on purpose, and the node's own hook depends on the hearth existing).

**Recommended pair for the two callback questions: `world-the-ice-made` and `firelight-economy`.** They split cleanly: one is climate and mechanism, one is politics and ownership, and together they carry both of the node's threads. Ranked alternates: `spark-of-mind`, then `tribe-of-strangers`, then `great-dispersal`.

**Suggested `threadsOut` (3 to 5):** `into-the-last-continents`, `trap-of-seeds`, `horse-and-wheel` (the three graph edges, each with a fresh why), plus a backward thread to `world-the-ice-made` if the callback questions do not already spend it, or to `firelight-economy` if they do.

## 8. Node-data flags

Precise problems in the committed graph data. Reported, not edited.

1. **The hook asserts a hunting partnership the evidence contradicts.** "an animal is buried in the same grave as the people it hunted with." The Bonn-Oberkassel dog was about 27 to 28 weeks old, spent weeks 19 to 23 gravely ill, and is described by the excavating team as likely untrained with no obvious utilitarian value. The hook's own headline site is the wrong evidence for the claim. Recommend replacing "hunted with" with something the grave supports, for example "the people who kept it alive."

2. **The date field excludes the node's own headline evidence.** `date: 'c. 20,000 to 14,000 BCE'`. Bonn-Oberkassel is 14,223 ± 58 cal BP, that is about 12,270 BCE. Pınarbaşı, now the oldest genetically confirmed dog, is about 15,800 years ago, about 13,775 BCE. Both fall after 14,000 BCE. The packet's existing BP/BCE flag anticipates this; this node is the clearest case for shifting the range roughly two thousand years younger (to something like `c. 23,000 to 12,000 BCE`, which would also cover Perri et al.'s genetic estimate at the old end).

3. **The summary is out of date as of 25 March 2026.** "by genetic estimates deep in the Ice Age and by clear burials such as Bonn-Oberkassel roughly 14,000 years ago." Two *Nature* papers now put genetically confirmed dogs at Pınarbaşı (about 15,800 years ago), Gough's Cave (about 14,300) and Kesslerloch (about 14,200), pushing the direct genetic record back by more than five thousand years. Bonn-Oberkassel remains the oldest known dog burial with humans, which is a different and still-true claim. Recommend the summary say that.

4. **The summary picks a side in a live argument.** "tamer wolves fed on human waste and hunted alongside people" is the Coppinger commensal-scavenging model, which presumes middens and therefore sedentism. It is one hypothesis among several and the phrase "One plausible model is mutual" only partly hedges it. Consider naming it as a model rather than describing it as an event.

5. **"It was the first time our species reshaped another living thing to live with us" needs the word "animal."** As written it invites a reader to ask about managed plants and commensal species. The defensible claim is that the dog is the first domesticated animal and the only one domesticated before agriculture.

6. **The `into-the-last-continents` edge is correct but fragile.** "Domesticated dogs accompanied later human migrations into the Americas" is right, and "later" is doing essential work: the target node's own White Sands evidence is 23,000 to 21,000 years old and dogs are inferred in only from about 15,000 years ago. Anyone editing that edge should not drop "later."

7. **Graph architecture observation.** The node has three outgoing edges and zero incoming edges at globalIndex 15, which makes it a pure source in the middle of Era I. Nothing in Era I points at it, so a reader walking the graph forward from `world-the-ice-made` or `great-dispersal` will not arrive here. Given that the node's best argument is a contrast with `world-the-ice-made` (the one domestication the volatile Pleistocene permitted), a wire from that node into this one would strengthen both. Codex decision, not a defect in this lesson.

8. **Threads.** `craft` and `wealth` are the right pair, but note that the lesson's most defensible material for `wealth` is negative: we do not know that Palaeolithic foragers held dogs as property, and Losey's Cis-Baikal evidence points at least partly the other way. The lesson should earn the pigment by arguing the question rather than assuming the answer. **Addendum from the second research pass:** there is now a positive route to the same pigment. Marsh et al. 2026 infer from a genetically homogeneous dog population spread across Magdalenian, Epigravettian and Anatolian hunter-gatherers that dogs were *exchanged* between culturally and genetically distinct human groups in the Late Upper Palaeolithic. An animal moving between peoples who do not otherwise mix is an item of long-distance traffic, which is `wealth` in the atlas's own sense (compare the ostrich-eggshell beads of `beads-and-alliances` and the hxaro network of `tribe-of-strangers`). This is the strongest available justification for the pigment and it did not exist before March 2026.

9. **The `trap-of-seeds` edge word "rehearsed" claims more than the evidence.** "Taming the wolf rehearsed the domestication of plants and herds that settled farming would soon demand." There is no demonstrated technical or causal transfer. The events are separated by several thousand years, the genetic weight puts dog domestication in eastern Eurasia rather than the Levant, and "rehearsed" implies a shared curriculum and a common cast. "Preceded, by several thousand years and in a different region and by a different route" is the honest version. The lesson can still argue the strong form of the point, which is that the *concept* of a species reshaped by living beside us was proved out here first, and that farming is that concept applied to grasses.

10. **The summary is now two claims out of date, not one.** Beyond the 2026 genetic record (flag 3), it omits the Erralla humerus at 17,410 to 17,096 cal BP, which is the oldest published claim of a domestic dog anywhere and the only one that falls inside this node's own committed date range. Whether Codex adds it depends on how much weight a 2022 morphology-plus-mitochondrial claim should carry against 2026 nuclear data that does not reach it, which is a real editorial judgement rather than an oversight. Flagged so the judgement gets made deliberately.
