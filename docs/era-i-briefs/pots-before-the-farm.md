# Lesson brief: pots-before-the-farm

Research and architecture scaffolding for Fable. Not draft prose. Every claim below carries its evidentiary status. Where this brief and a source disagree, follow the source and say so in the completion report.

Method note: this brief is the product of two research passes. The first verified DOI records (Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, Europe PMC) and read four open-access full texts (Boaretto et al. 2009, Kuzmin 2015, Shoda et al. 2020, Farbstein et al. 2012). The second added web search plus a direct read of **Cohen 2013**, a free 38-page review that turned out to be the single richest source available on this node, and which **resolves two items the first pass had to flag as unverifiable** (the Xianrendong scorch marks, and the absence of residue analysis on the Chinese caves). Items still unopened are flagged UNVERIFIED and must be confirmed or dropped.

## 1. Graph target

- Node id: `pots-before-the-farm`. Title: Pots Before the Farm. Era 1 (The Long Dawn), date `c. 18,000 to 14,000 BCE`, `sort: -14000`, region `easia`, `x: 90`. Threads: **craft**, **wealth**. Global index 17. Question mix the gate will demand: **1 recall, 2 why, 2 callback**.
- Hook as committed: "Foragers who plant nothing shape wet clay into a pot and harden it in a fire. The oldest known pottery on Earth belongs to people with no fields."
- **No incoming edges.** This node is a source, not a confluence, so `threadsOut` carries the whole connective load and the three forward edges sit at the spec minimum. Add one earned backward wire; see section 6.
- Three outgoing edges:
  1. `village-before-the-farm` (echoes, Era I, c. 12,500 BCE). Demands that this lesson establish, cleanly, that the Xianrendong and Yuchanyan potters were **mobile**, so that the Natufian node can carry the separate claim about sedentism. The pairing only works if this lesson refuses to smuggle in sedentism. Set up: pottery without settlement here, settlement without farming there, two independent refusals of the same textbook sequence.
  2. `trap-of-seeds` (collides, c. 9500 BCE). Demands an explicit arithmetic gap between the first pot and the first field, and a statement that pottery is not a step on the road to agriculture. The collision is only felt if the reader has been given the years. It also demands restraint: surplus, ownership and inequality belong to `trap-of-seeds`, and this node must not pre-empt them. Pottery at 20,000 cal BP is a processing technology, not a storage economy.
  3. `song-economic-miracle` (echoes, c. 1000 CE). Demands that the lesson locate the first ceramic tradition in **southern China** specifically. This edge survives the dating dispute below, because Xianrendong (Jiangxi) and Yuchanyan (Hunan) are both southern China under either chronology. But see node-data flag 7: the edge as written smuggles in a national-destiny teleology, and the `threadsOut` prose must be written to disclaim descent.

**This node's job in the atlas.** It is the first technology node in Era I that is neither about the body (fire, needles, clothing) nor about meaning (beads, paint, music). It is the atlas's first synthetic material: the first time humans changed the chemistry of a substance permanently and on purpose. It is also the node that breaks the ladder. Every reader arrives carrying a sequence they were taught, farming, then surplus, then villages, then pots, then cities, and this node takes the fourth rung and puts it ten thousand years below the first. Its second job is planetary: pottery was invented independently at least three or four times in East Asia alone, again in a green Sahara, again in Amazonia, and never once diffused from a single hearth of genius. Its third job is to give Era I an East Asian centre of gravity, which the era otherwise lacks almost entirely.

## 2. Preferred scene

**Yuchanyan Cave, Daoxian County, Hunan Province, southern China, in autumn, around 18,000 cal BP: a cave floor of ash and bone, migratory waterfowl on the wetlands outside, and one crumbling cauldron with a pointed base.**

Not Xianrendong. Two reasons. First, Yuchanyan produced a **reconstructable vessel**, an object the prose can put in a hand, while Xianrendong produced sherds and a dating argument. Second, and more important, Yuchanyan's date is the one that survives the live chronological dispute (see Trap 1): even Kuzmin, the loudest critic of the Xianrendong claim, accepts Yuchanyan as the oldest secure pottery in greater East Asia. Opening at Yuchanyan means the lesson's scene does not stand on the contested foundation. Xianrendong then gets its own paragraph, as the disputed older claim, which is a far better beat than an unexamined superlative.

**The object:** a *fu* (釜) cauldron, **formed by attaching slabs of clay together**, conical with a **pointed base**, **29 cm** tall, round rim **31 cm** in diameter, thick uneven walls **up to 2 cm**, both interior and exterior surfaces impressed, apparently with cordage. The clay carries inclusions of **charcoal, crushed quartz and water-polished pebbles up to 5 mm**. Infrared analysis of fired clay surfaces in the cave puts firing at **400 to 500 °C**; Cohen's summary gives a low firing temperature for the vessel itself, "perhaps 600 °C". That is a hearth, not a kiln. Two or more vessels are represented in total; only one could be reconstructed, because the sherds were too crumbly.

**The clay, and this is the best single detail in the node:** people **carried red, kaolinite-bearing clay into the cave from sources outside it** and laid it down as **discontinuous massive lenticular bands 1 to 3 cm thick and 30 to 50 cm long**. Lenses of ash from wood fires overlie those clay surfaces. Someone hauled raw material uphill into a cave, spread it out on the floor, and lit fires on it. The purpose of the prepared surfaces is unknown, which is itself worth a sentence.

**The setting:** the cave measures 12 to 15 m along its east-west axis and 6 to 8 m north to south, in a karst hillside roughly 450 km south of the Yangzi. Excavated 1993 to 1995 (46 m² opened) and again in 2004 to 2005 in 1 x 1 m squares for tighter stratigraphy. The stone assemblage is dominated by **core-choppers and retouched flakes**; there are also polished bone and antler tools and perforated shell tools.

**The menu, which is unusually rich and gives the scene its season:** over **30% of the faunal assemblage is bird**, with **27 bird species** identified, an amount and diversity Cohen calls a rare discovery in China and evidence of specialised bird hunting not previously seen. **17 of those species are migratory**, mostly waterfowl, whose cycle carried them into South China in **early fall** and back north in early spring. There are **35 species of mammal**, with deer at **43% of mammalian MNI**, taken primarily **prime-aged**, their limb bones fragmented in a pattern indicating **marrow and grease extraction**. There are **18 species of freshwater and terrestrial snail**, and fish including **cyprinids, carp, catfish and perch**, among the earliest evidence in China for regular exploitation of freshwater fish. The excavators read the site as a place mobile foragers used **ephemerally and seasonally**, and the birds argue for autumn to early spring visits.

**The dating:** **40 radiocarbon measurements on 27 prescreened samples**, including duplicate analyses of 10, run by AMS on both charcoal and bone collagen, with heavy prescreening because calcite in the sediments wrecks charcoal preservation. Result: pottery between **18,300 and 15,430 cal BP**, and 18,300 to 17,500 cal BP at one standard deviation.

### Documentation ledger

- **Attested by excavation and publication:** the cave's dimensions and excavation history; the two sherd clusters found 1993 to 1995 plus two further sherds in 2004 and 2005; the cauldron's rim diameter, height, wall thickness, slab construction, pointed base and cord impressions; the 400 to 500 °C fired clay surfaces; the imported red kaolinite clay bands and the ash lenses over them; the faunal list including the 27 bird species, the 43% deer MNI and the fish; the marrow and grease fragmentation pattern; the core-chopper and retouched-flake toolkit; the rice phytoliths and husks; the forty radiocarbon measurements and the calibrated range. From Boaretto et al., *PNAS* 106(24), 2009, and Cohen 2013 pp. 65 to 67 (which cites Prendergast et al. 2009 for the taphonomy and Gu and Yuan 2006 for the birds).
- **Reconstruction, and `storyContext` must say so plainly:** any human being in the scene, any name, gender or age, the weather, who made the pot, exactly where the clay came from, what was cooked in it, whether it was carried or made on the spot, and any dialogue. Yuchanyan has no writing and no burials that name anyone. The **season** is a defensible inference from the migratory birds, not a fact; say "autumn, if the birds are read as the excavators read them", or make it ambient.
- **Real named person available:** **no.** None exists for any Late Glacial pottery site anywhere on Earth. The composite is unavoidable; label it as `spark-of-mind` and `great-dispersal` do.
- **Quantities that must be checked before printing:** 31 cm, 29 cm, 2 cm, 400 to 500 °C, 18,300 to 15,430 cal BP, 40 measurements on 27 samples, 12 to 15 m by 6 to 8 m, 46 m², 450 km, 27 bird species, 17 migratory, 43% deer MNI, 35 mammal species, 18 snail species, clay bands 1 to 3 cm by 30 to 50 cm. Every one is in Boaretto et al. 2009 or Cohen 2013; re-read those rather than trusting this brief.

**Why this scene:** a pointed-based cauldron is a pot designed to sit in embers or in sand, not on a table, and it weighs several kilos wet. It dramatizes the node's real mechanism, which is not "someone discovered fired clay" but "people who moved for a living made something heavy, fragile and expensive, and did it anyway."

### Alternate 1a (new to this pass, and the best-documented *manufacture* anywhere in the node)

**Xianrendong Cave, Wannian County, northern Jiangxi, about 100 km south of the Yangzi, c. 20,000 cal BP: the building of a pot, step by step.** Everything here is attested by Cohen 2013 p. 64, summarising MacNeish et al. 1998, Zhang 2002a/b and Wu et al. 2012:

- The clay was taken from **flood deposits and not cleaned**: the earliest sherds commonly contain **ferric oxide nodules**, read as showing the clay-bearing sediment came from floodplain deposits and went in as it came.
- Temper: **crushed quartzite, occasionally feldspar, in pieces 1 to 5 mm and greater**.
- Build: the earliest pottery was made by **laminating**, joining sheets of clay in layers upward to form the wall. Coiling and paddling with a cord- or fibre-wrapped paddle appears **later** in the sequence, from layer 3B2 upward.
- Surface: the earliest pottery is **"stripe-marked"**, decorated with parallel striations formed by dragging a **tooth-edged tool** across the interior and exterior, then smoothed by scraping the same way. Plain laminated pottery also occurs.
- Rim: **regularly spaced U- or V-shaped notches**, and on some vessels several rows of irregularly spaced punctates below the rim, made by punching a stylus in from the outside, which raises dots on the inside where nobody would ever look.
- Fabric: walls **0.7 to 1.2 cm**; **low-temperature firing**, indicated also by **black cores from incomplete oxidation**.
- Form: **round bottoms**. Only one rounded-bottom vessel could be reconstructed (excavated 1962, uncertain provenience); other sherds indicate round bottoms too. **282 sherds** total from the 1990s excavations, most from later layers.
- Use signal: **many sherds reportedly have scorch marks on the exterior**, indicating the vessels were possibly used in cooking.
- Food: the Xianrendong faunal assemblage is **over 90% deer bone**, with many bones fragmented; marrow and grease rendering is the plausible reading.
- Occupation: the cave was first used ca. 29,000 cal BP and used until ca. 17,500 cal BP, then **abandoned for about 3,000 years**, then reoccupied ca. 14,500 to 12,000 cal BP.

If the writer wants the *making* on the page rather than the *object*, this is the sequence to dramatize, and it is more granular than anything Yuchanyan offers. It can serve as the story's second movement without leaving China, at the cost of opening on the contested date. Recommended handling: open at Yuchanyan, and use this material inside the Xianrendong paragraph so the disputed claim arrives with real texture attached rather than as a bare number.

### Alternate 1b (best-documented *use*; strong as the story's leap, or as the opening if Yuchanyan feels static)

**The Lower Amur, Russian Far East, around 15,000 cal BP: an Osipovka fishing camp at Gasya or Khummi.** Shoda et al. 2020 read lipids from 28 sherds at Khummi, Gasya, Goncharka-1 and Gromatukha. The Lower Amur pots carried **aquatic oils**, most plausibly salmonids and freshwater fish; the Amur today holds over 100 freshwater species plus anadromous runs from late spring to early autumn. **Net sinker weights** were recovered at Gasya from the same layers as the early pottery. A Gasya vessel was reconstructed as flat-bottomed, **25 to 27 cm high, walls 1.2 to 1.7 cm, capacity about 5.5 to 6 litres**, plant-fibre tempered, with **carbonised remains on both external and internal surfaces**, which the authors read as cooking. Sherd counts are tiny: about **40** Osipovka sherds at Khummi despite extensive excavation; **130 of more than 2,000** from the oldest layers at Goncharka-1. The authors doubt that this pottery marks a rush to sedentism, and suggest vessels were **made and cached at seasonal fishing sites** by people mobile the rest of the year.

This is the single best-documented *use* scene available. Its liability is that it is 3,000 years younger than the node's headline and 5,000 km from the node's region. Its gift is the pivot (below).

### Alternate 2 (safest, no composite person at all)

Open inside the measurement rather than the past: a sherd from Khummi, a solvent extraction, a chromatogram, and the moment isoprenoid fatty acids and long-chain omega-(o-alkylphenyl)alkanoic acids say *fish* about a pot nobody had been able to interpret for fifty years. Everything is attested, nobody is invented, and the lesson gets to open on the pleasure of a question being answered rather than asked. Weaker on human warmth, stronger on candour. Hold in reserve.

### Scenes to AVOID

**The discovery myth.** A clay-lined basket falls in the fire, someone notices the clay has hardened, pottery is born. It is untestable, it appears in no source I could open, and it makes the node's subject an accident rather than a decision. It is also wrong in the specific way that matters: fired clay was **not** a discovery waiting to be made, because people had been deliberately firing clay for at least ten thousand years already (Trap 4). The invention was not the material. Opening on an accident destroys the lesson's best idea before paragraph two.

**Opening at Xianrendong with a confident 20,000-year date.** The scene would be built on the exact claim the lesson is obliged to argue about eight paragraphs later, and the reader will feel the switch.

**Opening in Japan.** See Trap 3a. The Japan-first story is the one most readers carry, and the lesson should correct it, not stage it.

## 3. Story movement

Six paragraphs, one place and then one leap.

1. **The pot, cold.** Yuchanyan. Start with the object and the fire it was made in, not with a sunrise. Slabs of clay joined, the pebbles and charcoal in the fabric, the cord pressed into the surface, the drying, the low fire that will never exceed the heat of a good hearth. Give the reader the physical fact that clay does not simply dry: at a few hundred degrees the water bound inside the mineral leaves for good and the stuff becomes a new substance that cannot be un-made. Concrete and sensory: the smell of a firing, the sound a cooling pot makes, the crack that ruins three weeks of work.
2. **The pot, in use.** What it does that a fire alone cannot. Fire had been cooking food for hundreds of thousands of years. A pot does something fire cannot: it holds water at a boil, indefinitely, at a temperature that will not rise above 100 degrees, so that things which cannot be roasted can be rendered, leached, softened and drunk. Smashed deer limb bone into grease. Waterfowl into broth. Bitter seeds into food. An old woman's dinner. A child's.
3. **The cost.** The counter-argument, felt rather than stated. It is heavy. It breaks. It takes days. It cannot be slung on a back across a river. These people move.
4. **The leap north.** The Amur, three thousand years later and five thousand kilometres away. Salmon coming upriver on a schedule. Net weights in the same layer as the sherds. Forty sherds in a whole excavation, which means the pot is not everyday equipment but something reserved.
5. **The resolution of the cost.** You do not carry it. You leave it. And leaving it means you have decided you are coming back to this bend of this river next year.
6. **Pivot.** End there, on the pot left behind, and let the reader arrive at the question the significance answers: if this is not a farming technology and not a settling technology, what is it, and why does it appear in three or four separate places within a few thousand years of one another?

**Governing image: a stomach you can put down.** A pot boils, extracts and holds; it is a second gut that never gets full, that works while you sleep, and that you can walk away from. Return to it once at the leap north (the gut left at the fishing camp) and once at the close (the gut we still cannot walk away from, since every kitchen is a room built around one).

*Secondary image available if the writer prefers something quieter: **the bottom that will not stand.** The earliest vessels are round-bottomed or pointed; they loll on a floor and sit upright only in embers and sand. The flat base and the shelf arrive with the village, ten thousand years downstream. Use one image or the other, not both.*

**Words and framings to avoid.** Never "mysterious", "vanished", "primitive", "crude", "surprisingly sophisticated", "ahead of their time", "prehistoric man", "the dawn of civilization". Never "the first step toward agriculture" or any phrasing that makes the pot a rehearsal. Never stage East Asia, the Amur, the Sahara or Amazonia as an early draft of somewhere else; the Near East is the outlier here, not the standard. Equally, do not swap Euro-teleology for a Chinese one: nothing here is "China on the road to porcelain". Do not write "kiln" (there was none) or "potter's wheel" (millennia away). Do not write "invented pottery" in the singular. Do not use "Neolithic" as a stage word for these caves, which were once labelled that and have been reclassified; if the word appears, it should be the label being broken. Do not call the Late Glacial climate a backdrop of misery; the Amur pottery appears in both cold and warm phases.

## 4. Significance spine

Five movements. The lesson must tie explicitly, in words, to **craft** and **wealth**, the node's two committed threads. Craft carries the weight easily. Wealth needs care: storage is *not* a demonstrated function of Late Glacial pottery, so the wealth thread must be argued as **yield** (the same carcass, more calories) and as the container's long consequence, never as this pot's proven job. A light touch of **ideas** (a recipe held across generations; a material transformation understood before it could be explained) is earned and welcome, but craft and wealth are the two that must be named.

**1. Evidence, and the shape of the argument.** How we know: radiocarbon on charcoal and bone collagen, prescreened; radiocarbon directly on the charred food crusts stuck to sherds, which dates the *use* of the pot rather than something near it; thermoluminescence on the ceramic itself at Gasya; micromorphology and stratigraphy at Xianrendong. The single sharpest sentence available to the lesson: **Wu et al. did not date the pottery. They dated the sediments the pottery sat in**, using 45 radiocarbon determinations across two reopened trenches, with soil micromorphology to test whether sherds and samples had moved since burial. In cave archaeology "how old is this object" is usually the question "did this object ever move", and the answer is a microscope, not a machine. Then the map. Kuzmin's accepted centres: **South China c. 18,000 cal BP, the Japanese Islands c. 16,700 cal BP, the Russian Far East c. 15,900 cal BP**, with **Transbaikal c. 14,000 cal BP** as a probable fourth, independent of all three; he finds no reliable evidence that pottery-making diffused from any of these centres to its neighbours. Anchor sites: Odai Yamamoto 1 in northern Honshu (13,780 ± 170 BP on charred adhesion, 16,170 to 17,180 cal BP); Khummi (13,260 ± 100 BP, 15,640 to 16,240 cal BP). And the fact that best conveys how slowly this moved: **the earliest pottery in North China appears about eight millennia later than in South China**, at Younger Dryas sites such as Nanzhuangtou (12,400 to 11,400 cal BP), Zhuannian, Donghulin, Lijiagou and Yujiagou, which is also four millennia later than Japan and the Russian Far East.

**2. The named live debate, part one: how old is the oldest pot?** Wu, Zhang, Goldberg, Cohen, Pan, Arpin and Bar-Yosef published Xianrendong at **20,000 to 19,000 cal BP** in *Science* in 2012, on micromorphology and a large series of dates from what they argue are stable stratigraphic contexts, and concluded that pottery preceded agriculture by ten millennia or more. **Kuzmin (2015)** rejects the date on four specific grounds, all worth giving the reader because they are unusually legible: no sherds were recovered from the 2009 sections that produced the dated bone, so there is no direct association between sample and pot ("We did not recover any sherds from the reopened sections"); age-depth reversals run through the profile, with Layer 3B1 dating older than the layers both above and below it; a previously published younger date from the second-oldest pottery stratum was left out; and dates that did not fit the model were called outliers without stated reason. Kuzmin's own figure for Xianrendong is **c. 14,600 cal BP**, and he awards the record to **Yuchanyan at c. 18,010 cal BP**. **David J. Cohen (2013)** answers in detail: MacNeish, whose "short" chronology Kuzmin prefers, **rejected 27 of his own 34 radiocarbon determinations** to preserve a seriation and kept a single date of 12,530 ± 140 BP; the rejected samples had exactly the same kind of association with the pottery as the one he kept; their reliability was never tested against stratigraphy at all, which is precisely what the 2009 re-dating did test. Cohen's case is internal consistency across a large systematic series, repeated in two separate parts of the cave, with micromorphology showing that post-depositional processes had not moved the samples. Kuzmin's rejoinder is that Cohen never closes the sherd-to-sample gap. **No verdict.** One tell that the argument is still live: **Jordan et al.'s 2016 diffusion model still uses c. 16,000 cal BP for the East Asian origin**, a third figure, younger than either. The dispute turns on something a lay reader can actually judge: whether a date on a bone is a date on a pot.

**3. Mechanism: what a pot is for, why then, and how we finally found out.** *Why there:* South China through the Last Glacial Maximum was the warmer, wetter, more resource-abundant end of a continent whose north went cold and near-desert; lithic traditions in the south stayed simple core-and-flake partly because **bamboo** could be worked into knives, spears, containers and matting, so nothing forced more complex stoneworking. *Why then:* **intensification**. At Yuchanyan the taphonomy shows deer taken prime-aged and limb bones fragmented for marrow and grease; birds enter the diet in force; freshwater fish appear as a regular resource for the first time in China. Those are the signatures of people squeezing more out of the same landscape, and Carmody, Weintraub and Wrangham showed experimentally that thermal processing raises the energy actually gained from both meat and starchy tubers, and raises it more than pounding does. A fireproof, watertight container is the machine that converts bone into grease and bird into broth. *How we know what was in them:* for fifty years the function of the earliest pots was guesswork; then residue chemistry answered it, and the answer was food, cooked, mostly from water. Craig et al. (*Nature*, 2013) recovered lipids from **101 charred deposits** on Incipient Jomon pottery of **c. 15,000 to 11,800 cal BP** and found most derived from **high-trophic-level aquatic food**. Lucquin et al. (*PNAS*, 2016) followed **143 vessels** through **9,000 years** at the single site of Torihama and found the link to fishing unbroken from the Late Palaeolithic well into the Holocene, with almost no signal of terrestrial animals or plants even though the site's occupants demonstrably ate them. Lucquin et al. (*PNAS*, 2018) scaled it to **more than 800 vessels at 46 sites** and found the aquatic association held **irrespective of ecological setting**, and held after the climate warmed. Shoda et al. (2020) then broke the pattern open: the Lower Amur pots carried aquatic oils as predicted, but at **Gromatukha on the Middle Amur, 700 km west**, the residues were dominated by **ruminant fats**, read as bone-grease rendering by mobile hunters, "a more continental flavour", mapping onto a genuinely different pottery-making tradition in temper, surface treatment and decoration. So: two trajectories, not one. Regional traditions with opinions, not a single technology spreading. *What it caused:* almost nothing, for a very long time, and the lesson should say so. Across East Asia early pottery appears in small amounts at few sites and stays episodic and low-scale from the Last Glacial Maximum to the early Holocene. This is the honest anti-triumphal shape of the node and it is far more interesting than a breakthrough narrative.

**4. The named live debate, part two: why then, and what for?** Three positions, all in print, no verdict. (a) The **aquatic model** (Craig, Lucquin, Jordan and colleagues): productive water-edge ecotones were being worked hard by Late Glacial foragers, and that intensification supplied the impetus for investing in ceramic containers. (b) **Kuzmin's plant model**: pottery answered a need for light, easily made vessels for boiling and leaching wild plants, nuts and fruits that are otherwise very hard to use, a view he traces back to Ikawa-Smith in 1976. (c) **The continental model** that Shoda et al. raise from Gromatukha: rendering bone grease from carcasses in lean seasons, an attractive option for hunting groups. Alongside these sits **Gibbs (2015)**, who reframes the whole question by contrast: early pottery made by **farmers in the Near East** is best read as *innovation* inside a long-running container tradition, while the first **hunter-gatherer** pottery of East Asia is the better case for genuine independent *invention*. And behind all of them sits **Hayden's** older reading of early pottery as a **prestige technology** for feasting and obligation-building, which Cohen takes seriously and which predicts elaborate forms and rapid decorative specialisation. Give the reader the negative result too: Shoda et al. explicitly could not tie the Amur's earliest pottery to a climatic threshold, because it turns up in the Older Dryas cold, the Bolling-Allerod warmth and the Younger Dryas cold alike. **And give the reader the hole:** there is no published residue, starch or physicochemical analysis of the Xianrendong or Yuchanyan pottery, so the Japanese answer cannot simply be exported to Jiangxi and Hunan. Honest uncertainty range: the invention is real, its trigger is not established, and the sherd counts (tens, not thousands, in the earliest levels) suggest something occasional rather than everyday.

**5. Landing: why the modern world still carries this node, and the anti-ladder.** Three moves. First, the ladder. In the Levant the Neolithic is literally named **Pre-Pottery**: farming there is thousands of years older than the first pot, exactly inverting East Asia. In West Africa at **Ounjougou, Mali**, pottery appears **before 9400 cal BC**, made by foragers of a green Sahara harvesting wild grasses that were boiled rather than ground into bread. In the **Brazilian Amazon at Taperinha near Santarem**, the earliest pottery in the Western Hemisphere comes from an **eighth-millennium shell midden**, made by people living off the river. Four continents, four sequences, no ladder. Second, the material, which is the **craft** thread. Fired clay is the first substance humans made that does not occur in nature, and the technique, take an abundant earth, add heat, get a new material with new properties, is the same sentence that later reads cement, glass, iron, porcelain and silicon. Say plainly that the ceramic technology was already thousands of years old **as art** before anyone used it to make a container: the invention was not the material but the idea of a thing that **holds**. Third, the container, which is the **wealth** thread and must be phrased carefully: nothing here is shown to have stored anything, but a pot converts the same animal into more food, and it is among the first valuable made things a person cannot carry away, which is a different kind of having. The idea of a durable vessel that outlives the meal inside it is the precondition of every granary, amphora, warehouse and shipping container in the atlas above. Close on the sherd: fired clay is nearly indestructible, which is why potsherds are archaeology's universal clock, and why a technology invented by people who left almost nothing else behind is the reason we can date almost everything after them.

## 5. Factual traps

Fifteen. This is the highest-value section of the brief.

1. **Trap:** "The oldest pottery in the world is from Xianrendong Cave, 20,000 years ago," stated flat, as the node's summary states it. **Truth:** contested, in print, by a specialist in exactly this chronology. Kuzmin (2015) puts Xianrendong at **c. 14,600 cal BP** and awards primacy to **Yuchanyan at c. 18,010 cal BP**. Wu et al. (2012) and Cohen (2013) hold 20,000 to 19,000 cal BP; Jordan et al. (2016) model the East Asian origin at c. 16,000 cal BP. Write "the oldest **securely dated**" or "the oldest **claimed**", and name the dispute. **Source:** Kuzmin, *Documenta Praehistorica* 42 (2015) 1 to 11, DOI 10.4312/dp.42.1 (read in full); Wu et al., *Science* 336(6089), 2012, 1696 to 1700, DOI 10.1126/science.1218643; Cohen, *Journal of Austronesian Studies* 4(2), 2013, 55 to 92, pp. 62 to 63 (read in full); Jordan et al., *Antiquity* 90(351), 2016, 590 to 603, DOI 10.15184/aqy.2016.68.

2. **Trap:** "Soot and scorch marks on the pots prove they were used for cooking." **Truth, now resolved at paper level and more nuanced than either the node summary or the first research pass had it.** Cohen 2013 p. 64 reports that **"many sherds reportedly have scorch marks on the exterior, indicating that the vessels were possibly used in cooking"** at Xianrendong, attributing the observation to MacNeish et al. 1998. So the scorch marks are real and published, but they arrive doubly hedged ("reportedly", "possibly"), and the word is **scorch**, not soot. Cohen p. 65 then states flatly: **"We still have no clear data as to how pottery at Xianrendong (or Yuchanyan) was used. There have been no residue, starch, or other physiochemical analyses done on the pottery."** I found nothing published since that changes this. The Boaretto et al. Yuchanyan paper discusses no soot, charring or use-wear at all. **Demonstrated** cooking evidence comes from residue chemistry in Japan and on the Amur, not from the Chinese caves. Recommendation: write "scorch marks", attribute the inference as an inference, and anchor the *demonstrated* cooking claim on the lipid work. **Source:** Cohen 2013, pp. 64 to 65; Boaretto et al., *PNAS* 106(24), 2009, 9595 to 9600.

3. **Trap:** "Pottery was invented once, in China, and spread from there." **Truth:** at least three East Asian centres, independently, within about two thousand years of one another (South China c. 18,000 cal BP, Japan c. 16,700, Russian Far East c. 15,900), plus Transbaikal at c. 14,000 "most probably independent of the primary East Asian centres". Kuzmin states that no reliable evidence exists for diffusion from any of the three to its neighbours. Add Ounjougou in Mali before 9400 cal BC and Taperinha in Amazonia in the eighth millennium and the count rises again. Statistical modelling of the whole Old World radiocarbon record supports **at least two independent hunter-gatherer origins**, East Asia c. 16,000 cal BP and North Africa c. 12,000 cal BP. Cohen adds that whether East Asia itself had one origin or several is **still open**. **Source:** Kuzmin 2015; Jordan et al. 2016; Huysecom et al., *Antiquity* 83(322), 2009, 905 to 917; Roosevelt et al., *Science* 254(5038), 1991, 1621 to 1624; Cohen 2013, p. 57.

3a. **Trap:** "Japan has the world's oldest pottery; the Jomon invented it." **Truth:** Incipient Jomon begins ca. 16,800 cal BP, thousands of years after South China under any of the competing chronologies except Kuzmin's lowest figure for Xianrendong. This was the consensus for decades and is **still in circulation in museum copy**: a Tokyo National Museum exhibition page currently carries the line that the earliest pottery in the world may have been made in Japan, and the UNESCO World Heritage page for Odai Yamamoto calls its sherds "the earliest known pottery in Northeast Asia", which is a narrower and defensible claim readers will over-read. Correct it explicitly; do not stage it. **Source:** Cohen 2013, pp. 55 to 57; Jomon Prehistoric Sites in Northern Japan, Odai Yamamoto page.

4. **Trap:** "The oldest fired clay is pottery." **Truth:** by roughly ten thousand years, no. **Dolni Vestonice** in Moravia yielded **more than 5,000 ceramic artefacts**, made of local loess, **fired at 500 to 800 °C**, and overwhelmingly **figurines, not vessels**; many fracture surfaces show thermal shock, and the excavators concluded that "the making, firing, and sometimes exploding of figurines may have been the prime function of ceramics at this site rather than manufacturing permanent, portable objects." **Vela Spila** on Korcula, Croatia, produced **36 ceramic artefacts**, zoomorphic figurines, dated **c. 17,500 to 15,000 BP**, contemporary with the Chinese pots and still not a single vessel; the authors argue independent invention given the ten-thousand-year gap from the Pavlovian. So the knowledge of how to fire clay and the idea of firing it into a container are two different pieces of knowledge, separated by millennia. This is the single most useful correction in the brief. **Sub-trap:** the 1989 *Science* paper says the figurines "predate the earliest pottery vessels by some 14,000 years." **Do not repeat that figure.** It was written when the oldest known vessels were thought to be about 12,000 years old; the denominator has moved and the gap is now roughly six to ten thousand years depending on whose Xianrendong date you accept. **Source:** Vandiver, Soffer, Klima and Svoboda, *Science* 246(4933), 1989, 1002 to 1008; Farbstein, Radic, Brajkovic and Miracle, *PLoS ONE* 7(7), 2012, e41437.

5. **Trap:** "The first pots were for storage, or for brewing beer." **Truth:** neither is demonstrated anywhere in the Late Glacial record. Every residue study that has looked has found **cooked food**: aquatic in Incipient Jomon Japan (101 charred deposits) and on the Lower Amur, ruminant fats on the Middle Amur. Storage and fermentation remain live possibilities and are not findings; Oliver Craig, who established the aquatic signal, later argued for fermentation in delayed-return economies, but that is a proposal about a class of economies, not a finding about these caves. The node summary already hedges this correctly; keep the hedge. Note also the physical argument: a round or pointed base does not stand on a floor, it sits in embers or sand. **Source:** Craig et al., *Nature* 496(7445), 2013, 351 to 354; Lucquin et al., *PNAS* 113(15), 2016, 3991 to 3996; Lucquin et al., *PNAS* 115(31), 2018, 7931 to 7936; Shoda et al., *Quaternary Science Reviews* 229, 2020, 106124; Craig, *Current Anthropology* 62(S24), 2021, S233 to S241, DOI 10.1086/716610 (metadata only, full text 403; do not paraphrase beyond the title).

6. **Trap:** "Pottery marks the beginning of settled village life." **Truth:** Wu et al. describe the Xianrendong potters as **mobile foragers hunting and gathering during the Late Glacial Maximum**. On the Amur, Shoda et al. write that the sites "only appear to have had ephemeral surface structures" and that "it seems unlikely that the earliest appearance of pottery was somehow associated with a rapid transition to fully sedentary village-based societies", proposing instead that vessels were made and **cached at seasonal fishing sites**. Cohen places Incipient Jomon sites as contemporaneous with Final Upper Palaeolithic sites, and Xianrendong's pottery precedes the first sedentary Early Neolithic villages in China by **some ten millennia**. Watch for one live contradiction the writer may meet: the UNESCO page for Odai Yamamoto says its plain pottery "demonstrates the emergence of sedentism" and, in the same breath, that the site has **no post holes or underground structures** and its people probably used portable tents. This is load-bearing: the `village-before-the-farm` edge depends on it. **Source:** Wu et al. 2012; Shoda et al. 2020; Cohen 2013, pp. 56 to 57.

7. **Trap:** "Pottery came before farming everywhere." **Truth:** the opposite in the Near East, where the Neolithic's own period names, Pre-Pottery Neolithic A and B, record the fact that agriculture there is millennia older than ceramics. Gibbs (2015) argues that "early pottery production by farmers in the Near East can be viewed as a process of innovation in a longer tradition of container technology, while the first hunter-gatherer pottery production in East Asia provides a better case for independent invention." **On the absolute date, partly resolved and still soft:** Gibbs (2022) states that sustained production of ceramic containers begins at sites in Anatolia, Upper Mesopotamia and the northern Levant **around 7000 BC or slightly later**, and that pottery does not become common in the southern Levant and Cyprus until centuries after that. **I could not open that article's full text; the phrasing reached me through its abstract.** Safest wording: "the seventh millennium BCE", or simply "thousands of years after farming", with the Pre-Pottery Neolithic named. **Source:** Gibbs, *Cambridge Archaeological Journal* 25(1), 2015, 339 to 351, DOI 10.1017/S0959774314001139; Gibbs, *Quaternary International* 608 to 609, 2022, 194 to 202, DOI 10.1016/j.quaint.2020.09.040 (metadata verified at Crossref, full text UNVERIFIED).

8. **Trap:** "Before pottery, people could not boil water." **Truth:** hot-rock boiling in skin, bark, wooden or pit containers is documented in the Upper Palaeolithic, and pottery is a **better** boiling technology rather than the first one. **UNVERIFIED:** I confirmed only the bibliographic record of Nakazawa, Straus, Gonzalez-Morales, Cuenca Solana and Caro Saiz, "On stone-boiling technology in the Upper Paleolithic: behavioral implications from an Early Magdalenian hearth in El Miron Cave, Cantabria, Spain", *Journal of Archaeological Science* 36(3), 2009, 684 to 693, DOI 10.1016/j.jas.2008.10.015; I did not read its content. Confirm before making any specific claim about El Miron, or make the point generically as "containers that do not survive". Note that the same argument underwrites Gibbs's Near Eastern case: the container is older than the ceramic.

9. **Trap:** "Rice at Yuchanyan and Xianrendong means these people were already growing rice." **Truth:** both caves were once described as "Early Neolithic" and thought to be inhabited by cultivators of perhaps domesticated rice. That reading has been abandoned. **Five grains of rice** were recovered at Yuchanyan in the 1990s; the later excavations, with finer provenience control, screening and flotation, **recovered no further rice macroremains**, and the rice phytoliths are consistent with gathering in the abundant wetlands surrounding the site. Cohen's abstract states there is **"no evidence of plant cultivation"** at these sites and that they are Late Palaeolithic forager camps. Wild rice gathered is not rice farmed. This is exactly the detail that turns into "the first farmers" in a careless sentence. **Source:** Cohen 2013, abstract and p. 67; Boaretto et al. 2009.

10. **Trap:** "These pots were crude." **Truth:** low-fired and thick-walled, yes, but the makers had opinions, and Cohen's technical word "crudely made" describes fabric and firing, not skill. At Xianrendong the earliest vessels were **laminated** from sheets of clay, scraped with a **tooth-edged tool** into parallel striations, then smoothed, with rims cut in **regularly spaced U- or V-shaped notches** and rows of punctates below the rim raising dots on the **interior**, where nobody would see them. Osipovka potters on the Lower Amur tempered with **gravel, grog and dried clay**, used plant fibre sparingly, scraped surfaces with **hard comb-like tools** as a diagnostic habit, and moved from shallow cord impressions to combed patterns; Gromatukha potters on the Middle Amur used **grass temper**, cord-rolled grooves and dense zigzag bands in horizontal registers. Two distinct traditions that never converged. Write "low-fired" and "hand-built", never "crude". **Source:** Cohen 2013, pp. 64 to 65; Shoda et al. 2020; Kuzmin 2015.

11. **Trap:** BP and BCE confusion. **Truth:** "about 20,000 years ago" is **c. 18,050 BCE**, not 20,000 BCE (20,000 minus 1,950). The committed node date, `c. 18,000 to 14,000 BCE`, is a **correct** conversion of a roughly 20,000 to 16,000 cal BP window, so do not "fix" it. The lesson should reconcile the two conventions explicitly once, early, because a reader who sees "18,000 BCE" in the header and "20,000 years ago" in the first paragraph will assume one of them is a typo. Worked comparisons the lesson can use: Xianrendong's claimed 20,000 to 19,000 cal BP is **c. 18,050 to 17,050 BCE**; Yuchanyan's 18,300 to 15,430 cal BP is **c. 16,350 to 13,480 BCE**; Odai Yamamoto 1's 17,180 to 16,170 cal BP is **c. 15,230 to 14,220 BCE**; the earliest Osipovka horizon at 16,200 cal BP is **c. 14,250 BCE**, sitting right on the node's lower bound. Related sub-trap: the UNESCO Odai Yamamoto page gives "around 13,000 BCE", which does not reconcile with Cohen's ca. 16,800 cal BP for the start of Incipient Jomon. **Use "years ago" throughout and avoid converting the Japanese dates to BCE at all.**

12. **Trap:** "Pottery led to agriculture." **Truth:** no causal link is demonstrated, and Kuzmin's framing of **two trajectories** in the neolithization of Eurasia, pottery in one and agriculture in the other, exists precisely to keep them apart. Do the arithmetic on the page instead: from Xianrendong's claimed date to the atlas's own `trap-of-seeds` node at c. 9500 BCE is roughly **8,500 years**; from Xianrendong to rice domestication in the Yangzi is longer still, which is why *Science* could write "10 millennia or more before the emergence of agriculture". Say **which** agriculture, and give the number. **Source:** Kuzmin, "Two Trajectories in the Neolithization of Eurasia", *Radiocarbon* 55(3 to 4), 2013, 1304 to 1313, DOI 10.2458/azu_js_rc.55.16201 (bibliographic record verified; full text not opened, so cite the framing, not its figures); Wu et al. 2012.

13. **Trap:** "Xianrendong was continuously occupied," or "the potters lived there." **Truth:** the dated series indicates the cave was used from ca. 29,000 cal BP until ca. 17,500 cal BP, then **abandoned for about 3,000 years**, then reoccupied ca. 14,500 to 12,000 cal BP. Yuchanyan shows a comparable pattern of gaps. Cave deposits with cultural material throughout were long assumed to mean continuous occupation, and micromorphology has shown that the gaps are stratigraphically invisible. If the lesson wants one haunting detail, this is it: the potters stopped coming, and nobody came back for three thousand years. **Source:** Cohen 2013, p. 64, summarising Wu et al. 2012.

14. **Trap:** "The oldest pottery in the world" is a fixed title. **Truth:** it has moved twice in living memory (Japan to China, then Yuchanyan to Xianrendong) and may move again; every such claim is a minimum on present evidence and present sampling. Write "the oldest we have found", which is more honest about how archaeology works and rhymes with `paint-in-the-dark`'s identical lesson about Sulawesi. **Source:** Cohen 2013, pp. 55 to 57.

15. **Trap:** "The pot arrives and everything changes." **Truth:** across East Asia early pottery appears in **small amounts at few sites** and persists in that episodic, low-scale way from the Last Glacial Maximum to the early Holocene, roughly nine thousand years, before it becomes ordinary. Forty sherds at Khummi after extensive excavation; 282 at Xianrendong across the whole 1990s excavation, most from later layers; two or more vessels at Yuchanyan. The invention is real and its uptake is glacial. Any sentence implying rapid transformation is wrong. **Source:** Cohen 2013, p. 56 and p. 64; Shoda et al. 2020.

## 6. Callback design

Six earlier nodes have written lesson files and are therefore legal targets. All six were read in full. The `earlierUnwrittenPeers` in the packet (first-of-our-kind, the-tended-fire, ochre-for-the-dead, ash-across-asia, the-other-humans, crossing-to-sahul, beads-and-alliances, the-hollow-bone, the-eyed-needle, wolf-at-the-fire, into-the-last-continents) have no lesson files and **must not** be used as `callbackTo` targets. `wolf-at-the-fire` is the painful loss: its date range `c. 20,000 to 14,000 BCE` is nearly identical to this node's, and the first dogs and the first pots are the same few thousand years, the first two things humans made that were not made of stone, bone, wood or hide. Keep that contemporaneity for the writer's own use in prose; do not make it a callback question.

1. **`world-the-ice-made` (strongest; use it).** Its thesis is that agriculture was close to impossible in the volatile Pleistocene and close to inevitable once the Holocene calmed, because "you cannot stake your life on a harvest a year away when next year is a coin flip." This node is the sharpening counter-case, not a contradiction: the ice made *farming* impossible and did **not** make *technology* impossible. A pot is a bet on a season, not on a year, and a season is exactly the size of bet a lurching climate permits; a cooking pot is an immediate-return tool that pays back tonight, while a harvest is a delayed-return gamble. The arithmetic is a gift: that lesson dates the Last Glacial Maximum floor to around 20,000 BCE, and the Xianrendong claim is 20,000 to 19,000 cal BP, that is c. 18,050 to 17,050 BCE, so the first pots come roughly two thousand years *after* the coldest point, in the long cold that lesson's composite Enu is already living in. The two lessons sit within a couple of millennia of one another at opposite ends of Eurasia, one on a bone-house steppe eating from permafrost pits, the other in a warm wet southern refuge boiling waterfowl, which also shows the ice age as a differentiated planet rather than one cold. Ask the reader to reconcile "climate permitted farming only later" with "climate did not stop this."

2. **`firelight-economy` (strong; recommended as the pair).** That lesson's most quotable line is that the forager economy "did not store grain. It stored relationships." A durable container is the first serious candidate for the other kind of storage, and the honest answer, which the reader should be made to sit with, is that we cannot show it was used that way. What we *can* show is a middle case that neither lesson alone contains: on the Amur, people cached the **vessel**, not the food, at a fishing camp they meant to return to. That is storing a tool rather than a surplus, and it is the smallest possible first step from an economy of circulation toward an economy of accumulation. Also available, and genuinely fresh: boiling divides differently from roasting. A carcass yields joints; a pot yields broth, which portions to as many hands as you choose. Flag clearly that this is an argument, not a finding.

3. **`great-dispersal` (strong alternate).** That lesson states outright that humans had filled the planet "before a single seed was deliberately planted, before the first pot or the first street." This node is that sentence's next clause: the first pot arrives, and it arrives in three or four places nearly at once, because equally capable people were already standing on all of those rivers. It is the same argument the Sulawesi paintings make in `paint-in-the-dark`, and it is the atlas's cleanest weapon against a diffusionist reading. Use it if the writer wants the planetary beat as a question rather than as a paragraph. Slight risk of restating that lesson's own ending.

4. **`spark-of-mind` (good, but heavily spent).** The ratchet, and the multi-step recipe. Nka's paint kit (grind, crush, render, mix, store) and a pot (select clay, temper, form, dry, fire at the right heat, and know when it is right) are the same cognitive object: a sequence held in a person's hands and passed on. The fresh angle, and it is genuinely fresh, comes from Trap 4: at Dolni Vestonice people already knew how to fire clay ten thousand years before anyone fired it into a container, so **the recipe was not the bottleneck; the idea of what to make was**. That is a real refinement of the ratchet rather than a restatement, and it teaches the limit of the earlier lesson's own idea: cumulative culture explains how knowledge survives, not when it gets used. The liability is that `spark-of-mind` is already the callback target in three of the six written lessons.

5. **`paint-in-the-dark` (moderate).** Two real angles. Its Europe-first correction rhymes with this node's diffusion-first correction, and its argument that "art arrives already fluent" applies here **in the negative**, which is more interesting: pottery does not arrive fluent, it arrives thick-walled, black-cored and rare, and stays that way for thousands of years. Ask why one craft appears mature and the other appears to crawl. There is also the chronological rhyme with Trap 4: fired clay served **meaning** before it served **food**. The risk is restating that lesson's ending; `great-dispersal` carries the planetary point better here. Spend it in `threadsOut` if at all.

6. **`tribe-of-strangers` (weakest; do not use).** Beads, gossip and Dunbar's number. A pot is not a symbol and does not vouch for a stranger. The only routes in are feasting (not demonstrated for any Late Glacial pottery) and the down-the-line exchange networks Cohen invokes for how pottery knowledge could travel, which is too thin to build a question on. Forcing it would produce exactly the trivia-style callback the spec forbids.

**Recommended pair for the two callback questions: 1 and 2.** Rank of alternates: 3, then 4, then 5. Do not use 6.

**Suggested `threadsOut` (3 to 5, mirroring the graph plus one earned backward wire):** `village-before-the-farm`, `trap-of-seeds`, `song-economic-miracle` (written per flag 7 to disclaim descent), and `world-the-ice-made` backward. `great-dispersal` substitutes cleanly for the last if the ending leans planetary rather than climatic. Each `why` must be new prose, not the edge label from the data file.

## 8. Node-data flags

Precise problems in the committed graph data. **Do not edit any repo file. Reported for Codex.**

1. **The summary states a contested date as settled.** "The oldest known pottery, from Xianrendong Cave in southern China, dates to about 20,000 years ago." Kuzmin (2015) puts Xianrendong at c. 14,600 cal BP and gives primacy to Yuchanyan at c. 18,010 cal BP; Wu et al. (2012) and Cohen (2013) hold 20,000 to 19,000; Jordan et al. (2016) model c. 16,000. Recommend "the oldest securely dated pottery" or a summary that names both caves. The hook's "the oldest known pottery on Earth" is defensible as written, because "known" already does the work.

2. **The summary's "Soot and scorch marks" is half right and should be tightened.** Resolved this pass: the published term for Xianrendong is **scorch marks**, reported by the 1990s excavators and relayed by Cohen with two hedges ("reportedly", "possibly"). **Soot** belongs to the Japanese and Amur material, where it comes with lipid analysis of charred adhering deposits. The Yuchanyan report discusses no soot, charring or use-wear. Recommend "scorch marks" for the Chinese caves, or "soot and scorch across the East Asian early-pottery record" if the plural region is intended. The rest of that sentence, and the clause about storage and fermentation being possibilities rather than demonstrated functions, is exactly right and should be preserved.

3. **Threads `craft` and `wealth`: the wealth pigment is the weaker half.** The node's own summary correctly says storage is not demonstrated, which leaves wealth resting on a consequence rather than a finding. It is defensible if the lesson argues **yield** (the same carcass, more calories, via boiling and grease rendering) plus the container as the precondition of later accumulation, and this brief tells the writer to do exactly that. But if Codex wants a second pigment the evidence carries outright, `ideas` (a multi-step recipe transmitted across generations; the first synthetic material) is better supported than `wealth`. Flagging, not recommending a change.

4. **Hook wording undersells the mechanism.** "Harden it in a fire" describes drying. Firing at 400 to 600 °C drives off chemically bound water and produces a new substance that cannot revert. "Hardens" is not wrong so much as it is the least interesting true thing to say about the node's core event.

5. **The date field is correct; the packet's BP/BCE flag over-worries this node.** 20,000 cal BP minus 1,950 is c. 18,050 BCE and 16,000 cal BP minus 1,950 is c. 14,050 BCE, so `c. 18,000 to 14,000 BCE` with `sort: -14000` is a faithful conversion of the real evidence window and **should be left alone**, and should not be shifted if the auditor moves `wolf-at-the-fire` and `into-the-last-continents`. The residual risk is presentational: the node's summary says "about 20,000 years ago" while its date field says 18,000 BCE, and a reader will read that as an error unless the lesson reconciles the conventions once.

6. **Graph architecture: no forward wire into East Asia.** The node's three exits go to the Levant (`village-before-the-farm`), to a global node (`trap-of-seeds`), and to China in 1000 CE (`song-economic-miracle`). A reader following the graph forward from the world's first pot leaves East Asia for nineteen thousand years. The packet's own adjacency note names Era II's "Rice and the Yangzi" as a coordinate target; a wire from this node to it would close the gap and let the `trap-of-seeds` collision be felt regionally rather than only globally. Codex decision, not a defect in this lesson.

7. **The `song-economic-miracle` edge survives the dating dispute on geography, but its "why" text asserts a continuity that does not exist.** "The oldest ceramic tradition on Earth began in the same East Asia that would later fire porcelain for the entire world" reads as descent. Between them sit roughly nineteen thousand years, the abandonment of Xianrendong itself for three millennia, several population turnovers, and the entire subsequent history of Chinese ceramics; the geography of Late Glacial cave pottery is not the geography of Song kilns. Two problems: it is not supportable, and it smuggles in a national-destiny teleology, which is the same error as Euro-teleology in a different hat. Recommend rewording toward: two societies at opposite ends of the record found the same solution to the same physical problem, at incomparable scales, with no line of descent. The lesson's `threadsOut` entry must be written that way regardless of whether Codex changes the edge.

8. **Region and adjacency.** Region `easia` and `x: 90` are right, but note that the node's strongest *evidence* is Japanese and Russian Far Eastern rather than Chinese, and the lesson will spend real time on the Amur and on Honshu. `easia` still holds; Codex should know the lesson roams.

9. **"Southern China" is right by convention and may misread.** Xianrendong is in **northern Jiangxi**, about 100 km **south** of the Yangzi; "South China" in this literature means south of the Qinling and Huai line, not the southern coast. The lesson should place it concretely ("in the hills of northern Jiangxi, south of the Yangzi") so the reader is not left picturing Guangdong.
