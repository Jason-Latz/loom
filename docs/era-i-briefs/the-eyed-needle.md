## 1. Graph target

- Node id: `the-eyed-needle`. Title: The Eye of the Needle. Era 1 (The Long Dawn), date `c. 40,000 BCE`, region `world`, globalIndex 13. Threads: `craft` only.
- Question mix the gate will demand: 1 recall, 2 why, 2 callback.
- Outgoing edges (three), and what each demands this lesson set up:
  1. `world-the-ice-made` (enables): the lesson must establish that garments are survival equipment at glacial temperatures, so that the later climate node can treat the deep cold as survivable rather than as a wall. Set up thermal insulation as a physical quantity, not a mood.
  2. `into-the-last-continents` (enables): the lesson must put sewn skin clothing physically in the far north of Asia before the peopling of the Americas, without claiming the needle caused the crossing. The atlas's own White Sands node sits at roughly 23,000 to 21,000 years ago; Yana RHS at 71 degrees north is roughly 33,000 cal B.P. State that the sewing kit is older than the footprints and stop there. The decisive arithmetic is in trap 7: the earliest eyed needle in the Americas is thousands of years *younger* than the footprints.
  3. `trap-of-seeds` (echoes): the edge's stated analogy is "sewing reshaped hides, farming reshaped landscapes." This is the weakest of the three (see flags). If the lesson wants it, earn it through a different and better idea: both are cases of humans manufacturing their own environment rather than moving to a better one, which is the deepest continuity in the atlas.
- Incoming edge (one): `the-other-humans` (echoes), whose "why" reads: Neanderthals endured glacial Europe with cold-weather skill our lineage would extend once it learned to sew fitted clothing. That node assumes this lesson delivers a non-triumphalist account of what sewing added. It must not be spent on a parka-versus-cape morality tale (see trap 5 and the avoided scene). Free structural gift: Denisova Cave, source of the earliest eyed needle, is also the type site of the Denisovans named in that node, so the atlas can return to one cave for a second reason.

**This node's job in the atlas.** It is the atlas's argument about the archaeological record itself: that the material bias of the ground has quietly written the history of technology for us, and that the technologies that mattered most in the Ice Age were soft, sewn, and gone. Nothing a Pleistocene person actually wore survives anywhere on Earth. We infer clothing from three completely separate kinds of evidence, none of which is clothing: the tools that made it, the animals that were skinned for it, and the parasites that live in it. The node also does a second job the graph does not say out loud. Blombos put symbols on skin and on a cord; Chauvet put them on a wall; the eyed needle is where symbols move onto the surface a covered body actually presents to other people. That is the transition from clothing to dress, and it is the most interesting claim available to this lesson.

## 2. Preferred scene

**Yana RHS, the lower Yana River, Arctic Siberia, 71 degrees north, around 33,000 years ago: a sewing kit in eight grades of needle, open on a floor at the top of the world.**

Yana RHS is the richest Palaeolithic sewing assemblage on the planet. Gilligan and colleagues (2024) record from it **192 eyed needles and eyed needle fragments, 4 needle cases, and 79 bone awls** at a site dated to **33,000 cal B.P.** at latitude **71 degrees north**, and note **eight varieties of eyed needle**, read as specialized functions in the production of complex clothing (underwear, mittens, outer garments). **[pass 2: needle counts, latitude, date and the eight varieties re-verified verbatim in the open-access full text.]** The site was published by Pitulko and colleagues in *Science* in 2004 (occupation about 27,000 radiocarbon years B.P., which the 2024 review calibrates to roughly 33,000 cal B.P.) **[pass 1]**, and the sewing assemblage itself by Pitulko and Pavlova, "Upper Palaeolithic sewing kit from the Yana site, Arctic Siberia," *Stratum Plus* (2019), 157 to 224 **[pass 2, citation from secondary listings only: I could not open the article, so do not quote it and do not use it as a clickable item]**. The site also yielded personal ornaments and symbolic objects, published in *Antiquity* as the oldest art of the Eurasian Arctic **[pass 1]**. Some Yana needles carry four or five dots or cuts placed symmetrically near the eye: decorated tools **[pass 2, secondary]**.

The scene writes itself out of the object list: a box of needles graded fine to coarse, exactly like a modern sewing kit, at a latitude where an uncovered hand fails in minutes. The finest needles in that box are the argument. You do not need a fine needle to close a seam. You need one to put a bead on a sleeve.

**Documentation ledger**

- *Attested:* the 192 needles and fragments, the 4 needle cases, the 79 awls, the eight varieties, the latitude, the date, permafrost preservation, the presence of ornaments at the site, and the general Palaeolithic hide-working toolkit (scraper, borer, awl, smoother, needle) as recorded object by object at other sites with inventory numbers.
- *Reconstruction:* every person, every name, every gesture, the weather, the season, who sewed and who did not, the light they worked by, what the garment was for. Yana has no writing and no burials that name anybody. `storyContext` must say plainly that the seamstress is a composite assembled from the excavated kit.
- *Real named person available:* **no.** None exists for any site in this lesson, at any date.
- *Quantities that must be checked before printing:* do not invent a needle length or eye diameter for Yana. If a physical measurement is wanted, take it from the Lascaux needle instead, where the custodian publishes it: height 3.6 cm, width 0.27 cm, thickness 0.2 cm (Musée national de Préhistoire, inv. D 2004.3) **[pass 1]**. Do not state a thread material for any site (trap 9). Do not print the Denisova needle's "7.6 cm bird bone" as fact: it is press-level (trap 3).
- *Date arithmetic the story must not fudge:* 33,000 cal B.P. is roughly nine thousand years after the node's own date field, and roughly seven thousand years after the earliest eyed needle. If the lesson opens here, the gap belongs in the first two paragraphs or in `storyContext`.

**Why this scene.** It is the only place on Earth where the lesson's whole argument is physically present in one floor: needles fine enough to be pointless for warmth, at the coldest inhabited latitude then known, next to beads. It is also emphatically not in Europe, which matters, because Europe is the last region in the world to get this technology.

### Alternate 1 (the object that overturns the intuitive story; strongest second movement) **[pass 2, fully verified]**

**Terrasses de la Riera dels Canyars, Gavà, near Barcelona, roughly 39,600 cal B.P.: a slab of hip bone used as a punch board, hide laid over it, holes driven through both.**

Doyon, Faure, Sanz, Daura, Cassard and d'Errico (*Science Advances* 9(15), eadg0834, 12 April 2023) describe **28 human-made punctures** on a **107 mm** fragment of the right ischium of a large bovid or equid, in **at least six groups made with different tools**, one set of **ten punctures** showing remarkable uniformity in morphology, distance and alignment; puncture depths run **44.7 to 294.6 micrometres, mean 155.7**. Experiments with **dihedral burins and indirect percussion** reproduced the marks: one blow drives through hide and dimples the bone beneath. Radiocarbon on the layer gives **33,800 ± 350 to 34,900 ± 340 B.P.**, calibrating to **37,405 to 40,916 cal B.P. at 2 sigma**. The authors' conclusion is the sentence this whole lesson turns on: 14,000 years before eyed needles are introduced in Europe, Palaeolithic hunter-gatherers were able to make tailored leather clothing.

*Ledger caution the writer must respect:* **Canyars is not a camp.** The excavators describe an open-air fluvial terrace acting as a natural sediment trap, with over 5,000 bone remains accumulated mainly by carnivores (hyenas prominent), only six stone artifacts of which two carry Aurignacian retouch, and no hearths, no knapping floors and no ornaments. Staging a tailor "in her camp at Canyars" invents a settlement the excavation explicitly denies. Write the object and the hand, not the village.

This is the single most valuable addition to the lesson's second movement: it is nearly on the node's date, it is peer-reviewed to the micrometre, and it kills the origin myth (trap 1) with a physical thing rather than an argument.

### Alternate 2 (best-documented craft sequence; use for the manufacturing detail) **[pass 1]**

**Grotte du Placard, Vilhonneur, Charente: a Magdalenian sewing kit, five objects, five inventory numbers.** Held by the Musée d'Archéologie nationale and published as its object of the month for September 2024: flint scraper (grattoir), bone eyed needle (aiguille à chas), flint borer (perçoir), bone awl (poinçon), bone smoother (lissoir). Magdalenian, roughly 20,500 to 13,000 years ago. Maximum lengths 9.0, 6.0, 5.2, 12.3 and 15.5 cm. Inventory numbers MAN 53139, 55115, 55097, 55017, 54954. The museum's own text gives the operating sequence: after the carcass is skinned, the hide is cleaned with a scraper (which can be hafted), then thinned and softened with a bone smoother; a burnished hide becomes more water-resistant; drying is a critical stage that leaves no trace at all. The page also says, exactly, that the Palaeolithic eyed needle was identified immediately by the first prehistorians because its form and function have not changed since, while our picture of the clothes has changed a great deal.

That last sentence is the lesson's ending in one line. Its liability: the kit is French and Magdalenian, roughly twenty thousand years after the node's date, and opening there re-centres Europe on a story where Europe arrives last. Use it as a source of craft detail, not as the cold open.

### Alternate 3 (safest, no invented person, one gorgeous object) **[pass 1]**

**The eyed needle from the floor of Lascaux.** Musée national de Préhistoire, inv. D 2004.3 (série Glory), found 15 January 1958 in the floor of the Passage. Bone, almost complete (tip missing), circular in section below and thinned above, the **eye slightly stained with ochre**, made by pressure perforation from both faces. Badegoulian/Magdalenian, between 23,000 and 17,000 years ago; restored in 2019. The custodian notes that it is one of many ordinary tools and human debris found on the floor of a painted cave generally considered a sanctuary, and that although some eyed needles are Solutrean, most are Magdalenian. A needle with ochre in its eye, lying on the floor of Lascaux, is an entire lesson compressed into 3.6 centimetres. Hold in reserve, or use as the story's last image.

### Scenes to AVOID as a cold open

- **The Neanderthal freezing in a cape while a modern human zips up a parka.** Three reasons. (1) The underlying paper (Collard et al. 2016) argues a hypothesis from relative frequencies of hare, dog-family and weasel-family bones; the popular headline version is press, not the paper. (2) Neanderthals survived multiple glacial cycles across hundreds of thousands of years, made three-ply cord, and used bone smoothers on ochred hide. (3) The atlas's own incoming edge from `the-other-humans` is written as continuity, not as a race won. Parka-versus-cape material belongs inside significance, as a live and contested hypothesis, never as theatre.
- **The invention myth:** a shivering family, a cold snap, a flash of genius, the first needle, warmth. Wrong on the evidence and wrong in kind, because it stages technology as a lightbulb rather than the refinement of an old craft.
- **An Ötzi or Areni-1 style scene of an actual surviving garment.** No Pleistocene garment survives anywhere (trap 8).

## 3. Story movement

Six paragraphs, two movements and a hinge. Governing image: **the eye.** The hole in the needle, and the eye that reads a stranger's clothes across a hundred paces. Let the word turn once at the hinge and once at the close. Do not over-press the biblical phrase; the title already carries it, and a nod is a nod.

1. **The kit.** Yana, the top of the world. Open inside the work: the bone split into a blank, the shaft ground down, the eye made by pressure from both faces (attested technique, per the MNP record). Cold that is a fact about the hand, not an adjective. Sensory anchor: bone dust, grease, the sound of a scraper on a hide.
2. **The grades.** The box has eight kinds of needle. Coarse for the outer skin, medium for the seams that face the body, and one so fine it would be absurd for anything structural. Let the reader notice the fine one before the lesson points at it.
3. **What the fine needle is for.** Beads. The parka's chest, the cuff, the hood's edge. Not warmth. Somebody is going to look at this.
4. **The hinge.** Widen: this person did not invent clothing. Hides were being scraped, softened and stitched with awls and burins for tens of thousands of years before any eye was drilled through bone, and the oldest tools we can point to for it come out of a cave on the Atlantic coast of Morocco. If a second concrete object is wanted here, use the Canyars punch board: a hide pinned over a slab of hip bone and pierced ten times in a line, four thousand miles west and six thousand years earlier than the Yana kit. What is new at Yana is not covering. It is fineness, layering, and the fact that a covered body still has to say who it belongs to.
5. **The problem the cold created.** Once you are covered from crown to ankle, ochre on the skin, scarification, painted lines: none of it can be seen. The mark has to move outward, onto the thing everybody can see. (This is the Gilligan et al. 2024 argument; present it as an argument, not as settled fact.)
6. **The pivot, not the moral.** End on the finished garment, worn, walking into another band's firelight, and on the plain question significance answers: if none of this survives, how does anyone know any of it, and what else has the ground silently deleted from the history of human ingenuity?

**Words and framings to avoid.** "Mysterious." "Vanished." "Primitive." "Cavemen." "Dawn of fashion" (it is the press headline for the 2024 paper and it trivialises the argument). Do not stage Siberia or China as a warm-up for Europe; the sequence runs the other way and the lesson should let the reader feel that. Do not say the needle "unlocked" or "opened the door to" the Americas. Do not assign the sewing to women, or to men (trap 10). Do not call the Yana people "the first Arctic peoples" without qualification. Do not use the phrase "second skin"; it is the obvious reach and it is wrong, because the whole point is that a garment is a surface, not a skin.

## 4. Significance spine

Five movements. Threads that must be tied in words: **craft** (the node's own pigment, and the visible one: bone, grinding, drilling, hide chemistry) and **ideas** (dress as a sign system, which is the lesson's real thesis and is not currently in the node's thread list). **Wealth** is available as a third if the word count allows: fur-bearing animals had to be trapped rather than hunted for meat, and Sunghir's ivory beads represent thousands of hours of labour worn on a body.

**1. Evidence: three streams, none of them clothing.** No garment survives from the Pleistocene anywhere. So:

- *The tools.* Awls first, and old: the earliest bone awls are southern African, at Blombos Cave roughly 73,000 to 70,000 years ago, with Sibudu around 61,000 and Border Cave around 44,000 cal B.P. At Blombos, use-wear analysis suggests **85 percent** of bone tools functioned to perforate fairly soft material such as well-worked hides, possibly in the manufacture of clothing or carrying bags **[pass 2, quoted verbatim in the 2024 paper]**. Eyed needles arrive only in the second half of the last glacial cycle. And the direct proof that awls and burins were enough: the Canyars punch board, above.
- *The animals.* At Contrebandiers Cave on the Atlantic coast of Morocco, deposits dated by three concordant methods (electron spin resonance, thermoluminescence, optically stimulated luminescence) to roughly 120,000 to 90,000 years ago yielded 62 worked bone tools including seven spatulate tools on ribs, alongside cut-marked bones of **sand fox (*Vulpes rueppellii*), golden jackal (*Canis aureus*) and wildcat (*Felis silvestris*)** in patterns consistent with modern fur removal rather than butchery for meat. The excavators call it highly suggestive proxy evidence for the earliest clothing, and say so as proxy, explicitly. **[pass 1]** Lead this movement with Morocco. It is the single best correction to the assumption that this story starts in the north.
- *The parasites.* The clothing louse lives in garments, not on the body, so its origin dates the habit of being dressed. Toups, Kitchen, Light and Reed (2011) put the divergence of clothing lice from head lice at a **median of about 170,000 years, mode about 83,000, with a 95 percent highest posterior density interval of 29,000 to 691,000 years** **[pass 2, verified at the publisher]**. Print that interval. It is one of the most honest numbers in Era I.

**2. Mechanism: why there, why then, and what it caused.** Gilligan et al. 2024 give the geography, and the writer should put the sequence on the page in order **[pass 2, re-verified against the full text]**: Denisova Cave, Siberia, about **40,000 cal B.P.**; Mezmaiskaya, Caucasus, about **38,000**; Zhoukoudian Upper Cave **35,000 to 33,000**; Yana RHS **33,000**; Xiaogushan **33,000 to 21,000**; Shuidonggou 2 about **32,000**; a cluster of central Siberian sites (Kunalei-4, Sabanikha, Kurtak-4, Ust-Kova, Shestakovo, Mal'ta, Khayrgas, Strashnaya) **32,000 to 23,000**; Potočka Cave and Kostenki-15 about **30,000**; Aitzbitarte III **28,000 to 22,000**; Shizitan **26,000 to 23,000**; and western Europe only with the **Solutrean**; then Broken Mammoth in interior Alaska, 64 degrees north, about **14,000 cal B.P.** The mechanism is not "now we can sew." It is *fineness*: finer stitching, thinner seams, and the multilayer assemblage. Gilligan's thermal note, verbatim-adjacent: up to four layers are needed in the subarctic, and four layers are mandatory for outdoor survival during Arctic winters when mean monthly temperatures are between **minus 10 and minus 20 Celsius**, though the modern four-layer classification likely corresponds to a two-layer situation with Palaeolithic garments. What it caused, at minimum: continuous rather than ad hoc body cover in the coldest inhabited places, and the trapping of small fur-bearers as a distinct subsistence activity.

**3. Named live debate (use this one, and give no verdict).** *What is an eyed needle actually evidence of?*

- *Side A, the 2024 revision.* Gilligan, d'Errico, Doyon, Wang and Kuzmin, in *Science Advances*, argue that the production of fitted garments does not require eyed needles, and that fitted garments were likely manufactured with awls, burins and other devices before the advent of eyed needles. The needle's real advantage is efficiency, in their own formulation: it combines two separate processes into one, the piercing of holes in hides and the threading of sinew or fibre through the holes. The benefits therefore may pertain to adornment of clothes and to the need for underwear in multilayered garment assemblages. Gilligan's public formulation: eyed needles document a transition in the function of clothing from utilitarian to social purposes.
- *Side B, the functional reading, still the default in the literature.* The eyed needle is the tailoring instrument that made close-fitting cold-weather clothing practical, and the clothing difference is a real variable in Upper Palaeolithic success at high latitude. Collard, Tarle, Sandgathe and Allan (2016) tested this with a novel proxy, the frequency of mammal families ethnographically used for cold-weather clothing, and found leporids, canids and mustelids more frequent in early modern human strata than in Neanderthal strata, which they read as fur trim on fitted garments.
- *Why it stays open.* Side A concedes its own weak point in print: despite the logic, convincing evidence for underwear in the late Pleistocene is scant **[pass 2, verbatim]**. Side B rests on bone frequencies that have many possible causes. Do not resolve it. The honest statement is that the needle is the best-dated object in the story and the worst-understood one.
- *Available second debate if wanted (a):* Neanderthal clothing. Collard et al. 2016 (cape-like only) against Nathan Wales (2012, *Journal of Human Evolution* 63(6), 781 to 795), who modelled clothing from 245 recent hunter-gatherer groups and inferred substantial coverage. Note also the three-ply cord from Abri du Maras and the Châtelperronian bone awls, which Gilligan et al. call a possible late adoption of fitted garments among some Neanderthal populations. **[pass 1]**
- *Available second debate if wanted (b):* whether the discipline systematically discounted perishable technology. Olga Soffer and J. M. Adovasio have pressed that case for three decades, most sharply in "Their Fingers Were too Fat to Weave: Ancient Textiles and Academic Politics Today," *North American Archaeologist* 35(4), 419 to 437, 2014, DOI 10.2190/NA.35.4.h, recounting the reception of their 1993 recognition of textile impressions on fired clay from Upper Palaeolithic Moravia and arguing that the deep past lives in the political present. **[pass 2, metadata and argument verified at the publisher]** This is, in effect, the meta-argument for why this node exists, and it is contested.

**4. Honest uncertainty.** Five ranges, stated plainly and not hedged sentence by sentence. (a) The louse interval, 29,000 to 691,000 years. (b) The Denisova needle: about 40,000 cal B.P. in the 2024 review, versus a widely circulated 2016 press figure of 50,000; the cave was occupied successively by Denisovans (roughly 130,000 to 73,000 years ago), Neanderthals (from more than 59,000 years ago to about 50,000 cal B.P.) and *Homo sapiens* probably after 40,000 cal B.P. **[pass 2, verbatim from the 2024 paper]**, so the maker's species is genuinely unknown. (c) The perishable record generally: a three-ply cord fragment 6.2 mm long on a stone flake at Abri du Maras, in layers dated to between 41,000 and 52,000 years ago, is the entire direct evidence for Neanderthal fibre technology; spun, dyed and knotted wild flax fibres at Dzudzuana Cave in Georgia from layers of about 32,000 to 26,000 years B.P. drew a published Comment in *Science* disputing the identification, with a published Response; woven-textile impressions on fired clay at Pavlov I are about 26,000 years old. This is a record of accidents. (d) The eastern dates: Zhoukoudian Upper Cave's chronology has been revised more than once, and the 2024 paper's regional prose summary does not exactly match its table entries; cite the table and say the range is under revision. (e) The node's own date: "about forty thousand years ago" is cal B.P., which is roughly 38,000 BCE, not 40,000 BCE.

**5. Landing: what the modern world still carries.** Two things, in this order.

- *The object.* The eyed needle is arguably the least-changed tool humans own. The Musée d'Archéologie nationale says it flatly: the Palaeolithic eyed needle was recognised instantly by the first prehistorians because its form and its function have not changed since. The wheel, the plough, the sword and the lamp have all been redesigned out of recognition. The needle has not. Whatever is in the sewing tin in the reader's drawer is, in every respect that matters, the Yana object.
- *The idea.* Once decoration moved off the skin and onto the garment, clothing became the fastest-read social signal our species has. A uniform, a veil, a wedding dress, a school tie, a logo on a chest: each is a bead sewn onto a sleeve for someone else's eye. Tie this explicitly back to Blombos: the shell bead that vouched for a stranger has been transferred, whole, onto the surface a covered body presents. And close on the material irony: the technology that carries the most social information in the modern world is the one the ground almost never keeps.

## 5. Factual traps

The confident-sounding wrong things a writer will otherwise reach for. Twelve, ranked roughly by how likely each is to appear in a first draft.

1. **Trap:** the eyed needle made fitted, tailored clothing possible. **Truth:** the current authority says the opposite in as many words: the production of fitted garments does not require eyed needles, and fitted garments were likely manufactured with awls, burins and other devices before the advent of eyed needles. Awls run back to Blombos at 73,000 to 70,000 years, where 85 percent of bone tools show use-wear from perforating worked hide. And there is a physical proof object: the Canyars punch board, 28 punctures on a 107 mm hip-bone fragment, ten of them uniformly spaced and aligned, made by driving a dihedral burin through hide by indirect percussion, in a layer calibrated to 37,405 to 40,916 cal B.P., which its excavators state is 14,000 years before eyed needles reach Europe. The needle is a refinement, and what it refines is disputed. **Source:** Gilligan, d'Errico, Doyon, Wang and Kuzmin, "Paleolithic eyed needles and the evolution of dress," *Science Advances* 10(26), eadp2887, 28 June 2024, DOI 10.1126/sciadv.adp2887 **[pass 1 and pass 2]**; Doyon, Faure, Sanz, Daura, Cassard and d'Errico, "A 39,600-year-old leather punch board from Canyars, Gavà, Spain," *Science Advances* 9(15), eadg0834, 2023, DOI 10.1126/sciadv.adg0834 **[pass 2]**.

2. **Trap:** the date is 40,000 BCE. **Truth:** the literature's figure is about **40,000 cal B.P.**, which is about 38,000 BCE. The node's `date` field mixes the conventions and reads roughly two thousand years too old. In prose, use "about forty thousand years ago" and never convert silently. **Source:** Gilligan et al. 2024; Table 1 is in cal B.P. throughout. **[pass 2]**

3. **Trap:** the world's oldest needle is 50,000 years old and was made by a Denisovan. **Truth:** that figure comes from a 2016 excavation announcement by Mikhail Shunkov of the Institute of Archaeology and Ethnography, Novosibirsk (a bird-bone needle from Denisova Cave, reported as two and three-quarter inches), carried through the press. The 2024 peer-reviewed review places Denisova's earliest eyed needles at about 40,000 cal B.P., and sets out three successive occupations (Denisovans roughly 130,000 to 73,000 years ago, Neanderthals from more than 59,000 to about 50,000 cal B.P., *Homo sapiens* probably after 40,000 cal B.P.), so no attribution is secure. Write "about forty thousand years ago, in the Altai, by people whose species we cannot name from the object alone." Do not print the 7 cm / 7.6 cm length as an established measurement. **Source:** Gilligan et al. 2024 for the date and occupation sequence **[pass 2]**; Douka et al., "Age estimates for hominin fossils and the onset of the Upper Palaeolithic at Denisova Cave," *Nature* 565 (2019), 640 to 644, for the site chronology **[pass 1]**; Archaeology Magazine news item, 23 August 2016, for the press claim **[pass 2]**.

4. **Trap:** lice DNA proves clothing is 170,000 years old. **Truth:** 170,000 is the median of a posterior distribution whose 95 percent interval runs from **29,000 to 691,000 years**, with a mode near 83,000. Worse for the headline: head and body lice are now usually treated as two ecotypes of *Pediculus humanus* rather than two species, they produce fertile offspring in the laboratory, and the divergence may be recurrent rather than a single dateable event. The louse evidence establishes that habitual clothing is much older than any needle. It does not establish a date. **Source:** Toups, Kitchen, Light and Reed, "Origin of Clothing Lice Indicates Early Clothing Use by Anatomically Modern Humans in Africa," *Molecular Biology and Evolution* 28(1), 2011, 29 to 32, DOI 10.1093/molbev/msq234 **[pass 2, publisher page read: divergence "at least by 83 Ka and possibly as early as 170 Ka," mode 83 Ka, median 170 Ka, 95 percent HPD 29 to 691 Ka]**; the earlier estimate is Kittler, Kayser and Stoneking, *Current Biology* 13(16), 2003, 1414 to 1417, whose molecular clock was later compromised by a chimpanzee-louse sequence identified as a contaminant, with a published erratum (*Current Biology* 14, 2004, 2309) **[pass 1 for the erratum citation; pass 2 confirmed the contaminant story via the 2011 paper's own discussion, and could not open the erratum itself]**.

5. **Trap:** Neanderthals had no real clothing and froze out while sewn-up moderns took over. **Truth:** three separate corrections. Collard et al. 2016 argue for cape-like clothing as a hypothesis, from relative frequencies of leporid, canid and mustelid bones, and say so as a hypothesis; Wales 2012 models substantially greater coverage; Hardy et al. 2020 recovered a three-ply cord fragment on a stone tool at Abri du Maras in layers dated 41,000 to 52,000 years ago; and Gilligan et al. 2024 note Châtelperronian awls and ochre-stained bone smoothers at Grotte du Renne, 45,000 to 42,000 cal B.P., and describe Neanderthal garments as probably simple and poncho-style rather than absent. Neanderthals were not naked and they were not incompetent with fibre. **Sources:** Collard, Tarle, Sandgathe and Allan, *Journal of Anthropological Archaeology* 44, Part B, December 2016, 235 to 246, DOI 10.1016/j.jaa.2016.07.010 **[pass 1 verified at the Aberdeen repository; pass 2 confirmed the same metadata but could not render the author's PDF, so quote nothing from it]**; Wales, *Journal of Human Evolution* 63(6), 2012, 781 to 795 **[pass 1, Crossref only]**; Hardy, Moncel, Kerfant, Lebon, Bellot-Gurlet and Mélard, *Scientific Reports* 10, 4889, 2020, DOI 10.1038/s41598-020-61839-w **[pass 1; note an Author Correction exists, check before printing a number]**; poncho reading in Gilligan et al. 2024 **[pass 2]**.

6. **Trap:** Europe invented the eyed needle, or Europe is where it matters. **Truth:** on the published sequence Europe is **last**, by something like fourteen thousand years: Siberia about 40,000, the Caucasus about 38,000, East Asia in the mid-thirties to low twenties of thousands, Potočka and Kostenki-15 about 30,000, Aitzbitarte III 28,000 to 22,000, and western Europe only with the Solutrean. Note also the local distortion: the Musée d'Archéologie nationale's own object page dates the invention of the eyed needle to about 20,000 years ago, which is the French Magdalenian record speaking, not the world. A good chance to show the reader how a regional record becomes a global claim. **Source:** Gilligan et al. 2024, Table 1 and text **[pass 2]**; MAN, "Un nécessaire de couture magdalénien," objet du mois, September 2024 **[pass 1]**.

7. **Trap:** the eyed needle opened the Arctic and let people into the Americas. **Truth:** the sequence is compatible and the causation is not demonstrated, and one number settles it. The earliest eyed needle in the Americas is at **Broken Mammoth, interior Alaska, 64 degrees north, about 14,000 cal B.P.**, which is roughly **seven to nine thousand years after** the atlas's own White Sands footprints at 23,000 to 21,000 years ago. Gilligan et al. say only that eyed needles *accompanied* the expansion into Siberia and across Beringia. Separately, people crossed open water into Sahul between roughly 65,000 and 50,000 years ago with no eyed needles anywhere on Earth, and there is no eyed needle in the Australian Pleistocene record at all. Say "accompanied," never "because of." **Sources:** Gilligan et al. 2024 for Broken Mammoth and the accompaniment wording **[pass 2]**; Alaska Department of Natural Resources, Office of History and Archaeology, Broken Mammoth Archaeological Project, directed by Charles E. Holmes, whose own page gives the earliest occupations as about 11,500 and about 10,400 B.P. uncalibrated (same occupation, different units: never mix the two figures in one sentence) **[pass 2]**; Pitulko et al., *Science* 303, 2004, 52 to 56 **[pass 1]**; the atlas's own `crossing-to-sahul` and `into-the-last-continents` nodes.

8. **Trap:** the oldest surviving garment is Ötzi's, or some Ice Age fur has survived somewhere. **Truth:** nothing worn in the Pleistocene survives anywhere. The oldest surviving leather footwear is the one-piece cowhide shoe from Areni-1 cave in Vayots Dzor, Armenia, at about 5,500 years old; the oldest woven garment is the Tarkhan dress in the UCL Petrie Museum, radiocarbon dated at Oxford in 2015 to 3482 to 3102 BCE at 95 percent confidence. Between the Yana needles and the Armenian shoe lie roughly twenty-six thousand years with no garment at all. **Source:** UCL news release, February 2016, on the Tarkhan dress dating, published in *Antiquity*; Areni-1 shoe published in *PLoS ONE*, excavation co-directed by Boris Gasparyan and Ron Pinhasi. **[pass 1]**

9. **Trap:** the thread was sinew. **Truth:** thread material for the Palaeolithic is inference from ethnography, not evidence; the peer-reviewed phrasing is "sinew or fibre." What is directly attested is plant fibre: spun, dyed and knotted wild flax at Dzudzuana Cave, Georgia, from layers of about 32,000 to 26,000 years B.P., and even that identification drew a Comment in *Science* in 2010 arguing the fibres could not be identified as flax on the published evidence, with a Response from the original team; plus woven impressions on fired clay at Pavlov I at about 26,000 years ago. If the story wants a thread in a hand, say what it is and label the inference. The node's hook currently asserts sinew flatly. **Sources:** Kvavadze, Bar-Yosef, Belfer-Cohen, Boaretto, Jakeli, Matskevich and Meshveliani, "30,000-Year-Old Wild Flax Fibers," *Science* 325(5946), 2009, 1359, DOI 10.1126/science.1175404; Bergfjord et al., Comment, *Science* 328(5986), 2010, 1634, DOI 10.1126/science.1186345; Kvavadze et al., Response, DOI 10.1126/science.1187161; Adovasio, Soffer and Klíma, *Antiquity* 70(269), 1996, 526 to 534. **[pass 1 and pass 2; the science.org pages are paywalled to automated fetching, so the DOIs are metadata-verified rather than full-text read]**

10. **Trap:** the needle was a woman's tool, or the seamstress was a woman. **Truth:** there is no direct Pleistocene evidence for who sewed. The strongest ethnographic material is modern and specific: Nomokonova, Losey, Gusev, Kohut, Razdymakha, Vozelova and Plekhanov, "The one-eyed Elder woman stitches an ornament: Needles, needle cases, and women from the Iamal-Nenets region of Arctic Siberia," *Journal of Anthropological Archaeology* 74, 2024, 101589, is about living Nenets people and their own relationship to needles and needle cases, not about the Palaeolithic. If the lesson wants a living Arctic sewing tradition in the close, cite it as living and contemporary, never as a survival. **[pass 1 for the citation; pass 2 could not open the publisher page, 403]**

11. **Trap:** Africa never had needles because African toolkits lagged. **Truth:** no eyed needles are reported from the Pleistocene Southern Hemisphere at all, and in Gilligan's own framework the reason is thermal: fitted, layered clothing is a cold-response technology, and where it is not needed it is not made. Africa holds the world's oldest hide-perforating bone awls (Blombos 73,000 to 70,000; Sibudu about 61,000; Border Cave about 44,000 cal B.P.) and the Contrebandiers skinning assemblage. A possible Later Stone Age sewing needle made from a tooth root has been argued on use-wear grounds at Little Muck Shelter in northern South Africa (*Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports*, 2023). The Smithsonian's own institutional framing runs the other way from the stereotype: awls and perforators were probably invented in Africa and carried to colder climates. **Sources:** Gilligan et al. 2024 **[pass 2]**; Smithsonian Human Origins Program, "Making Clothing" and "Bone and Ivory Needles" **[pass 2]**; the Little Muck specimen abstract page **[pass 2, abstract only: treat as "argued," name no authors]**.

12. **Trap (handle with extreme care or omit):** "Tasmanians lost the ability to make bone tools, which proves technology can go backwards." Gilligan (2007, *Archaeology in Oceania* 42(3), 102 to 111) argues the disappearance of Tasmanian bone tools reflects a shift in clothing strategy under changed conditions rather than loss of capacity, and the older "devolution" literature on Tasmania has a genuinely ugly history. If used at all, frame it as Gilligan's argument that changing what you wear is a rational adaptation, never as a story about a people losing skills. Safer to leave out entirely. **[pass 1]**

**UNVERIFIED across both passes, confirm or drop:** the Denisova needle's exact length and its bird-bone identification (press-level only); the claim that Denisova yielded further smaller needles in younger layers (press-level only); any published duration for experimentally drilling a needle eye (the experiments exist, using flint and quartzite flakes on cattle and goat bone blanks, but I found no published time, so print no hours); the Zhoukoudian Upper Cave chronology as a fixed figure.

## 6. Callback design

Five earlier nodes have written lesson files. All five were read for this brief. Only these are legal callback targets; the unwritten Era I peers listed in the packet (`first-of-our-kind`, `the-tended-fire`, `ochre-for-the-dead`, `ash-across-asia`, `the-other-humans`, `crossing-to-sahul`, `beads-and-alliances`, `the-hollow-bone`) must go in `threadsOut` or in prose, never in `callbackTo`.

1. **`spark-of-mind`** (strongest; use it). That lesson's central object is a symbol worn on a body: ochre lines drawn across a girl's forehead "that mean their people and no other people," and grey shell beads rubbed red that let a stranger be placed before a word is spoken. This lesson is the sequel with a physical problem attached. In a place cold enough to need continuous cover, the forehead is not available. Neither is scarification. The mark has to migrate onto the garment, which is exactly what Gilligan et al. argue the fine needles are for. Ask the reader to compare a painted face with a beaded sleeve and say what is the same (an arbitrary sign, agreed by a group, read by strangers) and what is new (the sign now lives on a manufactured surface, which can be taken off, handed on, and made more elaborate without limit). The answer should reach the conclusion that clothing became the largest and most legible symbol a human body can carry.

2. **`great-dispersal`** (strong, and uniquely valuable, because it lets this lesson correct the atlas). That lesson says twice, in its own words, that the dispersal ran on "watercraft and tailored clothing sewn with bone needles," including inside a callback answer. That is the standard shorthand, and this node is where it has to be complicated. The crossing to Sahul happened roughly 65,000 to 50,000 years ago; the oldest eyed needle anywhere is about 40,000 years old; there are no Pleistocene eyed needles in Australia; and the earliest American eyed needle postdates the White Sands footprints by seven to nine thousand years. So people had already reached a continent across open ocean, and had lived in cold country, before the object this lesson is about existed. Ask what the needle actually added, given that. The honest answer is: not entry, but comfort at the margin, layering, fineness, and continuous cover, plus a place to put the signs that used to go on skin. A callback that improves an earlier lesson is the most elegant move available here.

3. **`paint-in-the-dark`** (strong alternate, and never yet used as a callback target). Its most memorable argument is that art "arrives already fluent," so the apprenticeship must have happened in perishable things we cannot see. That is precisely this lesson's evidentiary situation, stated the other way round: the Yana kit is already a graded professional set on its first appearance in the record, which means the craft is far older than the first eye drilled through bone. Date arithmetic worth putting on the page: Chauvet's lions are about 36,000 years old and the earliest eyed needle about 40,000 cal B.P., roughly four thousand years apart, near neighbours on a chart that spans hundreds of thousands of years. There is also a physical link worth one sentence: the Lascaux eyed needle was found on the floor of a painted cave, its eye stained with ochre. Careful not to reuse that lesson's own closing move about fluency, since it already delivered it.

4. **`tribe-of-strangers`** (good alternate). Its thesis is that a bead is a token that vouches for a person nobody has met, and its plot turns on strangers becoming readable across a waterhole before dark. A decorated garment is that token scaled to the whole body and readable at a hundred paces instead of arm's length. Genuine comparison: hxaro beads are exchanged and carried; sewn decoration is fixed and worn, so it broadcasts continuously and cannot be quietly handed on. That difference is the beginning of the uniform. Use if another Era I lesson has already claimed `spark-of-mind`, but do not run it alongside `spark-of-mind`, since the two overlap.

5. **`firelight-economy`** (weakest for a question, good for one line of significance). The real contrast is sharp but narrow: the Kalahari camp exists to stop any individual's success from becoming visible standing, and insults a huge kill to that end; a Sunghir burial puts thousands of hours of ivory beadwork on a single body where everyone can count them. A fitted garment is also stored labour sized to one body, which cannot be divided into rings of shares the way an eland is divided. Both are societies deciding what a body is allowed to announce. Use as prose, not as a callback question, and if used, flag the property angle as a question rather than a claim, since no Palaeolithic evidence bears on who owned a coat.

**Recommended pair for the two callback questions: 1 and 2** (`spark-of-mind` and `great-dispersal`). Hold `paint-in-the-dark` in reserve as the freshest substitute if another Era I lesson claims either.

**Suggested `threadsOut` (three to five, fresh "why" text, not the edge labels):** `world-the-ice-made`, `into-the-last-continents`, `spark-of-mind`, and either `trap-of-seeds` (if the manufactured-environment idea is earned) or `the-other-humans` (backward, and it repays the incoming edge).

## 8. Node-data flags

Precise problems in the committed graph. Do not edit the file. Report them.

1. **Date field is off and mixes conventions.** `date: 'c. 40,000 BCE'` renders the literature's "about 40,000 cal B.P." roughly two thousand years too old. Codex may want `c. 38,000 BCE`, or may prefer to leave the map placement and let the lesson state the convention. Either way the lesson must not repeat "40,000 BCE" in prose.

2. **Hook asserts a capability the current authority denies.** "Threaded with sinew, it turns loose hides into a fitted, layered skin a body can wear into the cold" credits the eyed needle with making fitted clothing possible. Gilligan et al. 2024 state that fitted garments do not require eyed needles and were made with awls and burins first, and the Canyars punch board dates that practice to within the node's own century range. The word "layered" is defensible; "fitted" is exactly the part the 2024 paper takes away. Suggested repair: keep the object, move the claim to layering and fineness.

3. **Hook states an unattested material.** "Threaded with sinew" is an ethnographic inference presented as fact; the peer-reviewed phrasing is "sinew or fibre," and the only directly attested Pleistocene thread material is plant fibre (Dzudzuana, itself contested; Pavlov I impressions). Recommend "threaded" without a material, or "threaded with fibre or sinew."

4. **Summary's causal chain is stronger than the evidence.** "It let people live where bare skin freezes, opening the far north and eventually the route into the Americas." The far north is defensible (Yana at 71 degrees north with 192 needles). The Americas is not: the earliest American eyed needle is about 14,000 cal B.P., seven to nine thousand years after the atlas's own White Sands footprints, and people crossed to Sahul across open water tens of thousands of years before any eyed needle existed. Recommend a temporal statement rather than a causal one.

5. **Summary understates the louse uncertainty.** "Genetics of the clothing louse hint that garments are far older still" is fair as far as it goes, but the published 95 percent interval is 29,000 to 691,000 years and the two lice are now generally treated as ecotypes rather than species. The word "hint" is carrying a lot; the lesson should print the interval.

6. **Summary's "in Siberia and elsewhere" flattens the most interesting pattern in the data.** At forty thousand years ago the eyed needle appears in Siberia and effectively nowhere else; the Caucasus, East Asia, Europe and the Americas follow across the next twenty-six thousand years, in that order. Recommend "in Siberia first" if the summary is ever revised.

7. **Thread list is arguably incomplete.** The node carries `craft` only. If the lesson lands the 2024 argument, this is also an `ideas` node: the transfer of social signalling from the skin to the garment surface is a symbolic development, and the atlas's own `spark-of-mind` and `tribe-of-strangers` nodes are wired to exactly that idea. Worth a Codex decision on adding `ideas`; the forge spec requires two threads named in words regardless, so the lesson will say it either way.

8. **The `trap-of-seeds` edge is the weakest of the three.** "Sewing reshaped animal hides into gear the body needs; farming would later reshape whole landscapes to the same end" is an analogy of scale rather than a mechanism, and it competes with much stronger existing wires into that node. Either rewrite the "why" toward the manufactured-environment idea (humans changing the world instead of moving to a better one), or consider retargeting.

9. **Incoming edge phrasing invites a wrong reading.** `the-other-humans` says our lineage would extend Neanderthal cold-weather skill "once it learned to sew fitted clothing," which implies our sewing postdates theirs. The African awl record (Blombos, 73,000 to 70,000 years) predates the Châtelperronian awls by tens of thousands of years. Defensible if "sew fitted clothing" means the Upper Palaeolithic needle horizon specifically, but it will read as a lineage race. Recommend rewording.

10. **Region `world` is right, and the map placement will therefore not carry the geography.** Since the node has no regional meridian, the lesson's prose is the only place the reader learns that this technology's sequence runs Siberia, then the Caucasus, then East Asia, then Europe last. Treat that as a requirement rather than a flourish.

11. **Graph architecture observation.** All three forward edges leave the subject entirely (climate, migration, farming), and nothing later in the atlas wires back to perishable craft, so a reader following the graph forward never meets the textile, cordage or fibre record again. If a later node ever covers weaving, wool or flax, an edge from here would be earned. Not a defect in this lesson.
