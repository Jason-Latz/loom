**`paint-in-the-dark` (written; this node's `echoes` edge) already owns:**

- The Sulawesi argument, in full, including the 2024 redating to 51,200 years, Leang Tedongnge's 45,500-year pig, and the uranium-series caveat. Its recall question is *literally* "what discovery broke the Europe-first story."
- "Art arrives already fluent."
- The explicit ideas-plus-craft thread pairing, in almost those words.
- The closing move: cathedral, temple, museum, cinema, "we still go down into the dark."

**`tribe-of-strangers` (written) already owns:**
- The all-night music-and-dance scene: interlocking clapping "that no single pair of hands could make alone," cocoon rattles, the trance healer, "there is only the fire and everyone around it, breathing together."
- Durkheim and collective effervescence, defined and named, landing on "rhythm shared is trust forged, cheaply and fast."
- Soldiers drilling in step, congregations singing as one, stadiums chanting.
- Dunbar's number and the ceiling on face-to-face cooperation.

**Therefore:** do NOT build this lesson's spine on "music bonds a group." That argument is spent, and spent well. Do NOT re-run Sulawesi as the anti-teleology move. Do NOT narrate the Lion Man. Do NOT close on sacred rooms.

**What is left is better, and completely unclaimed: the instrument as a specification.** A voice is anatomy; nobody designs it. A flute is a designed object with a set of pitches built into it, made to a plan, teachable to a stranger, and, crucially, capable of being *wrong*. Somebody at Hohle Fels scratched measuring lines on a vulture bone before cutting a single hole. Thirty-three thousand years later, on a different continent, a flute maker at Jiahu drilled hole seven, listened, decided it was too high, and re-drilled it 4.4 millimetres lower. Both acts are physically preserved. That convergence, in method rather than in date, is this lesson's anti-Euro-teleology argument, and it is stronger than a date race because it is about what people *did* rather than who got there first.

The line the lesson should be built to earn: **a flute is the oldest surviving object that could be out of tune.** Which means somebody held a standard that lived outside any single body.

## 2. Preferred scene

**Hohle Fels, in the Ach valley twenty kilometres west of Ulm: a maker scores four faint lines along a griffon vulture's wing bone, then cuts the first hole where a line says to.**

Everything load-bearing here is in Conard, Malina and Münzel 2009, which I read in full.

### Documentation ledger

**Attested by excavation:**
- The bone is the **radius of a griffon vulture (*Gyps fulvus*)**, a bird with a wing span of **230 to 265 cm**. The paper states that this species "provides bones ideal for large flutes" and that griffon vultures and other vultures are documented in the Upper Palaeolithic sediments of the Swabian caves, with examples identified from Gravettian and Aurignacian deposits at Geissenklösterle.
- An unmodified radius of that species runs **roughly 34 cm**. The preserved portion of the flute is **21.8 cm long and about 8 mm in diameter**.
- **Five finger holes.** The maker carved **two deep V-shaped notches into one end**, presumably the proximal end, into which the musician blew. The other end is broken **through the middle of the most distal of the five holes**, with several centimetres missing.
- **"As many as four very fine lines were incised near the finger holes. These precisely carved markings probably reflect measurements used to indicate where the finger holes were to be carved using chipped-stone tools. Only the partly preserved, and most distal, of the five finger holes lacks such markings."** This is the sentence the whole lesson should be built on.
- Recovered **17 September 2008**, in **12 pieces**: 11 documented in situ, 1 found during water screening. The fragments were spread over **3 cm vertically and an area of about 10 by 20 cm**, in **archaeological horizon Vb**, the basal Aurignacian, inside an 8-cm-thick clayey silt with limestone clasts that directly overlies a nearly sterile red-brown silty clay.
- Find density in horizon Vb is moderately high: abundant flint-knapping debris, worked bone and ivory, and bones of **horse, reindeer, cave bear, mammoth and ibex**, plus burnt bone.
- Same horizon, about **70 cm away**: the **Venus of Hohle Fels**, a mammoth-ivory female figurine (Conard, *Nature* 459, 2009, 248 to 252). Tübingen's excavation page gives its height as 5.97 cm. Conard et al. call the proximity **"a possible contextual link,"** nothing stronger.
- Also from the basal Aurignacian at Hohle Fels: two single fragments of **ivory** flutes. Flute 2, from feature 10 at the base of horizon Va, measures **11.7 by 4.2 by 1.7 mm**. Flute 3, from the lowest Aurignacian unit of horizon Vb, measures **21.1 by 7.6 by 2.5 mm**. Both were recovered by water screening. Think about that: an 11.7-millimetre sliver, identified as part of an instrument.
- **The cave.** The main hall has about **500 m² of floor** under a **30-metre dome**, "the size of a cathedral," one of the largest chambers in southern Germany (Geopark Schwäbische Alb, verified).

**Reconstruction, and `storyContext` must say so as plainly as `spark-of-mind` does:** any person, any name, any age or gender, any dialogue, the season, the weather, who was listening, what was played, whether the maker was also the player, how the vulture was obtained, and whether anyone cared about the room's acoustics. **There is no named person available for this node and there never will be.** There is no writing, and, per trap 4, there is not even a securely associated human fossil.

**Quantities to check against the paper before printing:** 21.8 cm, 8 mm, 34 cm, 230 to 265 cm, five holes, two V-notches, twelve pieces, four incised lines, 70 cm to the Venus, 11.7 mm and 21.1 mm for the ivory slivers, 500 m² and 30 m for the hall. Do not print a note count, a scale name, or a hole spacing in millimetres for the Hohle Fels flute; see traps 2 and 3.

**Why this scene.** A story about someone blowing a bone is a story about a noise. A story about someone **marking a bone before cutting it** is a story about a mind holding a design, and the marks are physically on the object. It also produces a clean cold open: the reader watches a tool being made and does not learn until the holes go in that this tool cannot cut, carry, warm, or feed anyone.

### Alternate 1 (recommended as the story's second movement, not the opening)

**The ivory flute.** Four of the eight Swabian instruments are mammoth ivory, which is solid. Conard et al. give the sequence: rough out the shape along the long axis of a naturally curved piece of tusk; **split it open at the interface of the cementum and dentine**, or along another bedding plane in the ivory; hollow out both halves; carve the holes; then **rejoin the halves with air-tight seals along the seams**. The Geissenklösterle ivory flute "preserves dozens of finely carved notches along the edges of the two halves to facilitate binding and sealing the flute." It was recovered in **31 small pieces**. The point that lands with no help from the writer: hollow bird bones were lying around, and somebody built a tube out of a solid tusk anyway. Nobody takes that road unless they want a sound a bird bone will not give.

### Alternate 2 (the anti-teleology opening, if the writer wants to start outside Europe)

**Jiahu, Wuyang county, Henan, phase 2, between 6600 and 6200 BCE: a flute maker drills hole seven, listens, and drills it again 4.4 millimetres lower.** From Zhang, Xiao and Lee, *Antiquity* 78 (2004), 769 to 778, read in full. On pipe **M282:20** the maker left drill markings, circular impressions visible at the lower right of holes 2, 3 and 4. A small hole 7 was drilled about **4.4 mm above** the real hole 7. The authors read the sequence: the maker marked out all the hole positions, bored a small hole 7 first, found it produced too high a pitch, and bored the real hole 7 **4.4 mm below it, toward the end of the flute**, to correct it, then shifted holes 2, 3 and 4 about **1.5 mm** from their initial markings. On flute **M78:1** the markings for the fifth, sixth and seventh holes had been **adjusted three times**. Liability: it is thirty-three millennia from the node's date and belongs, if anywhere, to a later East Asian node. Strength: it is the same act as the incised lines at Hohle Fels, performed by people with no possible connection, and it makes the lesson's argument about method rather than priority. **My recommendation: use it as the third movement or in significance, not as the cold open.**

### Scene to AVOID

**The firelit gathering where everyone sings and dances and feels as one.** `tribe-of-strangers` has already written that scene at length, with clapping, rattles, trance and Durkheim, and writing it again here is the most avoidable failure available in this whole node.

Also avoid, with reasons:
- **A hunter imitating a birdcall and "discovering" music.** Pure invention, and it makes music an accident, which is the precise opposite of what the incised layout lines show.
- **The shaman playing to summon spirits.** No evidence at this site and date; it is a Lewis-Williams style reading that `paint-in-the-dark` has already gestured at; and Hohle Fels has no Aurignacian wall paintings, so the imagery the reader will picture belongs to a different cave and a different lesson.
- **A vulture hunt.** Nothing in the record says how the bird was obtained, and griffon vultures are scavengers.
- **Flute and Venus in a single ceremony.** They were 70 cm apart in one horizon. That is stratigraphy, not liturgy. See trap 11.

## 3. Story movement

Six paragraphs, one workshop, one governing image, one late widening.

**Governing image: the hollow.** Every other tool in this atlas so far is made by adding, an edge, a point, a barb. A flute is made by removing, and what the maker is actually shaping is a column of air, a thing with no substance at all. Let the image turn twice: once at the pivot, once at the very end, changed. A song is also a hollow: a shape made of nothing that exists only while someone is breathing through it, which is exactly why we have the machine and will never have the music.

1. **The layout.** Cold open on the bone in someone's hands: scraped, cleaned, a metre-and-a-half bird's forearm. The stone flake. The four faint lines going on before anything is cut. Withhold what the object is. Let the reader assume a tool.
2. **The cutting.** The first hole, then the rest. The moment the reader realises this will never cut, carry, warm, or feed anyone. Give the cost in daylight: a winter afternoon in a cave mouth on the mammoth steppe, spent on this.
3. **The listening and the correcting.** Whoever made this had an opinion about what it should sound like before it existed. An opinion implies a standard; a standard implies a teacher; a teacher implies this is not the first one. (Keep this speculative in register but note that the layout marks are the physical warrant.)
4. **The harder road.** Widen one valley over. Somebody is splitting a mammoth tusk lengthwise, hollowing both halves, notching both edges so the seam will hold air, to build by hand a tube that a bird's wing gives away for free. The absurdity first, then the reason: tone.
5. **The room.** The hall, 500 square metres under a 30-metre dome, and the note going up into it. *Flag:* no acoustic study of Hohle Fels was found in this pass. Treat reverberation as physics and inference, or leave the room silent and let the reader supply it. Do NOT write that the cave was chosen for its sound.
6. **The pivot.** End on the arithmetic, not the moral. A day of work, a teacher, a standard, an object that improves nobody's odds of surviving the winter, and a species that has done this in every place it has ever lived. So what is a flute *for*?

### Words and framings to refuse

- Never **"mysterious," "haunting," "eerie," "primal," "primitive," "cavemen," "dawn of civilization," "the first artist."** The first four are the register that converts Ice Age people into atmosphere.
- Never **"music began here"** or **"the birth of music."** The flute is the birth of *evidence*, and collapsing the two is the central error this lesson exists to correct.
- Never **"more sophisticated than we thought."** Nobody who works on this thought otherwise.
- Never stage the Swabian Jura as the cradle from which culture radiated. If the **Kulturpumpe** model comes up, name it as a named hypothesis. Higham et al.'s 2012 abstract says only that their new dates **"fail to refute"** it, which is a beautifully careful phrase and worth borrowing.
- Never let the flute score a point against Neanderthals. See trap 12.
- Do not use the word **"drilled"** of the Hohle Fels holes; the paper says carved, with chipped-stone tools. (Drilling is correct for Jiahu, where the paper says drill.)

## 4. Significance spine

Five movements. **Thread obligation: the lesson must tie explicitly, in words, to `craft` and `ideas`.** Craft is the layout marks, the ivory seams, and the transmission of technique. Ideas is the standard: a notion of "right" held outside any one body. `power` may be touched in a single clause at the `gods-of-the-city` handoff and must not be claimed as this node's pigment.

### 1. The evidence, and why it is where it is

Eight instruments, three caves, two neighbouring valleys. Conard et al.'s Table 1, verbatim in structure: **Geissenklösterle** flute 1, horizon II, Upper Aurignacian, swan radius, 23 pieces, excavated 1990; flute 2, horizon II, bird bone of swan size, 7 pieces, 1973; flute 3, horizon II, mammoth ivory, 31 pieces, 1974 to 1979. **Hohle Fels** flute 1, horizon Vb, basal Aurignacian, griffon vulture radius, 12 pieces, 2008; flute 2, horizon Va feature 10, mammoth ivory, 1 piece, 2008; flute 3, horizon Vb, mammoth ivory, 1 piece, 2008. **Vogelherd** (Lone valley, 25 km northwest of Ulm) flute 1, bird bone, 3 pieces, 2005; flute 2, mammoth ivory, 1 piece, 2008. **Four of bone and four of ivory.**

Then the sentence that makes the node honest, and it is the excavators' own. Hohle Fels, Geissenklösterle and Vogelherd are, in their words, "the only Palaeolithic cave sites in the region where systematic water screening of all archaeological deposits has been conducted. The other Aurignacian sites in the region were excavated before the 1970s without employing excavation methods appropriate for locating small, highly fragmented finds." Half the corpus consists of single fragments a centimetre or two long, recovered from a screen. **The oldest instruments in the world are where they are partly because that is where somebody built a sieve.** Say it plainly. It is the most quietly devastating fact in the brief and it costs the lesson nothing.

Conard et al. also state that apart from the Swabian caves "there is no convincing evidence for musical instruments pre-dating 30 kyr ago," that one of the 22 Upper Palaeolithic bird-bone flutes from **Isturitz** in the French Pyrenees could be Aurignacian but came from poorly documented early-twentieth-century excavation, and that the only instrument of roughly comparable age from an open-air site is a bone flute from **Grubgraben** in the Wachau, Lower Austria, at about 19 kyr. Use these to make the point about sampling, not about priority.

### 2. The mechanism: why this is craft, not luck

Three legs, all attested.

**(a) The layout marks.** Measured before cut. That is a plan.

**(b) The ivory flutes.** A hollow object built out of a solid material by splitting at the cementum-dentine interface, hollowing both halves, carving the holes, and rejoining with airtight seams; the Geissenklösterle example preserves dozens of tiny notches along both edges for binding and sealing. This is only rational if the maker wanted a specific sound that available bird bones could not produce. Conard et al. note, without fuss, that hundreds of ivory artefacts and thousands of pieces of ivory-working debris are known from these deposits and only the flute fragments have the hollowed convex morphology, the finger holes, and the edge notches. So the identification is not a stretch, and the technique was a specialty.

**(c) Repetition.** Eight instruments, three sites, more than one archaeological horizon. That is a **tradition**, which means teaching, which means the technique outlived the people who invented it. Name it as the atlas's ratchet running on sound, and then let it go; `spark-of-mind` owns the ratchet and this lesson only needs one sentence of it.

Name `craft` here in words.

### 3. The mechanism that is actually new: pitch put outside the body

This is the movement no other lesson can write, and it should be the longest.

Everything humans use to make music with the body is unspecified. A voice has a range but no fixed notes; two people clapping are not tuned to anything; the pitch of a shout is not a decision anyone made once and can hand on. **A flute with cut holes is a decision made once and made permanent.** Its notes are in the object. It can be copied. It can be compared. It can be *wrong*.

The Jiahu evidence, thirty-three millennia later and eight thousand kilometres away, shows what that means in practice, and it is worth spending real space on because it is the only place in the record where we can watch the correcting happen. From Zhang, Xiao and Lee 2004:

- More than thirty bone flutes have been recovered from burials at Jiahu, a Peiligang culture site in Wuyang, in the middle Huai river basin. All are made from the **ulnae of the red-crowned crane, *Grus japonensis***. Nineteen radiocarbon dates bracket phase 1 at about **7000 to 6600 BC**, phase 2 at **6600 to 6200 BC**, phase 3 at **6200 to 5800 BC**.
- The correcting: the drill markings on **M282:20**, the small hole 7 drilled 4.4 mm above the real one, the 1.5 mm shifts to holes 2, 3 and 4, and the markings on **M78:1** adjusted three times.
- The standardising, which is the real prize: flutes **M282:20 and M282:21** came from the same tomb. One is 23.6 cm long, the other 22.7 cm, made from crane ulnae of different sizes. Both have their **keynote on the fourth hole**, at D6 minus 30 cents and D6 minus 32 cents respectively: **a difference of two cents, one fiftieth of a semitone, undetectable to the human ear.** The authors read M282:21 as the earlier instrument and M282:20 as a deliberate replacement built to match it. Two bones of different lengths, tuned to each other, nine thousand years ago.
- And the object that ends the argument: at **Zhongshanzhai**, in Ruzhou, Henan, 130 km northwest of Jiahu, in a stratum from the first half of the sixth millennium BC, excavators found a bird ulna 15.6 cm long with **ten holes in two parallel rows, spaced about 100 cents apart, so close together that there is no room for fingering.** It cannot be played. It is a **tuning instrument**. A tuning fork, in bone, from the sixth millennium BC.

The Jiahu paper also delivers, from the other side of the world and in a peer-reviewed venue, the caution this lesson needs about its own object: "For the Palaeolithic bone flute that has been studied from a musical perspective, the test results are inconsistent in that the pitch and scale cannot be determined." (See trap 2.)

Land the movement on `ideas`: a standard is an idea that survives outside a skull and outside a body, and a tuner is an idea about an idea.

### 4. The named live debate, with both sides and no verdict

**Recommended: the 2021 *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* pair.** This is unusually clean, because the journal received two rival target articles on the origin of music, reviewed them in parallel and independently, accepted both, and published them side by side with open peer commentary.

- **Savage, Loui, Tarr, Schachner, Glowacki, Mithen and Fitch, "Music as a coevolved system for social bonding"** (*BBS* 44, e59): musicality is an adaptation, and social bonding is the overarching function that unifies the rival accounts; music let humans bond at scales grooming could never reach.
- **Mehr, Krasnow, Bryant and Hagen, "Origins of music in credible signaling"** (*BBS* 44, e60): music is honest signalling, of coalition strength between groups and of parental attention to infants, and the bonding account is wrong.

Give both. Give no verdict. **Handle with care:** the bonding side is what `tribe-of-strangers` already argued, so present it as the position under attack rather than as the lesson's thesis, and give the credible-signalling side the fresher framing (a lullaby as a costly, hard-to-fake signal that a parent is present and attending; group song as an audible advertisement of how many people can act as one). Mention Pinker's "auditory cheesecake," music as a byproduct pleasure, only as the position both 2021 papers were written against, not as a live third option.

The mechanistic evidence the bonding camp leans on is worth one sentence: **Tarr, Launay and Dunbar** (*Frontiers in Psychology* 5, 1096, 2014) review two proposed routes, self-other merging through interpersonal synchrony, and endorphin release during exertive rhythmic activity. **Flagged: I did not open this paper's full text or a DOI record.** Confirm before naming it.

**Secondary debate, if a second is wanted or the first is claimed elsewhere: Divje Babe.** See trap 1. It has the advantage of being about the object itself.

### 5. The honest uncertainty, stated in three registers

Say all three out loud and then say what survives them.

- **Date.** See trap 8. The published anchors are "about 40,000 calendar years ago" for the Hohle Fels basal Aurignacian, a floor of "pre-date 35,000 calendar years ago" for the flutes, and 42,000 to 43,000 years for Geissenklösterle after the 2012 ultrafiltration redating. These are not the same claim.
- **Sound.** The distal end is broken through the fifth hole with several centimetres missing, so the original length, and therefore the tuning, is unknown. Potengowski et al. (2023) built **eight** reconstructions of the Geissenklösterle ivory flute at different lengths precisely because, in their words, "due to its incompleteness, this Palaeolithic instrument allows for variability in reconstructing."
- **Maker.** No diagnostic human bone from the Swabian Aurignacian. The attribution to *Homo sapiens* is an inference, and the excavators say so.

Then the turn: none of the three touches the load-bearing claim. Whoever they were, whenever exactly it was, and whatever it sounded like, somebody measured before they cut. That is the finding.

### 6. Landing: why the modern world still carries this node

Do not close on cathedrals, museums or cinemas; `paint-in-the-dark` owns that ending. Close on the **standard**.

The arithmetic to put on the page: the Blombos paint kits are about 100,000 years old and this flute about 40,000, so roughly **sixty thousand years** of symbolic behaviour precede it, and none of it is European. Mehr et al. (*Science*, 2019) coded the ethnographic record for 315 societies and a 60-society representative sample and found song in **all** of them, with 97.8 percent of songs carrying a tonal centre an expert listener could identify. Song is not a European invention or a Eurasian one; it is a species-level fact, and the flutes are simply the earliest instance that happened to be made of something a cave floor would keep.

Then the three different kinds of first, on three continents, none of them a rung on a ladder:
- **Europe** holds the oldest surviving instrument, largely because of where the sieves were.
- **China** holds the oldest instruments anyone can still play, and the oldest evidence that people tuned two objects to each other and built a device whose only purpose was to define the pitch.
- **The Levant** holds the oldest instrument we can say what it was *for*: seven perforated waterfowl wing bones from Eynan-Mallaha in northern Israel, about 12,000 years old, cut to produce sounds close to the calls of kestrel and sparrowhawk (Davin et al., *Scientific Reports* 13, 8709, 2023). Raptor talons are disproportionately common in Natufian bird-bone assemblages, so the makers may have been calling the birds, or calling *to* them.

End on the sentence the whole lesson exists to earn. Every tuning fork, every A440 broadcast, every metre bar in a vault, every calibration standard on Earth is a descendant of the moment somebody decided that a hole belonged *there* and not a finger's width along, and scratched a line to remember it by. We got the machine. We will never get the music. And the reason we can be sure there was music is that nobody builds a machine this careful for a sound they do not already know how to want.

## 5. Factual traps

Twelve. This is the highest-value section of the brief.

**1. Trap:** "The world's oldest musical instrument."
**Truth:** the oldest *widely accepted* one. The rival claim is the **Divje Babe I** bone from Slovenia, a perforated juvenile cave bear femur from a Mousterian layer, which the **National Museum of Slovenia** dates to 60,000 to 50,000 years, describes as having four pierced holes (two complete, two partial), attributes to a Neanderthal, and calls the oldest musical instrument in the world, stating that analyses "proved the impossibility of the holes being attributed to animal bites." Against it: **d'Errico, Villa, Pinto Llona and Ruiz Idarraga**, *Antiquity* 72(275), 1998, 65 to 79, arguing carnivore damage; and **Diedrich**, *Royal Society Open Science* 2, 140022, 2015, arguing spotted hyena premolar punctures on cave bear cub femora and redating the layer to the Aurignacian. For it: **Turk, Turk and Otte**, *Applied Sciences* 10(4), 1226, 2020, defending the artificial origin. **Write "the oldest instruments that are not disputed,"** and give the Slovenian case two honest sentences rather than a dismissal. The fact that a national museum's own label takes a side in a live dispute is itself worth a clause.
**Sources:** all four verified; URLs in section 7.

**2. Trap:** "The Hohle Fels flute plays a pentatonic scale," or "you can play the Star-Spangled Banner on it."
**Truth:** those are properties of a modern replica, not of the artifact. Conard, Malina and Münzel wrote in the discovery paper itself: **"We have not yet been able to produce a replica of the flute, but it is possible that the flute was played by blowing directly into the proximal end without using a mouthpiece."** The playing technique was undetermined at publication. The distal end is broken through the fifth hole with several centimetres gone, and length sets pitch, so the original tuning is unrecoverable. Potengowski et al. (2023) built eight reconstructions of the Geissenklösterle ivory flute at different lengths because incompleteness "allows for variability in reconstructing." And Zhang, Xiao and Lee (2004), writing from outside the debate, state flatly: "For the Palaeolithic bone flute that has been studied from a musical perspective, the test results are inconsistent in that the pitch and scale cannot be determined." **Never print a named scale for the Hohle Fels flute as fact.** If the replica is mentioned, attribute it to the reconstruction and say what is unknown. The pentatonic-scale claim is the single most likely thing for a writer to reach for here, and it is the single most likely thing to fail review.

**3. Trap:** "The flute produces four notes plus three overtones."
**Truth:** that figure belongs to the **three-holed swan-radius flute from Geissenklösterle**, not to the Hohle Fels vulture flute. Conard et al.: the Geissenklösterle bone flute "can be played by blowing obliquely into its proximal end to produce four basic notes. Three additional overtones can be produced by blowing more sharply." Of the Hohle Fels flute they say only that they *expect* a comparable or perhaps greater range, and that its larger bore would have made its tone **deeper** than the Geissenklösterle bone flute's and closer to that of a reconstruction of the Geissenklösterle ivory flute. Keep the instruments apart.

**4. Trap:** "*Homo sapiens* made it."
**Truth:** an assumption, and the paper says so in as many words: **"No diagnostic human bones have been found in deposits of the Swabian Aurignacian, but we assume that modern humans produced the artefacts from the basal Aurignacian deposits shortly after their arrival in the region, following a migration up the Danube Corridor."** I searched for a later study closing this gap for the Swabian Aurignacian specifically and found none. Write "the Aurignacian people of these valleys," or state the inference as an inference. This matters more than it looks, because the Divje Babe dispute is exactly about whether an instrument can be attributed to a species.

**5. Trap:** "Archaeologists found an intact flute."
**Truth:** twelve pieces, eleven in situ and one out of the water screen, over 3 cm of depth and a patch about 10 by 20 cm, with the distal end missing. The Geissenklösterle ivory flute came out in **31** pieces, Geissenklösterle flute 1 in **23**. Conard et al.'s own phrase for the Hohle Fels flute is "by far the most complete of the musical instruments so far recovered from the caves of Swabia." Use **"most complete."**

**6. Trap:** "Somebody found a hollow bone and blew through it."
**Truth:** half the Swabian corpus is mammoth ivory, which is solid, and required splitting at the cementum-dentine interface, hollowing both halves, carving the holes, and rejoining with airtight seals, with dozens of tiny notches cut along both edges of the Geissenklösterle example to bind and seal the seam. Add the four incised layout lines on the vulture flute. This is the trap most worth inverting into the lesson's argument rather than merely avoiding.

**7. Trap:** "Music began in Ice Age Europe" / "the oldest evidence of human music."
**Truth:** these are the oldest surviving **instruments**, which is a different sentence. The voice leaves nothing. Hands, hide drums, wooden pipes, struck stones, rattles and shaken seed pods leave nothing. Zhang, Xiao and Lee found turtle shells containing small stones in the Jiahu burials, read as rattles for dancing, and could not resist speculating "that there might have been other instruments made of perishable material" that left no trace at all. Conard et al. supply the deflationary explanation for Swabia's apparent uniqueness themselves (see spine movement 1). **The lesson's anti-teleology argument should be the sieve and the Jiahu convergence, NOT Sulawesi, which `paint-in-the-dark` has already spent.**

**8. Trap:** "The flutes are 42,000 years old, so they date to about 42,000 BCE."
**Truth:** an arithmetic error of about two thousand years, and the node's own data files make it (see flag 1): 42,000 years old is roughly 40,000 BCE. Beyond the arithmetic, the published anchors are:
- Conard, Malina and Münzel 2009: ten AMS radiocarbon dates from horizon Va feature 10 and horizon Vb fall **between 31 and 40 kyr** (uncalibrated); available calibrations put dates of about 32 kyr at roughly 36 kyr calibrated; **"Thus, we can be certain that the flutes from Hohle Fels pre-date 35,000 calendar years ago."** The same paper places the initial Upper Palaeolithic settlement of the region, and hence these flutes, at **"about 40,000 calendar years ago."**
- Higham, Basell, Jacobi, Wood, Bronk Ramsey and Conard, *Journal of Human Evolution* 62(6), 2012, 664 to 676: redated Geissenklösterle with an ultrafiltration protocol; the Early Aurignacian levels come out earlier than dated sites in southern France and Italy but **not as early** as claims of a pre-Aurignacian modern human dispersal to Italy by about 45,000 cal BP; the dates "fail to refute" the Kulturpumpe model. The widely repeated **42,000 to 43,000** figure comes from the press coverage of this paper, not from any page I was able to open.
**Safest prose:** call the Hohle Fels flute **about forty thousand years old**. If 42,000 to 43,000 is used at all, attach it to **Geissenklösterle** and to the 2012 redating.
**PARTLY UNVERIFIED, and Fable should resolve or avoid it:** Conard et al.'s Table 1 places all three Geissenklösterle flutes in archaeological horizon **II**, labelled Upper Aurignacian, while Higham et al.'s headline range describes the **beginning** of the Aurignacian at that site, i.e. the levels below. I could not open the Higham 2012 full text (ScienceDirect 403, PubMed captcha, Oxford press release 403) to confirm whether horizon II was itself redated into that range. **Do not attach 42,000 to 43,000 to the flutes specifically without checking the paper.**

**9. Trap:** "The vulture flute is the oldest of the Swabian instruments."
**Truth:** it is the **most complete**. The three Hohle Fels flutes come from the basal Aurignacian, and Conard et al. argue on stratigraphic grounds that they predate the Geissenklösterle instruments; but the 2012 redating of Geissenklösterle complicates any simple ranking (see trap 8). Use the excavators' own word and avoid the race.

**10. Trap:** "A hunter brought down a griffon vulture for its wing bone."
**Truth:** nothing in the record says how the bird was obtained. The paper says only that the species "provides bones ideal for large flutes" and that vultures are documented in the Upper Palaeolithic sediments of these caves. Griffon vultures are scavengers with a 230 to 265 cm wing span. Do not stage a hunt; if the bird's death is needed for the scene, leave it unexplained, which is also what the evidence does.

**11. Trap:** "The flute and the Venus were found together in a ritual deposit."
**Truth:** the Venus of Hohle Fels lay about **70 cm** from flute 1 in the same archaeological horizon Vb, and Conard et al. describe this as **"a possible contextual link between these two finds."** Proximity within one horizon is not a ceremony, and a horizon can represent a great deal of time. State the distance and let the reader do the wanting.

**12. Trap:** "Neanderthals had no music, and that is what made us different."
**Truth:** unresolved, and the flutes cannot resolve it, because a record that preserves almost nothing about sound cannot demonstrate an absence. Note honestly that Conard et al.'s own closing paragraph leans this way: early Upper Palaeolithic music, they suggest, "could have contributed to the maintenance of larger social networks, and thereby perhaps have helped facilitate the demographic and territorial expansion of modern humans relative to culturally and demographically more isolated Neanderthal populations." Present that as their hypothesis, in their hedged terms, set the Divje Babe dispute beside it, and move on. `the-other-humans` is an unwritten peer in this era and will not thank a lesson that quietly writes Neanderthals out of the story.

**Bonus trap worth one line if space allows:** "The Swabian Jura was the cradle where art and music were invented and spread from Europe." That is the **Kulturpumpe** model, a named hypothesis associated with Conard, which Higham et al. 2012 say only that their dates "fail to refute." Name it as a model or leave it out.

## 6. Callback design

Four legal earlier targets. All four have written lesson files and I read all four.

### 1. `tribe-of-strangers` (RECOMMENDED, and it must be a contrast, not an echo)

That lesson's fourth story paragraph is the all-night dance, and its third significance paragraph is Durkheim: "Synchrony is a technology for manufacturing belonging faster than friendship ever could." **The comparison is obvious and therefore worthless on its own.** The genuine question is the one that lesson could not ask: *the dance needed no object at all.* Clapping, voices, feet on sand and rattles tied at the ankle. Most of the world's musical traditions, then and now, need no manufactured instrument. So why would people who already had the bonding technology spend a winter afternoon and a rare skill building a machine for it?

Good answers for the model to teach: an instrument produces a pitch that no body can produce; it carries further than a voice and through the noise of a crowd; and, above all, **it fixes the notes in an object, so a tune can be handed to someone who never heard it played.** The dance dies with the dancers. The flute is a score in the shape of a bone. That is a real advance and it is this lesson's own.

### 2. `spark-of-mind` (RECOMMENDED)

That lesson's thesis is that a tool works on the world and a symbol works on other minds, and that "creatures that maintain symbol systems in stone and shell almost certainly maintained them in sound." **The flute is that inference cashed in, and it is also the inference's limit.** The comparison: a note, like a bead, means nothing physically and everything socially. The contrast, and it is the better half: **at Blombos the symbol itself survives; at Hohle Fels only the machine survives and the symbol is gone forever.** A bead is the meaning. A flute is the apparatus. We can hold the apparatus and will never hear the meaning, which is a genuinely different epistemic situation from anything the reader has met in this atlas so far.

Put the arithmetic in the answer: the paint kits are about 100,000 years old and this flute about 40,000, so roughly **sixty thousand years** separate them. The flute is not a European beginning; it is a very late European survival of something already ancient, and the only reason it looks like a beginning is that sound does not fossilise.

### 3. `firelight-economy` (strong alternate, and the most interesting friction in the set)

That lesson's machinery exists to stop any individual from converting a skill into standing: insulting the meat, arrow ownership, Boehm's reverse dominance hierarchy. A flute is a specialist's object, and a specialist's object made by a person everyone can *hear* is precisely what the levelling machine is built to police. What does a band do with someone who is unmistakably, audibly the best at something? The honest answer is that we do not know, which makes this a superb **`why`** question and a slightly risky **`callback`**, since callback answers are supposed to teach rather than shrug. **Use it if `tribe-of-strangers` has been claimed by another Era I lesson**, and if used, ground the answer in what the archaeology does support: that the technique was transmitted across sites and horizons, which means the skill was shared rather than hoarded.

### 4. `great-dispersal` (weakest of the four; spend it in `threadsOut` instead)

The real link is cargo: songs weigh nothing and crossed to Sahul in the same bodies that carried the boat knowledge, and Wara teaching the boy to read a low-flying bird is the same act as teaching someone where the fourth hole goes. But that lesson has already made the cumulative-culture point twice and a callback here would restate it. Better as a thread with a fresh why.

**Recommended pair for the two callback questions: 1 and 2.** Hold 3 in reserve.

**Suggested `threadsOut` (four; the node has only two forward edges, so backward threads are required):**
- `paint-in-the-dark` — meaning made audible beside meaning made visible, in the same caves, but only one of the two mediums leaves a record.
- `gods-of-the-city` — a temple needs not only a crowd but a sound it can repeat on schedule, and a made instrument is what makes a sound repeatable.
- `spark-of-mind` — the bead is the symbol and survives; the flute is only the apparatus and the symbol is gone, which is the difference between owning a poem and owning a printing press with the type melted down.
- `tribe-of-strangers` — the Kalahari dance needed no object; this is what changes when the pitch stops living in bodies and starts living in a thing that can be handed to a stranger.
- (Fifth, if wanted: `great-dispersal`, on weightless cargo.)

## 8. Node-data flags

Precise problems in the committed graph. **Do NOT edit any repo file. Reported only.**

**1. The date field and the summary contradict each other, and both are probably too old.** `date: 'c. 42,000 BCE'` is roughly 44,000 years before present. The summary says the flutes are "about 42,000 years old," which is roughly 40,000 BCE. They cannot both be right. And the literature supports neither cleanly: Conard et al. 2009 put the Hohle Fels basal Aurignacian at "about 40,000 calendar years ago" with a hard floor of "pre-date 35,000 calendar years," and the 42,000-to-43,000 figure belongs to Geissenklösterle after Higham et al. 2012 (and, per trap 8, may belong to the horizons *below* the flute-bearing one). **Recommend `c. 40,000 BCE` for the date field**, or at minimum reconciling the two. Secondary consequence worth noting: at c. 42,000 BCE this node sits two thousand years further from `paint-in-the-dark` (c. 40,000 BCE) than the evidence warrants, and the `echoes` edge between them reads better if they are near-contemporaries, which on the published dates they are.

**2. "Cut to a playable scale" in the summary is not supported and is the node's most likely review failure.** The distal end is broken through the fifth finger hole with several centimetres missing, so the original length and therefore the tuning are unrecoverable; the excavators had produced no replica at publication and were not certain how the instrument was blown. This phrase is the pentatonic claim in compressed form. **Recommend** replacing it with something attested that makes the same rhetorical point more strongly, e.g. "Cut with measured, deliberately placed holes," which is warranted by the four incised layout lines.

**3. The hook's verb "drills" is probably wrong.** Conard et al. describe finger holes **carved with chipped-stone tools**, guided by incised measuring lines. Drilling is a specific technique, and it is the technique at issue in the Divje Babe dispute, so using it here imports the wrong controversy's vocabulary. **Recommend "cuts" or "carves."**

**4. The hook's "and blows. A tune carries" over-commits.** The playing technique is explicitly uncertain in the primary report. Acceptable at the hook's register, but the lesson must not inherit the confidence.

**5. "A hollow vulture bone" should be a wing bone specifically.** The radius. Minor, but "the wing bone of a griffon vulture" is both more accurate and more vivid, and the 230-to-265 cm wing span is sitting right there unused.

**6. The summary's "Sound synchronizes feeling and knits a group faster than speech can" states a comparative claim nobody has measured.** Savage et al. 2021 argue musicality enabled bonding at *larger scales* than primate grooming; Mehr et al. 2021 dispute the bonding account entirely. "Faster than speech" is not a finding from either. **Recommend** rephrasing toward scale rather than speed, or attributing it.

**7. "Among the oldest instruments known" is correctly hedged. Keep it.** It is doing exactly the work trap 1 requires, and it is the one place the node data is more careful than the popular literature.

**8. "The oldest surviving art was not only painted; some of it was heard" is the node's best sentence and should survive into the lesson.**

**9. Region `europe` is correct but load-bearing in the wrong direction.** This is one of very few Era I nodes on the European meridian, and a reader scanning the chart will read early European priority off the map. Nothing to fix in the data; the lesson has to carry it, which is why the spine puts the water-screening argument and the Jiahu convergence into the significance rather than a footnote. Note that the obvious fix, Sulawesi, is unavailable: `paint-in-the-dark` has already run it.

**10. Graph architecture: this node has zero incoming edges.** Nothing earlier wires into it, so a reader traversing forward cannot reach it; it is reachable only by browsing, search, or the chart. Given that `spark-of-mind` (symbols in sound), `tribe-of-strangers` (synchrony without an instrument) and `firelight-economy` (skill versus levelling) all argue material this node completes, at least one backward wire looks missing. Worth a Codex decision. Not a defect in this lesson, and the lesson can compensate through `threadsOut`.
