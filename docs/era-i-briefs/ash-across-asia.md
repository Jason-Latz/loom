# Lesson brief: ash-across-asia

Research and architecture scaffolding for Fable. Not draft prose. Every claim below carries its evidentiary status. Where this brief and a source disagree, follow the source and say so in the completion report. Anything marked UNVERIFIED must be confirmed against a fetched publication or dropped.

## 1. Graph target

- Node id: `ash-across-asia`. Title: Ash Across Asia. Era 1 (The Long Dawn), date `c. 74,000 BCE`, sort -74000, region `sasia`, x 74, globalIndex 4 (fifth node in the atlas). Threads: `craft`, `ideas`.
- Question mix the gate will demand: 1 recall, 2 why, 2 callback. Both callbacks are mandatory; there is no 1-callback dispensation at this index.
- Outgoing edges (three, all forward):
  - `great-dispersal` (collides). Demands that this lesson set up the dating problem: the eruption sits near, but does not settle, the contested date for the southern dispersal out of Africa. The lesson must leave the reader holding a live question that `great-dispersal` then answers at world scale, so do not pre-empt the dispersal narrative here. Establish only the South Asian evidence and its limits.
  - `the-other-humans` (echoes). Demands that the lesson make the toolmaker's species genuinely open, not rhetorically open. The reader should finish able to say why an unidentified Middle Palaeolithic knapper in India at 74 ka is a real possibility rather than a coy hedge.
  - `world-the-ice-made` (echoes). Demands one clean paragraph on abrupt climate as a force on where humans can live. Note: the edge's stated "why" is now the weaker reading of the evidence (see flags, section 8). The lesson should carry the mechanism forward while correcting the overstatement.
- Incoming edges: none. This node is a source, not a hinge. It has no upstream obligations at all, which is unusual and freeing.

**Job in the atlas.** This is the atlas's first lesson about *how we know*, and it should be built as one. The four nodes before it (`first-of-our-kind`, `the-tended-fire`, `ochre-for-the-dead`, `spark-of-mind`) all read a human capacity out of an object. This node is the one where the objects refuse to answer. It carries three loads: it plants deep-time climate catastrophe before the ice-age nodes; it opens South and Southeast Asia, which the world-scale `great-dispersal` node glosses; and it teaches the reader the discipline that the rest of the atlas depends on, which is the difference between what a find shows, what it is consistent with, and what a good story wants it to mean. It is also the atlas's first chance to show a famous, beautiful, widely repeated scientific idea dying in public. Treat that as the gift it is.

## 2. Preferred scene

**Jwalapuram Locality 22, Jurreru River valley, Kurnool District, Andhra Pradesh, in the last days before the ash: a knapping ground on a flat, grassed rise above a seasonal wetland, worked over and over by people who carried limestone down from the ridge behind them.**

The reason this scene and not another: the excavators reconstructed the actual buried ground surface, three-dimensionally, from the depth of every artefact, and matched it to the stratigraphic contour. We are not imagining a floor. We have one. And it is a *working* floor, not a burial or a shrine, which is exactly right for a node whose threads are craft and ideas.

### Documentation ledger

Attested by excavation (Haslam et al. 2012, Quaternary International 258:148-164, verified from the full text):

- Site coordinates 15 degrees 19' 23.9" N, 78 degrees 07' 48.5" E. Open-air, no cave. Excavated over two seasons, 2008 to 2009, first as a 5 by 5 m trench, then expanded to 10 by 10 m; the exposed area at the tephra-palaeosol interface was 22.75 square metres and then 95.5 square metres.
- **1,628 stone artefacts** on the buried surface. Raw material was recorded for 1,626 of them.
- Raw material breakdown, verbatim from Table 5: limestone 1,528 (94.0 percent), dolerite 61 (3.8), chert 17 (1.0), quartzite 13 (0.8), chalcedony 4 (0.2), quartz 2 (0.1), **ochre 1 (0.1)**.
- The cherty limestone comes from the southern flank of the valley, **about 900 m from the excavation**, available as tabular or sub-rounded pieces, "varies greatly in quality," the best pieces with excellent fracture qualities, some with laminations that spoiled the work.
- Dolerite outcrops as a dyke along the upper margin of that same southern flank, under 1 km away, and was carried in as hammerstones. Both intact hammerstones and fragments are present.
- 50 cores, classified into 12 technological types, **all** of them local limestone. Levallois and multiplatform cores dominate; discoidal, semi-discoidal, single-platform and bidirectional cores are also present.
- Retouched flakes are nearly double the length of unretouched ones (mean 39.9 +/- 16.8 mm against 22.8 +/- 13.1 mm), so larger flakes were being chosen deliberately. Scrapers and notches dominate the retouched pieces; retouch is steep (73 +/- 11 degrees).
- Hammerstones lie in a rough outline around the densest part of the scatter. There are blank patches with almost no discard; one possibility the excavators raise is that vegetation was standing there.
- **Eight fire-cracked rocks** (three dolerite, five limestone). The excavators do not claim a hearth: low magnetic susceptibility in the upper palaeosol points to the stones having been carried in already cracked.
- Environment, from soil-carbonate stable isotopes plus a phytolith sample: a grassy woodland. C4 grasses fall to a minimum of roughly 30 to 45 percent of biomass in the upper (later) part of the sequence, with C3 woodland and grassland increasing. Phytoliths include a notable 24 percent trichomes, mostly long pointed hair cells.
- The excavators' own reconstruction of the living scene, which Fable may use nearly as written because it is theirs, not ours: "a clear, flat, grassed area, perhaps overshadowed by isolated trees, with views across the surrounding grasslands to the river and valley slopes." A depressed lacustrine or marshland environment sat a few hundred metres at most to the east. The site would have been visible from much of the valley flanks.
- Large vertebrates in the valley in the lead-up to the eruption, from Billa Surgam caves 13.5 km north-northeast, "may have included" the ungulates *Boselaphus* (nilgai), *Equus*, *Tetracerus* (four-horned antelope), *Rhinoceros* and *Axis* (chital deer). Note the hedge "may have included"; keep it.
- The Jurreru Valley's thickest ash accumulation is **over 2.5 m**. Locality 22 was found by walking the buried ash layer outward from that maximum to where it pinches out, because that thin edge is where the fall was most likely direct rather than washed in.
- Jwalapuram Locality 3, roughly 400 m east, sat in a topographic low; artefacts beneath the tephra there are dated to between 77 and 74 ka.
- After the ash: negligible tephra was mixed down into the palaeosol and no artefacts travelled up into the ash, which "may indicate that neither large herbivores nor termites were active at the site immediately following the Toba eruption." That is a haunting, publishable detail and it is the excavators' own sentence, not an inference of ours.
- Present day: villagers across the Jurreru Valley quarry the ash and farm the ground. Locality 22 survived because it had not yet been quarried; the excavators write that it "appears inevitable" that it will be within decades.

Also attested, from the same team's other papers:

- A cooling trend was already in effect in southern India **before** the eruption (Haslam et al. 2010, JAS 37:3370-3384, abstract verified). After it, "thick deposits of ash in the Jurreru Valley supported grassland communities before more wooded conditions were re-established."
- The ash did not land once. At Jwalapuram it arrived in **six annual monsoon pulses**: 2 to 3 m of tephra in six sub-units separated by six thin greyish-brown hardpans, wet-season remobilisation alternating with dry-season crusting (Jha et al. 2025, PNAS Nexus 4(4):pgaf109, verified). The valley was being re-buried every wet season for about six years.

Reconstruction, and `storyContext` must say so plainly: every person, name, age, relationship, word spoken, the weather, the hour, and above all whether anyone was standing there when the ash came. The evidence says the surface was exposed, vegetated and in use "closely prior to the eruption." It does not say occupied at the moment of the fall. Fable may bring the ash into the story, but the composite label has to carry that weight, and the significance section must say what the ground actually records.

Real named person available: **no**, and this is load-bearing rather than a shortfall. There are no human bones at Jwalapuram, no bones at Dhaba, and effectively no Pleistocene hominin fossils anywhere in India for this window. Say so; it is the node's whole argument.

Quantities to check before printing: everything in the ledger above is from the published tables and text and can be printed. Do NOT print the following without opening Haslam et al. 2010 first: that Locality 3 yielded 215 artefacts below the ash in a gravel lens dated to about 77 ka, 108 above it, and a piece of red ochre with striations. Those numbers reached me only through a secondary summary and a search snippet. Marked UNVERIFIED. The striated ochre in particular is exactly the kind of detail a writer will fall in love with, and exactly the kind that has burned this project before.

### Alternate 1: the excavation itself (safest, no invented person)

Open on the trowel reaching the contact: an undulating but sharp boundary where grey unmixed pods of tephra meet a yellowish brown palaeosol about two metres down, with limestone flakes and calcareous concretions lying on the interface, and termite passageways preserved as micritic carbonate tubes beside them. Every artefact piece-plotted in three dimensions by total station. Digging stopped in both seasons on hitting the water table, at about 3.5 m and about 6.4 m. Then the modern frame: the same ash is quarried out of this valley for a living, and this patch survived only because nobody had got to it yet. Weaker on human pressure, unimprovable on candour. Hold in reserve, or use its last beat as the lesson's hinge.

### Alternate 2: Lenggong Valley, Perak, Malaysia (356 km from Toba by my arithmetic from the coordinates)

Storey, Roberts and Saidin dated the eruption from sanidine crystals in the ash of this valley, and six kilometres from their borehole is Kota Tampan, where "in situ ash occurs among and above stone artifacts that may have been manufactured by anatomically modern humans," with the authors adding that "the absence of associated human fossils at Kota Tampan precludes a definitive verdict." Two virtues: it is the site that produced the eruption's actual date, and it repeats the species problem within a day's walk of the volcano. Liability: much thinner published excavation detail than Jwalapuram, and it moves the node out of `sasia`.

### Scene to AVOID as a cold open

The eruption itself, narrated from Sumatra. Do not write the mountain going up, the column climbing, the pyroclastic flows, a terrified band watching from a ridge. Four reasons. There is no evidence of any human being at the vent, so the entire scene would be invention wearing the costume of fact. It is the move every documentary makes, so it will read as stock footage. It centres catastrophe, when the node's actual subject is survival and epistemics. And it would force the writer to print exactly the quantities that are least settled (column height, sulfur mass, temperature drop), which is where the traps in section 5 live.

Second thing to avoid: opening on "the last few thousand humans." That is the discredited claim, and staging it as scene will make it true in the reader's memory no matter what the significance section says afterwards.

## 3. Story movement

Six or seven paragraphs. One workday and one aftermath, then a turn to the ground.

1. **The stone that will not co-operate.** Open inside the craft, at the hands. Someone is reducing a limestone nodule carried up from the southern flank about nine hundred metres away, and the piece has laminations in it, so it breaks wrong. Concrete: the weight of a dolerite hammerstone, the smell of a wetland a few hundred metres east, the sound of a chital herd, the flat grass and the isolated trees, the view down the valley that is also a view of who is coming. Let the reader learn Levallois by watching it, not by being told: the core shaped first, then the flake taken off it, the whole predetermined shape released in one blow. That is the "recipe" idea and it is the node's craft thread in a single gesture.
2. **The ordinary economics of the place.** Ninety-four percent of everything here came from one hillside; the hammerstones from a dyke on the same slope. Nobody knapped up on the ridge or the quartzite plateaus. This was the spot: water, sightlines, stone, and a reason to keep coming back. The excavators believe groups returned to this exact patch repeatedly. Let the reader feel a habit, not an event.
3. **The blank patches.** Where the flakes stop, something was standing. Grass, a bush, a tree. This is where the story should first admit that it is reading absences, and it should do so lightly, without breaking frame.
4. **The sky.** The ash. Handle it with restraint and get the physics right: this was not one night of falling grey. It came, and then the monsoon brought it down off the slopes again, and again, six wet seasons running, each with a hard pale crust baked on top in the dry months. The valley filled to over two and a half metres in places. Do not narrate screaming. Narrate burial.
5. **What the ash did to the valley.** Grassland first, then woodland coming back. And the strange stillness in the sediment: no termites tunnelling up through it, no herds churning it. The floor stayed exactly as it had been dropped.
6. **The pivot.** Move to the trowel, or to the villagers quarrying the ash today, and end on the question the significance section answers: we have the floor, we have every flake mapped in three dimensions, and we still cannot say what kind of human made them. End there. Do not resolve it.

Governing image: **two kinds of ground.** The brown surface people stood on, and the grey that sealed it. One is a place; the other is a clock. The image should return once at the burial and once at the close, changed. A second, subordinate image worth carrying: the limestone that breaks wrong. Material that will not do what you want is the whole node.

Words and framings to refuse. Do not write "mysterious," "vanished," "lost tribe," "brink of extinction," "Adam and Eve," or "apocalypse." Do not write that humans "clung on" or "barely survived"; the evidence says they carried on. Do not stage India, Sumatra or Africa as a corridor, waiting room, or rehearsal for anywhere. Do not call the Middle Palaeolithic "primitive" or the tools "crude"; they are the product of a taught, predetermined method. Do not use the word "modern" as a compliment. And do not say the eruption "changed everything," because the argument of the lesson is that it mostly did not.

## 4. Significance spine

Four to six movements. Threads that must be tied **in words**: `craft` and `ideas`. Craft is the Levallois method itself, a procedure held in the head and reproduced in the hand, transmissible and stable across a continent and across a catastrophe. Ideas is the harder and better one: the eruption's real legacy is that it became an instrument, a single grey line usable as a clock, and that a beautiful hypothesis about it was tested to death in public. Do not reach for `power` or `wealth`; there is nothing here to hang them on and forcing them will cheapen the piece.

1. **What is actually in the ground, and what it can carry.** The 1,628 artefacts, the 94 percent local limestone, the fifty cores, the hammerstones ringing the scatter, the reconstructed floor. Then the wall: no bones, no burials, no pigment worth the name, one ochre pebble out of 1,626 pieces. Give the excavators' own sentence, which is a small masterpiece of scientific manners: "The hominin taxon responsible for creating the site is not known, but the stone tool evidence is most consistent with contemporaneous Homo sapiens technologies in Africa and to the east of South Asia." Teach the reader to hear the difference between "is not known" and "most consistent with." That distinction is the lesson's spine and probably its best single teaching moment in the whole era.

2. **Mechanism: why there, why then, and what an eruption is good for.** Toba erupted around 73,880 years ago (73.88 +/- 0.32 ka, from sanidine in Malaysian ash). It is the largest explosive volcanic event of the past two million years. Ash reached India, the Bay of Bengal, the Arabian Sea, the South China Sea, Lake Malawi about 7,300 km away, and two archaeological sites on the south coast of South Africa about 9,000 km away (all distances my great-circle arithmetic from published coordinates; recompute before printing). Volume estimates: at least 2,800 cubic kilometres dense rock equivalent in the older literature; Costa and colleagues model roughly 8,600 cubic kilometres of ash, about 3,800 dense rock equivalent, spread over about 40 million square kilometres at more than 5 mm depth, plus about 1,500 more in pyroclastic flows. Give the range, not a number. Then the payoff: because that ash fell everywhere at once and can be chemically fingerprinted grain by grain, it is now the single most useful line in Late Pleistocene Asian stratigraphy, and it lets a cave in South Africa and a valley in Andhra Pradesh be pinned to the same instant. A catastrophe became a chronometer. That is the `ideas` thread landing.

3. **The named live debate. Use this one: Petraglia's team versus Mellars, Gori, Carr, Soares and Richards.** Michael Petraglia and colleagues, from the 2007 Science paper onward, argue that Middle Palaeolithic assemblages in India before and after the ash show local persistence, that the core technologies cluster with sub-Saharan African Middle Stone Age work and with early Southeast Asian and Australian assemblages, and therefore that *Homo sapiens* "cannot be ruled out" east of Africa before 74 ka. Paul Mellars, Kevin Gori, Martin Carr, Pedro Soares and Martin Richards replied in PNAS in 2013 that the pre-Toba dispersal model "is in serious conflict with both the most recent genetic evidence from both Africa and Asia and the archaeological evidence from South Asian sites." Their genetic ceiling: mitochondrial haplogroup L3, ancestral to all non-African maternal lineages, coalesces at an upper bound near 79 ka (95 percent range roughly 62 to 95 ka), with haplogroup N in Arabia near 61 ka, which they read as leaving no room for a successful exit before 74 ka. Give both sides. Give no verdict. Then complicate both honestly: modern human teeth from Lida Ajer in Sumatra date to 73,000 to 63,000 years ago, and the earliest Australian occupation at Madjedbebe is 65 +/- 6 ka, so a strict 60 ka exit is squeezed from the other direction too. The honest summary is that everyone's clock is loose and the fossils that would settle it do not exist in India.

4. **The bottleneck: an idea's life and death, told properly.** This is the section that will make the lesson. Build it as a narrative of a hypothesis, not a debunking. Michael Rampino and Stephen Self proposed volcanic winter and accelerated glaciation; Stanley Ambrose, in the Journal of Human Evolution in 1998, joined it to the genetics and argued that Toba's volcanic winter "could have decimated most modern human populations, especially outside of isolated tropical refugia," leaving survivors mainly in equatorial Africa, and that "modern human races may have differentiated abruptly, only 70 thousand years ago." It was a gorgeous idea: one mountain, one winter, one narrow gate, and everyone alive today on the far side of it. Then list what came in against it, and note that the field did this to itself, in the open, over twenty-five years. Lake Malawi cores show no volcanic winter in East Africa at 75 ka (Lane, Chorn and Johnson, PNAS 2013), and subdecadal phytolith and charcoal sampling across the same ash concludes the eruption did not "produce a 6-year-long volcanic winter in eastern Africa," did not "cause a genetic bottleneck among African AMH populations," and did not "bring humanity to the brink of extinction" (Yost, Jackson, Stone and Cohen, JHE 2018). At Pinnacle Point and Vleesbaai in South Africa, glass shards from the eruption sit inside sediments where occupation continues without interruption; the authors' word is "thrived." The bipolar ice-core linking "gives no support for a long-term global cooling caused by the Toba eruption as Antarctica experiences a major warming shortly after the event" (Svensson and 25 colleagues, Climate of the Past 2013). The climate models converge on the same map: even at the upper end of plausible sulfur, there is a near-zero probability of annual mean cooling above 4 degrees in most of Africa and under 5 percent across nearly all of sub-Saharan Africa and India, against near-certainty of cooling that severe in Asia and North America and 8 to 10 degrees in northern-hemisphere continental interiors (Black, Lamarque, Marsh, Schmidt and Bardeen, PNAS 2021). And at Jwalapuram itself, the year-by-year record shows warming and drying after the eruption, not a winter. One further honest twist to include, because it is the most interesting fact in the section: the strongest counter-evidence comes from Africa and India, the two regions the models identify as sheltered, so "no volcanic winter here" is not the same as "no volcanic winter anywhere." Ambrose's mechanism may have been real in the north and simply irrelevant where our ancestors were.

5. **Why the tools cannot name the hands, and why that matters beyond this node.** Stone tools track technique, and technique travels. Chris Clarkson and fifteen colleagues, reporting the Dhaba sequence in the Middle Son Valley in 2020, bracket an unchanging industry across the ash between 79.6 +/- 3.2 and 65.2 +/- 3.1 ka and read it as "likely the product of Homo sapiens." That is the strongest continuity evidence anyone has, and it still rests on resemblance. Note the tell for the reader: the same paper's abstract says "likely," while its discussion says "here interpreted as the product of Homo sapiens." Watching a hedge tighten between two paragraphs of one paper is a better lesson in how science is written than any amount of exposition. Then the open possibilities: India's only Middle Pleistocene hominin fossil is the Narmada calvaria from Hathnora, whose own classification is disputed (mark UNVERIFIED in detail; confirm before printing anything about it), and Denisovan ancestry in living South and Southeast Asians means other human kinds were somewhere in this landscape. This is the paragraph that pays the `the-other-humans` edge.

6. **Landing.** The modern world carries this node twice. First materially: that grey line is still how a great deal of Asian prehistory is dated, so an eruption that once told us we nearly died now mostly tells us *when* things happened. Second intellectually: this is the clearest case in the whole atlas of a scientific story being loved, tested, and released, and of the difference between an explanation that is thrilling and one that is true. Close on the ochre pebble if you can find the right sentence for it: one piece, out of 1,626, and the responsible thing to say about it is nothing.

## 5. Factual traps

Thirteen. Trap / Truth / Source.

1. **Trap:** Toba nearly exterminated humanity, cutting the species to a few thousand people, or to about a thousand breeding pairs. **Truth:** this was Ambrose's 1998 hypothesis, and it is not supported. His own abstract is conditional throughout ("could have decimated," "may have reduced") and states no headcount. The genetic arguments it rested on concern *effective* population size, which is not a census count and is not dated to 74 ka. High-resolution African records, the South African archaeology, the ice cores and the climate models all now run against it, and the recent synthesis literature states that the evidence "dictates rejection of previous population bottleneck hypotheses." **Source:** Ambrose 1998, J. Hum. Evol. 34(6):623-651 (abstract verified at PubMed); Yost, Jackson, Stone and Cohen 2018, J. Hum. Evol. 116:75-94; Ge and Gao 2020, Quaternary International 559:24-33 (metadata verified at Crossref, abstract via search summary, so treat Ge and Gao as corroboration rather than a load-bearing citation).

2. **Trap:** Toba caused a six-year volcanic winter and cooled the planet by 3 to 5 degrees, more than 10 degrees in places. **Truth:** modelled ranges are wide and regionally uneven, and the paleoclimate proxies do not show a winter where humans actually were. Black et al. 2021 model maximum global mean cooling of 2.3 +/- 0.4 degrees for 200 Tg SO2 and 4.1 +/- 0.3 degrees for 2,000 Tg, with 8 to 10 degrees in northern-hemisphere continental interiors, near-zero probability of 4 degrees annual mean cooling in most of Africa, and under 5 percent across virtually all of sub-Saharan Africa and India. Costa et al. 2014 give 1,700 to 3,500 Tg SO2 and note simulations with perturbations lasting only about 5 to 10 years. Yost et al. 2018 explicitly reject a six-year eastern African winter. **Source:** Black, Lamarque, Marsh, Schmidt and Bardeen 2021, PNAS 118(29):e2013046118 (abstract and figures verified); Costa, Smith, Macedonio and Matthews 2014, Frontiers in Earth Science 2:16 (full text verified).

3. **Trap:** Toba triggered the last glacial period, or caused the deep cold seen in the Greenland ice. **Truth:** two problems. Storey, Roberts and Saidin place the eruption "to the middle of the cold interval between Dansgaard-Oeschger events 20 and 19," meaning the stadial was already under way when the mountain went up, and Haslam et al. 2010 show a cooling trend already in effect in southern India before the ash. Storey et al. do report a sulfate peak followed by a roughly 10 degree Greenland surface cooling over about 150 years and call it "the possible climatic impact of the eruption"; Svensson et al., linking Greenland and Antarctic cores directly at the eruption, find the opposite at the other pole and write that the linking "gives no support for a long-term global cooling caused by the Toba eruption as Antarctica experiences a major warming shortly after the event." Present the tension; do not resolve it. **Source:** Storey, Roberts and Saidin 2012, PNAS 109(46):18684-18688; Svensson and 25 co-authors 2013, Climate of the Past 9(2):749-766; Haslam et al. 2010, J. Archaeol. Sci. 37(12):3370-3384 (abstract verified).

4. **Trap:** Jwalapuram proves *Homo sapiens* were in India before Toba, and therefore proves an early southern dispersal. **Truth:** the excavators say the opposite in print. "The hominin taxon responsible for creating the site is not known." Their strongest formulation anywhere is that *Homo sapiens* "cannot be ruled out," and they add the caveat that "while core technologies can track regional diversity, classifications may not reliably map onto biological taxa." **Source:** Haslam et al. 2012, Quaternary International 258:148-164 (abstract and discussion verified from full text); Haslam et al. 2010, J. Archaeol. Sci. 37(12):3370-3384.

5. **Trap:** at Jwalapuram, stone tools lie immediately above and immediately below the ash, so people were living there right through the eruption. **Truth:** this is the 2007 reading and the same team revised it. At Locality 22, OSL dating of the sediments above the tephra, there and elsewhere in the valley, "suggest a local sedimentation gap of c. 35,000 years following the Toba event ... contrary to initial reports (Petraglia et al., 2007)"; the artefacts above the ash there date to about 40 ka, and include a microblade core, a form that does not become common in the valley until 38 to 35 ka. The above-and-below claim rests on Locality 3, and the best modern continuity evidence is Dhaba in the Middle Son Valley, where an unchanging industry is bracketed between 79.6 +/- 3.2 and 65.2 +/- 3.1 ka. Write "toolmaking of the same character continues in India across the ash," not "people never left this spot." **Source:** Haslam et al. 2012, Quaternary International 258:148-164 (verified from full text); Clarkson et al. 2020, Nature Communications 11:961.

6. **Trap:** the Toba ash is a clean, continuous, planet-wide layer, a perfect isochron you can trace across India. **Truth:** in the Middle Son Valley it forms a discontinuous bed over more than 30 km of river, much of it reworked, and the authors conclude the occurrences there "may not be reliable chronostratigraphical markers for millennial scale palaeoenvironmental reconstruction." Even at Jwalapuram the deposit is six pulses of remobilised ash separated by hardpans, not one fall. And the ash that matters most now is *cryptotephra*: microscopic shards invisible in the field, recovered chemically, which is how it turned up in Lake Malawi and in South Africa. **Source:** Gatti, Durant, Gibbard and Oppenheimer 2011, Quaternary Science Reviews 30(27-28):3925-3934 (citation and findings via search results; open the paper before quoting it); Jha et al. 2025, PNAS Nexus 4(4):pgaf109 (verified); Smith et al. 2018, Nature 555(7697):511-515 (abstract verified).

7. **Trap:** the 2023 Science paper on a "severe human bottleneck" confirms Toba. **Truth:** different event, wrong era by more than 700,000 years. Hu, Hao, Du, Di Vincenzo, Manzi, Cui, Fu, Pan and Li, "Genomic inference of a severe human bottleneck during the Early to Middle Pleistocene transition," Science 381(6661):979-984 (2023), infers a small ancestral population between roughly 930,000 and 813,000 years ago. It has nothing to do with Toba, and the two are already being conflated online. **Source:** Crossref record for DOI 10.1126/science.abq7487 (title, all nine authors, volume, issue, pages verified).

8. **Trap:** the eruption happened 71,000 or 70,000 years ago (Ambrose's date), or "about 75,000." **Truth:** the best current age is 73.88 +/- 0.32 ka, from astronomically calibrated argon dating of sanidine crystals in Toba ash in the Lenggong Valley, Malaysia. Ambrose wrote in 1998 with a chronology of roughly 71 to 70 ka, which is why the older literature reads that way. Round to "about 74,000 years ago" in prose and give the precise figure once. **Source:** Storey, Roberts and Saidin 2012, PNAS 109(46):18684-18688 (verified at PMC and Crossref).

9. **Trap:** the two South African sites are 9,000 km apart. **Truth:** they are nine kilometres apart; it is Toba that is roughly 9,000 km away. The press coverage of the 2018 paper collapsed the two figures and the error is now everywhere. The abstract says the sites "are located nine kilometres apart." **Source:** Smith and 15 co-authors 2018, Nature 555(7697):511-515 (abstract verified at PubMed; author list verified at Crossref; nature.com itself is paywalled).

10. **Trap:** Toba was the largest eruption in Earth's history, and it ejected 2,800 cubic kilometres of rock. **Truth:** largest of the *Quaternary*, that is the past two million years or so, not of all time; larger eruptions sit deeper in the geological record. And the volume is a live range, not a number: at least 2,800 cubic kilometres dense rock equivalent in the older literature, against roughly 8,600 cubic kilometres of ash, about 3,800 dense rock equivalent, plus around 1,500 in pyroclastic flows, in the 2014 modelling. Caldera about 100 by 30 km. **Source:** Costa, Smith, Macedonio and Matthews 2014, Frontiers in Earth Science 2:16 (full text verified: caldera dimensions, volumes, 40 million square kilometres at over 5 mm); Haslam et al. 2010 abstract for "largest explosive volcanic event of the past two million years."

11. **Trap:** there was ochre at Jwalapuram, so the toolmakers were symbolic thinkers like the people at Blombos. **Truth:** the ochre at Locality 22 is a single piece, one artefact out of 1,626 with recorded raw material, listed in a raw-material table with no use, grinding, striation or pigment residue claimed. It supports nothing on its own. (Separately, a striated ochre piece is reported below the ash at Locality 3, but that report reached me only at snippet level and is UNVERIFIED; confirm against Haslam et al. 2010 or drop it entirely.) **Source:** Haslam et al. 2012, Quaternary International 258, Table 5 (verified from full text).

12. **Trap:** *Homo sapiens* must have made these tools because nobody else was in India. **Truth:** India has essentially no hominin fossils from this window at all; the only Middle Pleistocene hominin from the subcontinent is the Narmada material from Hathnora, whose taxonomic assignment has been argued over for four decades. Absence of other candidates in the fossil record is absence of a fossil record, not absence of candidates, and Denisovan ancestry in living South and Southeast Asian populations shows other human kinds were somewhere in this world. **Source:** Narmada details reached me only through search summaries and are marked UNVERIFIED; if the lesson names Hathnora, Sonakia, or a date, verify against a fetched publication first. The safe version needs no names: there are no human bones from India at 74 ka.

13. **Trap:** Jwalapuram supports the coastal southern-dispersal route. **Truth:** Jwalapuram sits roughly 210 to 220 km inland from the Bay of Bengal (my great-circle arithmetic from the published site coordinates, 15.32 N 78.13 E, against the coast at the same latitude; recompute before printing). It is an inland valley site with local limestone, a seasonal wetland and grazing ungulates. The coastal-route model is a model, and most of the coastline it proposes is under water. The node's own expansion rationale invokes the southern coastal route; the lesson should not let an inland knapping floor stand in for a beach. **Source:** coordinates from Haslam et al. 2012, Quaternary International 258:148-164; distance is my own calculation.

## 6. Callback design

Legal earlier targets are the four nodes before this one. Only `spark-of-mind` has a written lesson file; I read it in full. The other three exist as graph summaries only, so any comparison to them must be grounded strictly in the summary text quoted in the packet.

- **`spark-of-mind`** (WRITTEN, `data/lessons/spark-of-mind.js`). Strongest available, and it works in two directions at once. First, craft to craft: that lesson's central object is a recipe whose order matters, ground ochre plus charcoal plus marrow fat plus seawater, stirred in sequence in an abalone shell, "evidence of minds that could hold a sequence and pass it on." A Levallois core is the same claim in stone, a shape prepared so that one blow releases a predetermined flake, and it is the same claim reaching across a continent and across an eruption. Second, and better, evidence to evidence: Blombos is the case where material culture speaks, because a bead's only job is to mean something. Jwalapuram is the case where it goes silent: no beads, no engravings, one ochre pebble, and the excavators writing "the hominin taxon is not known." The reader who learned at Blombos that objects can testify to minds should be made to feel, here, exactly where that inference runs out. Third angle if a shorter version is wanted: `spark-of-mind` argues that cumulative culture, not biology, is the engine, and that a technique survives its inventor. Continuity of Levallois technique across the ash is that ratchet holding under load, in the one place we can watch it.

- **`first-of-our-kind`** (unwritten; use its summary only). Recommended as the second callback. Its summary says the Jebel Irhoud fossils show modern features accumulating "in a mosaic," that "Africa was the workshop, not a single point on a map," and, crucially, that "the Middle Stone Age tools beside them were already shared far and wide." That last clause is simultaneously the premise of the Jwalapuram and Dhaba argument (these Indian assemblages resemble African MSA work, therefore probably *Homo sapiens*) and the reason the argument cannot close (a technique shared far and wide does not name the hands holding it). One earlier node supplies both the tool and the objection. That is a real comparison, not trivia, and it is exactly the kind of question the forge spec wants.

- **`ochre-for-the-dead`** (unwritten; use its summary only). Strong alternate, and the best one if Fable wants the callback pair to be about evidentiary thresholds rather than about dispersal. Its summary already concedes that "archaeology cannot recover the mourners' exact beliefs or prove an afterlife doctrine," yet a body arranged with fallow deer antlers across the chest, in layers with ochre and pierced shells, does carry an inference about shared attention to death. Set that against one ochre pebble in 1,626 artefacts. Same material, same continent-spanning span of time, opposite verdicts, and the difference is context and quantity rather than the substance itself. Ask the reader to say what changed.

- **`the-tended-fire`** (unwritten; use its summary only). Weakest of the four, but honest and usable. Its summary describes a hearth at Qesem holding ash returned to the same spot for millennia, and admits the beginnings of tamed fire are disputed. Jwalapuram has eight fire-cracked rocks, three dolerite and five limestone, and its excavators decline to call them a hearth, leaning instead toward the stones having been carried in already cracked. A repeated hearth carries a social claim; eight cracked stones carry none. Use only if one of the first three is claimed elsewhere in the era.

**Recommended pair: `spark-of-mind` and `first-of-our-kind`.** Rank of alternates: `ochre-for-the-dead`, then `the-tended-fire`. If a reviewer objects to leaning on two unwritten summaries anywhere in Era I, swap the second callback to `ochre-for-the-dead` rather than doubling up on `spark-of-mind`.

Suggested `threadsOut` (3 to 5, mirroring the graph, fresh "why" text): `great-dispersal`, `the-other-humans`, `world-the-ice-made`, plus optionally `spark-of-mind` backward, which the lesson will have earned through the recipe-and-evidence comparison and which the graph does not draw.

## 8. Node-data flags

Precise problems in the committed graph data. Do not edit any repo file; report them.

1. **Hook, "Stoneworkers return afterward."** Overreaches in two ways. It implies a departure and a return within a legible interval, which no site shows; the evidence is continuity of technique across the ash horizon, at different localities, with wide dating brackets. And at Jwalapuram Locality 22 specifically, the excavators report a local sedimentation gap of about 35,000 years after the eruption, with the overlying artefacts dating to roughly 40 ka. Suggested repair: "Toolmaking of the same character continues on both sides of the ash, though the tools cannot tell us which human lineage held them."

2. **Summary, "Stone tools at Jwalapuram in India occur above and below its ash, evidence that toolmaking communities endured locally."** This is the 2007 claim, and the same team revised the Locality 22 half of it in 2012 in print ("contrary to initial reports"). The above-and-below evidence at Jwalapuram rests on Locality 3, and the best modern version of the claim is Dhaba in the Middle Son Valley, bracketed 79.6 +/- 3.2 to 65.2 +/- 3.1 ka. The summary is not false but it is dated by about thirteen years. Consider naming Dhaba alongside Jwalapuram, or softening to "in India."

3. **Summary, "the largest in two million years."** Supported. Haslam et al. 2010 call it "the largest explosive volcanic event of the past two million years" and Costa et al. 2014 call it the largest known Quaternary super-eruption. No change needed. Guard only against the lesson upgrading it to "largest ever."

4. **Edge to `world-the-ice-made`, "a single eruption showed how abruptly climate could rewrite where humans were able to live."** This is now the weaker reading. A cooling trend was already under way in southern India before the eruption; the eruption falls in the middle of an existing stadial; the ice cores show Antarctic warming shortly after; the models put Africa and India in the shelter; and the Jwalapuram sequence itself shows warming and drying rather than a winter. The wire is still worth having, but its "why" currently asserts the thing the lesson has to correct. Suggested repair: something like "The clearest test we have of whether a single catastrophe can redraw the human map, and the answer keeps coming back regional rather than global." Codex decision.

5. **Region `sasia` plus the expansion rationale's "southern coastal dispersal route through South and Southeast Asia."** Jwalapuram is an inland valley site roughly 210 to 220 km from the Bay of Bengal. Dhaba is further inland still. The node's rationale leans on a coastal-route framing that neither of its anchor sites supports, and most of the proposed coastline is now submerged. Not a data error, but the lesson must not let the rationale leak into the prose as though the sites were coastal.

6. **Threads `craft` and `ideas`.** Correct, and I recommend keeping both, but note that `ideas` cannot be earned here through symbolic behaviour, since the site has almost none. It has to be earned through epistemics: the ash as an instrument, and the public life and death of the bottleneck hypothesis. Worth recording so a later reviewer does not read the `ideas` pigment as a claim about Palaeolithic symbolism.

7. **Date field, minor.** `date: 'c. 74,000 BCE'` against a measured 73.88 +/- 0.32 ka BP, which is about 71,930 BCE. At this depth the offset is immaterial and the atlas's BCE convention is fine, but the prose should say "about 74,000 years ago" rather than "74,000 BCE" wherever it can, since the round number is a before-present figure.

8. **Graph architecture observation.** Zero incoming edges makes this the atlas's first true orphan-source node, and its three outgoing wires all land inside Era I. Nothing later in the atlas points back at it, so a reader who arrives here from the chart rather than from the main sequence gets no context at all. If Codex ever wants a wire from a later node, the natural candidate is whichever node covers volcanic or abrupt-climate shocks in the historical era, since the "catastrophe as chronometer" idea has a long afterlife. Not a defect in this lesson.
