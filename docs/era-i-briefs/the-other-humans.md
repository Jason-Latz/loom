# Lesson brief: the-other-humans

Research and architecture scaffolding for Fable. Not draft prose. Every claim below carries its evidentiary status. Where this brief and a source disagree, follow the source and say so in the completion report. Anything marked UNVERIFIED was not confirmed against a fetched publication in this pass and must be confirmed or dropped before it is printed.

## 1. Graph target

- Node id: `the-other-humans`. Title: The Other Humans. Era 1 (The Long Dawn), date field `c. 250,000 to 40,000 BCE`, region `world`, globalIndex 7. Threads: **ideas, craft**.
- Question mix the gate will demand: 1 recall, 2 why, 2 callback.
- Outgoing edges, three, all inside Era I:
  - `crossing-to-sahul` (echoes). Demands that the lesson establish that a small-bodied hominin reached Flores, an island never joined to the mainland, long before our lineage crossed to Sahul. The lesson must set up the water-crossing puzzle without answering it: leave open whether Flores was reached deliberately or by tsunami rafting, so the Sahul lesson can claim deliberate seafaring as its own.
  - `tribe-of-strangers` (transforms). Demands that the lesson end with one surviving lineage and pose the question of what held it together. Do not steal that lesson's answer (shared fiction, totem, reputation); hand it the question.
  - `the-eyed-needle` (echoes). Demands a paragraph of real Neanderthal cold-weather competence, so that the needle lesson can be an extension rather than a revelation. Note there is no eyed needle in the Neanderthal record; the honest setup is hide-working, fire, hafting adhesives and cordage, not sewing.
- Incoming edges, two:
  - `first-of-our-kind` (Jebel Irhoud, c. 300,000 BCE) assumes this lesson delivers the plural-family context for a mosaic origin: our species assembled itself in Africa while other lineages held Eurasia.
  - `ash-across-asia` (Toba, c. 74,000 BCE) assumes this lesson delivers the reason stone tools cannot name a species. That is a direct handoff: Jwalapuram's toolmakers are anonymous because lithics carry no molecules, and this node is where molecules enter the atlas.

**Job in the atlas.** This is the node that dismantles the ladder. Everything before it on the chart has been about our lineage becoming itself; everything after it is about our lineage alone. This node is the hinge where the reader learns that being the only human kind is a recent and strange condition, roughly forty thousand years old, and that the science establishing it is younger than most of the readers. It is also the atlas's first lesson about epistemics as such: how we know, and what changed when we learned to read species out of a gram of bone, a smear of protein, and a bucket of cave dirt. Geographically it is the node that has to pull Era I out of Africa-and-Europe and into the Altai, the Tibetan Plateau, the Taiwan Strait, Flores and Luzon.

## 2. Preferred scene

**Oxford, 2015 to 2016: Samantha Brown at the mass spectrometry bench, and the bone splinter catalogued DC1227.**

The scene is the identification of Denisova 11, later nicknamed Denny. It is the rarest thing available in Era I: a documented moment with real named people in it, which the forge spec explicitly prefers over composites.

What is attested:
- DC1227 was excavated in 2012 from layer 12 of the East Gallery at Denisova Cave in the Altai, Russia (Wikipedia's sourced summary; confirm against the paper before printing the layer).
- Brown, Higham, Buckley and colleagues screened a large assemblage of morphologically undiagnostic bone fragments by ZooMS, collagen peptide mass fingerprinting, and recovered a single hominin. The Oxford press account, reproduced at phys.org, gives roughly 2,300 unidentified fragments, of which about 2,000 were animal. **Check the exact N in Scientific Reports 6:23559 before printing a number.**
- The fragment is about 2.5 cm long. Reported dimensions before sampling: 1.68 g, 24.7 mm by 8.39 mm. **Verify against the paper or its supplement.**
- Radiocarbon put it beyond the method's reach, so "more than 50,000 years old" is what 2016 could say.
- The mitochondrial genome, published in 2016, read as Neanderthal.
- The nuclear genome, published in 2018 (Slon et al., Nature 561:113-116), showed a Neanderthal mother and a Denisovan father, first generation. Her father's own genome carried Neanderthal ancestry. Her mother's population was closer to Neanderthals who later lived in Europe than to the earlier Neanderthal from the same cave.
- Cortical thickness indicates an age at death of at least 13 years.
- Douka et al. 2019 model Denisova 11 to between 80,000 and 140,000 years ago. The point estimate quoted in popular accounts is about 90,000. Use the range, and say the point estimate is a convenience.
- One real quotation exists: Brown, to the Oxford press office, "When the ZooMS results showed that there was a human fingerprint among the bones I was extremely excited." It is a press-office quote, not a line from the paper. Attribute it that way or paraphrase.

What would be reconstruction: the weather, the hour, the look of a spectrum on a screen, what anyone felt, the order of the day, whether Brown was alone. Also every detail of Denny's own life. If the lesson moves to the Altai 90,000 years ago (it should, once), `storyContext` must say plainly that the girl herself is reconstruction and only the molecules are evidence.

Real named person available: yes, several. Samantha Brown, Thomas Higham, Michael Buckley, Viviane Slon, Matthias Meyer, Svante Pääbo. Spend this. Era I has had three composites in a row.

Quantities to check before printing: the fragment count, the fragment mass and dimensions, the 13-year age-at-death floor, the 80,000 to 140,000 modelled age, the excavation layer.

Why this scene. It dramatizes the node's core mechanism twice over. First, the human family was plural and it interbred, and here is a person who is literally the junction of two branches. Second, the reason we know any of this is a change in craft, not a change in the ground: the same cave had been dug for decades before a peptide fingerprint found a girl in the discard tray. And it contains a built-in reversal for the reader: in 2016 the answer was "Neanderthal," and it was wrong by half, because mitochondria come only from the mother.

### Alternate 1: Liang Bua, Flores

The chamber six metres down, the LB1 skeleton, and the fauna in the same deposits: Stegodon florensis insularis, giant marabou stork, Komodo dragon, stone tools. Strong on wonder and it moves the lesson decisively out of the northern hemisphere. Liability: I did not verify the discovery-day details (who was digging, the exact depth, the date in September 2003) in this pass, so anything of that kind is UNVERIFIED and must be sourced or left out. Build it from the published stratigraphy instead of from discovery anecdote if you use it. Best used as a second movement rather than the cold open, because opening on Flores makes the lesson an island curiosity rather than a global revision.

### Alternate 2: Baishiya Karst Cave, Tibetan Plateau, 3,280 m

The Xiahe mandible, at least 160,000 years old, the first Denisovan found outside Siberia, from a cave that is an active Buddhist site. There is a widely repeated provenance story about a monk finding the jaw in 1980 and passing it to a lama and thence to Lanzhou University. **That chain is UNVERIFIED in this pass. Confirm it against Chen et al. 2019 or a university source, or drop it entirely.** The altitude angle is superb because it pays off directly in significance via EPAS1.

### Scene to AVOID

The last Neanderthal at Gorham's Cave on Gibraltar, looking out to sea. Four reasons. It is the single most recycled image in popular palaeoanthropology. The very late Iberian survival dates it depends on are among those Higham et al. 2014 could not confirm with improved pretreatment. It turns the node into an elegy for a doomed race, which is the ladder-shaped frame this atlas exists to refuse. And it parks the entire lesson in western Europe, when the node's stated rationale is to reach Central Asia and island Southeast Asia on their own terms. Also avoid, for the same atlas-level reason, any scene staging a confrontation between sapiens and Neanderthals: this is not a military history, and the archaeological record does not supply the battle.

## 3. Story movement

Seven paragraphs, one bench and one cave, with a hinge in the middle.

1. **The tray.** Bone scrap from a cave that has been excavated for decades. Establish the problem before the method: almost everything that comes out of a Pleistocene cave is unidentifiable splinters, and for a century they were the part nobody could use.
2. **The method as craft.** Collagen outlasts DNA. Each species leaves a different peptide fingerprint. This is a laboratory procedure with the texture of a workshop: acid, filters, a machine that weighs molecules. Keep it physical.
3. **The hit.** One hominin among thousands. Radiocarbon says only "older than the method can see."
4. **The wrong answer.** Mitochondrial DNA reads Neanderthal. Let the reader close the case here. Do not tip the reversal.
5. **The hinge.** Two years, a nuclear genome, and the arithmetic the reader can do themselves: mitochondria descend only through mothers, so a maternal molecule can only ever tell half a story. Her father was a Denisovan.
6. **The girl.** Labelled reconstruction. At least thirteen. Somewhere between eighty and a hundred and forty thousand years ago, in a valley her father's people had used for something like a hundred millennia, with a mother whose line had come from far to the west. Resist inventing dialogue or a death scene; the restraint is the effect.
7. **The pivot.** She was not a marvel. The team's own inference is that turning up a first-generation offspring among the handful of archaic genomes ever sequenced means the mixing was ordinary. End at the question, not the moral: if this was ordinary, why is there only one kind of us now?

**Governing image: the splinter that turned out to be a junction.** A 24 mm sliver too small to identify by eye, holding two branches of the family tree in one body. Bring it back once at the pivot, changed: the smallest object in the lesson contains the largest claim.

**Words and framings to avoid.** "Mysterious," "enigmatic," "shrouded," "vanished," "disappeared without trace," "died out because they could not compete," "primitive," "brute," "caveman," "our closest cousins" as a pat phrase, "won," "dominant species," "the hobbit" used as if it were a taxon. Do not stage Neanderthals or Denisovans as failed drafts of us or as a rung below us. Do not let the lesson become a European story with Asian footnotes: Siberia, Tibet, Taiwan, Flores and Luzon should carry as much of the word count as Europe. Do not write "interbreeding" as if it were scandalous; write it as demography.

## 4. Significance spine

Five movements. The lesson must name the **ideas** thread and the **craft** thread in words: ideas, because the plural human family changed what the word "human" means and the change is only about fifteen years old in public consciousness; craft, because every fact in this lesson arrived through a new laboratory technique rather than a new hole in the ground.

**1. Evidence, and the four techniques that made this node possible.** Take them in order, because the order is the argument. (a) Mitochondrial DNA from a finger bone, 2010: Krause et al., Nature 464:894-897, announced a hominin nobody had ever seen, whose mtDNA diverged from ours and from Neanderthal mtDNA around a million years ago. A population named from a molecule. (b) The nuclear genome, 2010: Reich et al., Nature 468:1053-1060, named them Denisovans and estimated 4.8 plus or minus 0.5 percent of Melanesian genomes as Denisovan, with up to 7.4 plus or minus 0.8 percent archaic in total. (c) Collagen fingerprinting, 2016: Brown et al. found a hominin in the discard fraction. (d) Sediment DNA, 2021: Zavala et al., Nature 595:399-403, recovered hominin mtDNA from 175 of 728 sediment samples at Denisova Cave, with the earliest Denisovan signal in layers of roughly 250,000 to 170,000 years ago and modern human mtDNA appearing by about 45,000. You can now find a human in dirt that contains no bone. (e) Palaeoproteomics, 2019 to 2025: the Xiahe mandible (Chen et al., Nature 569:409-412) and then, in 2025, Penghu 1 from the seabed off Taiwan and the Harbin cranium. Say plainly that this is the craft thread: the ratchet turned on our own past.

**2. Mechanism: why plural then, why singular now.** The variable is connection. Lineages form when populations are isolated and dissolve when they are not. Isolation is the through-line: the Altai Neanderthal whose parents were related at the level of half-siblings (Prüfer et al. 2014, Nature 505:43-49); Thorin, a late Neanderthal from Grotte Mandrin whose lineage had been genetically separated from other late Neanderthals for something like 50,000 years despite living close by (Slimak et al. 2024, Cell Genomics); islands that shrink bodies, on Flores and again on Luzon. Against that, a lineage arriving in Eurasia in waves with reinforcements behind it. Kolodny and Feldman (2017, Nature Communications) showed that a model with no advantage for either side, only repeated migration from Africa and random local replacement, already predicts the outcome. That is a genuinely deflating and genuinely useful result: you do not need a superiority story to get this ending. State it without endorsing it as the answer.

**3. The named live debate (use this one): where did Homo floresiensis come from?** Two camps, both published, both current. Argue, Groves, Lee and Jungers (2017, Journal of Human Evolution) ran a phylogenetic analysis of cranial, dental and postcranial characters and concluded that floresiensis is a long-surviving relict of an early Homo lineage, older than 1.75 million years, implying a migration out of Africa nobody had previously posited. Kaifu, Kurniawan, van den Bergh and colleagues (2024, Nature Communications 15, "Early evolution of small body size in Homo floresiensis") reported an adult humerus from Mata Menge at about 700,000 years old, 9 to 16 percent shorter than the LB1 type specimen and the smallest adult hominin humerus on record, and read the whole sequence as dwarfing from early Asian Homo erectus within the first 300,000 years on the island. Give both, name both, do not adjudicate. The stakes are worth stating: one version makes Flores the last outpost of a lineage that left Africa before anything we would call human; the other makes it the most extreme demonstration on Earth of what an island does to a body.
*(Reserve debate if this one is spent elsewhere: whether Neanderthals made the Iberian cave art. Hoffmann et al. 2018, Science 359:912-915, gave uranium-thorium minimum ages beyond 64,800 years at La Pasiega, Maltravieso and Ardales. Slimak et al., comment in Science, and White et al. 2020, Journal of Human Evolution, argue open-system uranium behaviour inflates the ages, noting that the other side of the same La Pasiega figure dated to about 3,100 years.)*

**4. Honest uncertainty, stated as ranges.** Denisova 11: 80,000 to 140,000 years ago. The end of the Neanderthal archaeological record: the Mousterian ends across Europe at 41,030 to 39,260 calibrated years before present, with real regional variation. Homo floresiensis: skeletal remains 100,000 to 60,000, associated artefacts 190,000 to 50,000, with a faunal turnover at Liang Bua near 60,000. Penghu 1: dated only to 10,000 to 70,000 or else 130,000 to 190,000, which is an honest way to show how bad a seabed provenance is. The main admixture pulse: 50,500 to 43,500 years ago on one analysis, 45,000 to 49,000 on another. Denisovan ancestry in Papuans and Melanesians: roughly 4 to 6 percent depending on method and reference panel. Do not average these into false precision.

**5. Landing: what the modern world still carries.** Three concrete inheritances, then the idea. (a) EPAS1: a five-SNP haplotype matching the Denisovan sequence sits at high frequency in Tibetans, on the order of 87 percent of sampled Tibetan chromosomes, and is essentially absent in Han Chinese. The Tibetan Plateau is habitable partly on borrowed genes from people who got there first. (b) The chromosome 3 haplotype inherited from Neanderthals that was the major genetic risk factor for severe COVID-19, carried by roughly half of people in South Asia and about one in six in Europe. A Pleistocene meeting reached into an intensive care unit in 2020. (c) The cost side, which is the part popular accounts skip: Neanderthal ancestry is depleted about fivefold on the X chromosome and near testis-expressed genes, which reads as reduced male hybrid fertility. The merger was real, common, and biologically expensive. Then the idea: for the whole of recorded history, "human" and "our species" have been the same word. That identity is roughly forty thousand years old, it is the exception rather than the rule in the history of the genus, and it is partly a fiction anyway, because nearly everyone alive is a small percentage of somebody else's lineage. We are not the summit of the family. We are its only surviving branch, and we are made partly of the others.

## 5. Factual traps

The highest-value section. These are the confident-sounding wrong things a writer will otherwise reach for.

1. **Trap:** Denisova 11 was discovered in 2016 to be a Neanderthal-Denisovan hybrid.
   **Truth:** two papers, two years, two different molecules. In 2016 collagen fingerprinting identified the fragment as hominin and the *mitochondrial* genome identified it as Neanderthal. Mitochondria pass only through mothers. The Denisovan father appeared only when the *nuclear* genome was published in 2018.
   **Source:** Brown et al., Scientific Reports 6:23559, 2016, DOI 10.1038/srep23559 (Crossref metadata verified: 12 authors, Brown, Higham, Slon, Pääbo, Meyer, Douka, Brock, Comeskey, Procopio, Shunkov, Derevianko, Buckley). Slon et al., Nature 561(7721):113-116, 2018, DOI 10.1038/s41586-018-0455-x (PubMed 30135579, abstract verified).

2. **Trap:** "Neanderthals died out around 40,000 BCE," or any silent conversion of a BP figure into a BCE figure.
   **Truth:** the Mousterian ends across Europe at 41,030 to 39,260 calibrated years *before present*, which is roughly 39,000 to 37,300 BCE. Every "years ago" number in this literature is BP. The node's own date field commits this slippage (see section 8). Convert explicitly or stay in "years ago" throughout the lesson.
   **Source:** Higham et al., "The timing and spatiotemporal patterning of Neanderthal disappearance," Nature 512(7514):306-309, 2014 (abstract verified at the Oxford Research Archive).

3. **Trap:** Homo floresiensis survived until about 12,000 or 18,000 years ago, and overlapped with farming-era humans on Flores.
   **Truth:** the original short chronology was withdrawn. Revised stratigraphy puts the skeletal remains at about 100,000 to 60,000 years ago and the associated stone artefacts at about 190,000 to 50,000. A 2018 follow-up finds no in-situ floresiensis or associated large fauna after roughly 50,000, with a habitat and faunal turnover near 60,000. Write "the fossils stop around sixty thousand years ago and the tools around fifty," not "they lived until twelve thousand."
   **Source:** Sutikna et al., Nature 532:366-369, 2016, DOI 10.1038/nature17179 (PubMed 27027286, full author list and dates verified). Sutikna et al., Journal of Human Evolution, 2018, spatio-temporal distribution paper (findings verified through secondary reporting; **confirm volume and pages before citing**).

4. **Trap:** Denisovans are known only from a finger bone and a few teeth in one Siberian cave, and remain faceless and elusive.
   **Truth:** true in 2010, stale by 2025. The Xiahe mandible from Baishiya Karst Cave on the Tibetan Plateau, at least 160,000 years old, was assigned to Denisovans by ancient protein analysis in 2019. Penghu 1, a male mandible dredged from the seabed in the Penghu Channel off Taiwan, was assigned to Denisovans by palaeoproteomics in April 2025 (4,241 amino acid residues across 51 proteins, two Denisovan-specific variants, sex confirmed from amelogenin Y). And on 18 June 2025 two papers assigned the nearly complete Harbin cranium, more than 146,000 years old and previously named Homo longi, to a Denisovan population: 95 endogenous proteins with three Denisovan-derived variants clustering with Denisova 3, and Denisovan mitochondrial DNA recovered from dental calculus. Denisovans now have a face, a range from the Altai to the subtropics, and a taxonomic fight.
   **Source:** Chen et al., Nature 569(7756):409-412, 2019, DOI 10.1038/s41586-019-1139-x (PubMed 31043746, verified). Tsutaya et al., Science, 2025, DOI 10.1126/science.ads3888. Fu and colleagues, Science, 2025, DOI 10.1126/science.adu9677, and Cell, 2025, DOI 10.1016/j.cell.2025.05.040. **The Science and Cell landing pages returned 403 in this pass; verify author lists at the DOI record before naming authors in prose.**

5. **Trap:** Africans carry no Neanderthal DNA; only non-Africans do.
   **Truth:** African individuals carry on average about 17 megabases of Neanderthal sequence, roughly 0.3 percent of the genome, attributed largely to back-migration into Africa and to early modern-human gene flow *into* Neanderthals. Non-Africans carry roughly 1 to 2 percent. The honest sentence is "nearly everyone alive carries some," and the honest framing is that the gene flow ran both ways.
   **Source:** Chen, Wolf, Fu, Li and Akey, "Identifying and Interpreting Apparent Neanderthal Ancestry in African Individuals," Cell, 2020 (findings verified through the Cell landing page listing; **confirm volume, issue and pages at the DOI record before printing them**).

6. **Trap:** Melanesians are "4 to 6 percent Denisovan," full stop, or Denisovan ancestry came from a single meeting.
   **Truth:** the 2010 estimate was 4.8 plus or minus 0.5 percent for Melanesians, with up to 7.4 plus or minus 0.8 percent archaic in total. Later work finds Papuans carry ancestry from at least two deeply divergent Denisovan lineages that had separated from each other by hundreds of thousands of years, so "Denisovan" is a population label covering a very wide, very old family. Give a range and name the method, or give one paper's number and attribute it.
   **Source:** Reich et al., Nature 468(7327):1053-1060, 2010, DOI 10.1038/nature09710 (verified at PMC4306417). Jacobs et al., "Multiple Deeply Divergent Denisovan Ancestries in Papuans," Cell, 2019 (**confirm volume and pages at the DOI record**).

7. **Trap:** Neanderthals laid flowers on their dead, as shown by the Shanidar 4 burial.
   **Truth:** the pollen clumps are better explained by burrowing animals. Sommer proposed Persian jirds in 1999; a 2023 re-evaluation of the palynology favours animal transport, with burrowing bees also proposed. The genuinely load-bearing evidence at Shanidar is different and better: a tight cluster of at least four individuals within about a metre, and Shanidar Z, an articulated partial skeleton lying in a cut depression and covered rapidly. Use the bodies, not the bouquet.
   **Source:** Hunt et al., "Shanidar et ses fleurs? Reflections on the palynology of the Neanderthal 'Flower Burial' hypothesis," Journal of Archaeological Science, 2023 (title and thrust verified via the ScienceDirect listing; **confirm volume, article number and full author list**). Pomeroy et al., Antiquity, 2020, on Shanidar Z (**confirm volume and pages**).

8. **Trap:** Neanderthals painted the caves of Spain; it is settled.
   **Truth:** it is one of the liveliest fights in the field. Uranium-thorium dating of carbonate crusts gave minimum ages beyond 64,800 years for a rectangular sign at La Pasiega, hand stencils at Maltravieso and red traces on stalagmites at Ardales, which would put them at least twenty millennia before modern humans reached Iberia. Critics argue open-system uranium behaviour, pointing out that the other side of the same La Pasiega figure returned an age near 3,100 years. Write "dated by one team to at least 64,800 years and disputed by others on the chemistry," and go instead to the evidence that is not contested.
   **Source:** Hoffmann et al., Science 359(6378):912-915, 2018, DOI 10.1126/science.aap7778; Slimak et al., comment, Science, DOI 10.1126/science.aau1371; White et al., "Still no archaeological evidence that Neanderthals created Iberian cave art," Journal of Human Evolution, 2020. **All three landing pages returned 403 in this pass; confirm metadata at the DOI records.**

9. **Trap:** reaching for cave art at all when you need uncontested Neanderthal capability.
   **Truth:** two better facts, both robust. The Bruniquel Cave structures: two ring-like constructions of more than 400 deliberately broken stalagmite pieces, the larger roughly 6.7 by 4.5 metres, built 336 metres from the entrance in complete darkness, with traces of fire on them, dated by uranium series to 176,500 plus or minus 2,100 years ago, when nobody but Neanderthals was in Europe. And a 3-ply cord fragment of inner-bark fibre preserved on a stone tool at Abri du Maras in southeastern France, the earliest known cord, which implies twisting, plying and a working grasp of pairs and sets. Prefer these to the painted caves.
   **Source:** Jaubert et al., "Early Neanderthal constructions deep in Bruniquel Cave in southwestern France," Nature, 2016, DOI 10.1038/nature18291 (dimensions, distance, piece count and date verified via John Hawks's detailed account). Hardy et al., "Direct evidence of Neanderthal fibre technology and its cognitive and behavioral implications," Scientific Reports 10:4889, 2020, DOI 10.1038/s41598-020-61839-w.

10. **Trap:** modern humans arrived in Europe and the Neanderthals promptly disappeared; there was no real coexistence.
    **Truth:** the modelled overlap between Neanderthals and the earliest anatomically modern humans in Eurasia is 2,600 to 5,400 years at 95.4 percent probability. The Bacho Kiro individuals in Bulgaria, directly dated to roughly 45,900 to 42,600 years ago, carried Neanderthal ancestors about five to seven generations back, which is a great-great-great-grandparent. Coexistence was not a rumour; it was in people's families.
    **Source:** Higham et al. 2014 (as above). Hublin et al., "Initial Upper Palaeolithic Homo sapiens from Bacho Kiro Cave, Bulgaria," Nature, 2020. Hajdinjak et al., "Initial Upper Palaeolithic humans in Europe had recent Neanderthal ancestry," Nature, 2021. **Verify volume and pages for both Nature papers at their DOI records; nature.com would not open in this pass.**

11. **Trap:** "hobbit" is a species name, or Homo floresiensis was a diseased modern human.
    **Truth:** the nickname is a press and excavator coinage after Tolkien and belongs in quotation marks or not at all. LB1 stood about 106 cm with a brain roughly a third the size of ours. The pathology hypothesis (microcephalic modern human) is now a minority position, and the decisive problem is chronological: hominin fossils at Mata Menge on the same island date to about 700,000 years ago and are smaller still, which no modern-human pathology can explain.
    **Source:** Smithsonian Institution Human Origins Program, Homo floresiensis page (verified: 2003 discovery, 100,000 to 60,000 fossils, 190,000 to 50,000 tools, about 3 feet 6 inches, brain about a third of ours, tools and hunting attested). Kaifu et al., Nature Communications, 2024, DOI 10.1038/s41467-024-50649-7.

12. **Trap:** Neanderthals had bigger brains, so they were as smart or smarter; or the reverse, that they lacked language.
    **Truth:** endocranial volume does not resolve to cognition, and neither claim is supportable from bone. What the evidence supports is capability parity in the domains that fossilize (fire, adhesives, cordage, deep-cave construction, treatment of the dead) and a demographic difference, not a mental one. If the lesson wants a difference, put it in population size and connectivity, which is exactly where the atlas's own `spark-of-mind` lesson already put it.

13. **Trap:** Denny's parents were an extraordinary encounter between two isolated species.
    **Truth:** the authors argue the opposite from first principles: recovering a first-generation offspring among the tiny number of archaic genomes ever sequenced implies mixing was common when these groups met. Her father's genome itself carried Neanderthal ancestry, so his line had already mixed. The lesson's emotional punch should be ordinariness, not miracle.
    **Source:** Slon et al. 2018, abstract verified.

**Additional UNVERIFIED items to confirm or drop:** the Baishiya monk provenance story (section 2, alternate 2); the exact number of bone fragments Brown screened; the Liang Bua discovery-day details; the frequently cited "Neandertal Demise: An Archaeological Analysis of the Modern Human Superiority Complex" (Villa and Roebroeks, PLoS ONE, 2014), which is a useful framing for trap 12 but whose citation I did not resolve in this pass.

## 6. Callback design

Legal earlier targets. Written lessons, all read for this brief: `spark-of-mind`, `great-dispersal`, `firelight-economy`. Unwritten peers, usable only through their summaries and better spent in `threadsOut`: `first-of-our-kind`, `the-tended-fire`, `ochre-for-the-dead`, `ash-across-asia`.

1. **`spark-of-mind` (strongest; use it).** That lesson argues that symbols in shell and ochre are the fingerprint of language-capable minds, and it lands on a specific claim in its own words: what changed later "was never the quality of human minds" but "the size and connectedness of the populations doing the accumulating." This node is the hardest possible test of that. Neanderthals broke and stacked 400 stalagmites 336 metres underground 176,500 years ago, twisted three-ply cord, and buried their dead, and they still ended. If capability was not the difference, the reader is forced onto the very variable that lesson named: connectivity. Then supply the arithmetic that lesson could not: Thorin's lineage isolated for about 50,000 years while other Neanderthals lived nearby; an Altai Neanderthal whose parents were as related as half-siblings; against a lineage arriving in waves with more behind it. The callback is a genuine comparison and it strengthens both lessons.

2. **`great-dispersal` (strong; use it as the second).** That lesson's central claim is that humans "have no range," and that culture, not biology, made every biome home. This node complicates it beautifully in two directions. First, the archaic lineages had enormous ranges too: Neanderthals from Portugal to the Altai, Denisovans from a Siberian cave at 700 m to Baishiya at over 3,000 m to subtropical Taiwan, and an early Homo lineage that got onto Flores across water. Range-making was not our invention. Second, and better, the dispersal lesson's own frontier logic gets an unexpected footnote: the Tibetan Plateau is livable for Tibetans partly because of an EPAS1 haplotype inherited from Denisovans. Part of the map our lineage filled was filled on borrowed genes from the people who were already there. That is a real contrast with real teeth, and it is not trivia.

3. **`firelight-economy` (best alternate).** Its single most important caution is that the Ju/'hoansi "are not living fossils, and the Kalahari is not a time machine." The same error, in a different costume, is the entire popular image of Neanderthals: a contemporary lineage read as an earlier stage of us. Both are the ladder mistake. Strong and genuinely comparative, but slightly meta and less concrete than 1 and 2, so hold it in reserve or spend it if another Era I lesson claims `great-dispersal`.

4. **`ash-across-asia` (not recommended as a callback; it is an incoming edge and its lesson is unwritten).** The comparison is nonetheless exact and should appear somewhere in prose or in `threadsOut`: Jwalapuram's toolmakers are anonymous because stone carries no molecules, and this node is where molecules arrive. If a callback to it is ever needed, ground it strictly in that node's summary sentence that "their species is unknown."

## 8. Node-data flags

Precise problems in the committed graph. Do not edit any repo file; report them.

1. **The date field mixes BP and BCE.** `c. 250,000 to 40,000 BCE` reads as if it were converted, but both numbers are BP figures from the literature. The Denisovan sediment DNA signal begins about 250,000 years *ago* (roughly 248,000 BCE) and the Mousterian ends at 41,030 to 39,260 cal *BP* (roughly 39,000 to 37,300 BCE). As written, the field's end date of 40,000 BCE is about 42,000 BP, which is *before* the modelled end of the Neanderthal record. Harmless for map placement; dangerous if the lesson reprints it. Recommend the lesson stay in "years ago" throughout and never echo the field.

2. **Summary: "Until roughly 50,000 years ago several human lineages overlapped."** Off by about ten thousand years at the near end. Neanderthals persist to 41,030 to 39,260 cal BP; the youngest Denisovan at Denisova Cave is modelled at 52,000 to 76,000; Homo floresiensis artefacts run to about 50,000. "Until roughly 40,000 years ago" is the defensible number, and it is also the more striking one.

3. **Hook: "the elusive Denisovans."** Accurate in 2012, stale in 2026. Since the node was written, Denisovans have acquired a mandible from the Tibetan Plateau (2019), a mandible from the seabed off Taiwan (2025), and a nearly complete cranium from Harbin (2025), the last of which had already been named as a separate species. Recommend Codex revisit the hook; the interesting fact now is not that they are elusive but that they turned out to be everywhere and to have been sitting in a museum drawer under another name.

4. **Summary: "known first from a single Siberian cave."** The word "first" saves this, and it should be kept. Flagging only so a reviewer does not "fix" it into the present tense.

5. **Summary: "most people today carry Neanderthal or Denisovan DNA."** Conservative to the point of being misleading. With African individuals carrying on average about 0.3 percent Neanderthal sequence, the defensible claim is that essentially everyone alive carries some. Recommend "nearly everyone alive."

6. **Hook: "a meter-tall people on one Indonesian island."** Accurate for Flores, where LB1 stands about 106 cm, but it frames small-bodied island hominins as a single freak occurrence. Homo luzonensis, described from Callao Cave on Luzon in 2019 and dated to at least 50,000 to 67,000 years ago, makes it a pattern. The lesson should carry Luzon even if the hook does not; Codex may want to widen the hook to "islands."

7. **Edge `the-eyed-needle`, why text: "Neanderthals endured glacial Europe with cold-weather skill our lineage would extend once it learned to sew fitted clothing."** Defensible and well phrased, but note there is no eyed needle in the Neanderthal record and no direct evidence of tailored clothing. The lesson should populate "cold-weather skill" with what is actually attested (fire, hide-working, hafting adhesives, three-ply cord) rather than implying proto-sewing.

8. **Graph architecture observation, not a defect.** All three outgoing edges stay inside Era I, and nothing later in the atlas wires back to archaic admixture, so the reader who learns here that they are a few percent Neanderthal never meets that fact again. If any later node touches genetics, high-altitude adaptation, or the Tibetan Plateau, a long wire from this node would pay off. Codex decision.

9. **Threads.** `ideas` and `craft` are the right two, and the lesson should say both in words. There is no `power` or `wealth` content here and none should be manufactured.
