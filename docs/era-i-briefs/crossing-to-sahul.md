# Lesson brief: crossing-to-sahul

Research and architecture scaffolding for Fable. Not draft prose. Every claim below carries its evidentiary status. Where this brief and a source disagree, follow the source and say so in the completion report.

*Provenance: two independent research passes, merged. The second pass added the Gandini 2025 and Iasi 2024 genomics that put the chronology debate on both sides at once, the Cloggs Cave excavation that turns "continuous culture" from slogan into evidence, Madjedbebe's 300 km distance from the Phase 2 shoreline, the WALFA carbon-abatement landing, traps 11 to 13, and it cleared the Bradshaw author-list flag. Its significance paragraphs already deliver Madjedbebe's ten thousand artefacts, the ground-edge hatchets, the 65,000-year optical dates, the sinking-artefact critique, the never-bridged deep straits, and the Wallace Line. **The voyage scene and the basic Madjedbebe evidence are spent.** This node cannot re-run them. Its job is the other half of the crossing: what the far shore did with the next sixty-five thousand years, and how a single number about grains of sand became one of the loudest arguments in archaeology. Write the Sahul-side lesson.

## Preferred scene

**Madjedbebe rock shelter, western Arnhem Land, the 2015 excavation: Mirarr Traditional Owners and archaeologists working the same square metre of sand, with the sun deliberately kept off it.**

The register is a total inversion of `great-dispersal`'s open ocean: an enclosed, hot, meticulous, argumentative scene, indoors under a sandstone overhang, where the drama is a measurement. The physical stakes are exquisite and easy to dramatize: the whole 65,000-year claim rests on grains of quartz that must never be allowed to see daylight between the moment they are dug and the moment they are read, because sunlight resets the clock. Someone hammers an opaque tube into the section face in shadow, caps it, and tapes it. That is the scene's hinge object.

**Documentation ledger.**

- *Attested by excavation and publication:* the site is on Mirarr country in western Arnhem Land, inside the Jabiluka mineral lease and surrounded by Kakadu. It was excavated in 2012 and 2015 by a team led by Chris Clarkson (University of Queensland) under a landmark agreement with the Gundjeihmi Aboriginal Corporation that gave the Mirarr control and a right of veto over every aspect of the work. About 20 square metres were opened. More than 10,000 stone artefacts were recorded in place with a laser total station. More than 100 samples were taken for radiocarbon and optically stimulated luminescence dating, with thousands of individual sand grains measured. The lowest dense occupation band (Phase 2) is placed at 68.7 to 50.4 ka. It contained edge-ground hatchet heads, grinding stones, ground ochre including shaped ochre "crayons," and reflective mica-bearing pigment. Charred plant remains from 65 to 53 ka include roughly ten taxa across fruit and nut endocarp (*Buchanania*, *Canarium australianum*, *Pandanus*, *Persoonia falcata*, *Terminalia*), underground storage organs, and palm tissue (cf. *Livistona*); over 1,000 macrofossils were recovered. Seventeen grinding stones carry red pigment residue, thirteen of them from the Pleistocene phases. About 563 grinding-stone fragments across all phases.
- *Real named people are available, and this is the rarest asset in Era I.* **May Nango** and **Djaykuk Djandjomerr**, Mirarr Traditional Owners, are named co-authors on Hayes et al. 2022 in *Scientific Reports*, the paper on 65,000 years of grinding-stone use at their own country's shelter. Mark Djandjomerr and May Nango are named in the excavators' own 2017 public account of the dig; Djurrubu Aboriginal Rangers including Mitchum Nango worked the 2015 season. **You may use real names here.** Do not invent dialogue for them, do not put thoughts in their heads, and do not have them deliver exposition about their own antiquity. Show them working and deciding. If you want interior life, make the interior life belong to the sediment or to a named archaeologist's problem.
- *Palaeogeography, and the single most useful fact in the ledger:* during Phase 2, sea level stood about 50 metres below modern and **Madjedbebe was roughly 300 km from the nearest shoreline** (Hayes et al. 2022, verified in full text). The oldest people we can see in Sahul are not standing on a beach looking back at the boat. They are deep inland, already at home. Use this to break the beachhead cliché in the reader's head, and to make the point that the coastal sites of that era are all drowned, so the record we have is systematically biased toward the interior.
- *Climate, if a sensory detail is wanted:* Florin and colleagues (*Nature Ecology & Evolution*, 2021) read stable carbon isotopes in *Pandanus spiralis* nutshell from the hearths as a rainfall proxy across 65,000 years, and found that drier intervals correlate with peaks in exotic (long-distance) stone raw material and artefact discard at the site. Long-distance stone in the deepest layers means exchange, or very wide movement, from the beginning.
- *Reconstruction, and must be labelled as such in `storyContext`:* the weather, the order of the day, who said what, the specific square being dug on a specific afternoon, any emotion attributed to a named person.
- *Quantities to check before printing:* the 20 square metres, the 10,000 artefacts, the 100+ dating samples, and the Phase 2 bracket of 68.7 to 50.4 ka are all verified below and safe. **UNVERIFIED, do not print:** any "65,000 ± 5,700" style error term (I could not open the Clarkson 2017 paper itself; Crossref confirms only the citation). Write "about 65,000 years ago" and, if you want a bracket, use Hayes et al.'s Phase 2 span of 68.7 to 50.4 ka, which is verified. Also do not print a count of ochre pieces; the number that is verified is seventeen pigment-bearing grinding stones, not a total ochre count.

Why this scene: it dramatizes the node's actual mechanism, which is not paddling. It is *inference*. Everything this lesson knows about the crossing is inferred from where people ended up, because no raft from this age survives anywhere on Earth. A scene about extracting a date from sand, watched over by the descendants of the people who left the sand, is the honest dramatization of that, and it hands the story its pivot for free: the number comes back, and the number starts a fight that is still running in 2025.

### Alternate 1 (freshest evidence; strongest single image)

**Liang Metanduno, Muna Island off southeastern Sulawesi: a hand stencil whose fingers were deliberately narrowed into something like a claw, under a skin of calcite that has just been read with a laser.**

Published in *Nature* in 2026 by Oktaviana, Joannes-Boyau, Hakim, Burhan and colleagues, with Adam Brumm and Maxime Aubert as senior authors. Laser-ablation uranium-series dating of the overlying calcite returned 71.6 ± 3.8 ka, giving a minimum age of 67.8 ka for the motif beneath; a second stencil came in near 60.9 ka. Painting at the site continued for roughly 35,000 years afterwards.

Why it is tempting: it is the newest thing in the field, it is a human hand, it is in Wallacea rather than Australia, and it makes the "someone was already out there" point without a single word about rafts. Why it is a liability: **the species attribution is genuinely open.** John Hawks has argued in public that the null hypothesis should be the archaic hominins already documented in the region, possibly Denisovan-related, rather than assuming *Homo sapiens* newcomers, and notes the local stone-tool record shows no disruption until around 40 ka. If you open here you must carry that ambiguity through the whole lesson. My recommendation: use Liang Metanduno as the *second* movement of the story or the opening beat of significance, where the ambiguity is an asset rather than a trap.

### Alternate 2 (safest, no composite and no living person)

**Laili rock shelter, Timor-Leste: a scene about absence.** Shipton, Morley, Kealy, Norman, Boulanger, Hawkins, Litster, Withnell and O'Connor (*Nature Communications*, 2024) found sediment accumulating in the shelter with *no human occupation* between 59 and 54 ka, then an abrupt onset of intensive habitation at about 44 ka. A cold open inside a layer that proves nobody was home is the cleanest possible dramatization of how this field reasons, and it is entirely attested with no invented person at all. Weakest on human warmth; hold in reserve if the Madjedbebe scene reads as procedural.

### Scene to AVOID

**The voyage.** Rafts, straits, water rations, birds indicating land, the smell of smoke, first footfall on an empty continent. `great-dispersal` did all of it, beautifully, three nodes ago, and did it with a composite the reader already loves. Repeating it would make the atlas feel like it is stalling. Second thing to avoid: any opening built on hunting Diprotodon or other megafauna. The extinction debate is unresolved, it drags the lesson toward a story about killing, and Jason's atlas is explicitly not that. Third: do not open on a "mysterious" empty continent waiting to be discovered. The continent was a place; it simply had no people in it yet.

## Story movement

Six paragraphs, three movements, deliberately procedural where `great-dispersal` was elemental.

1. **The tube in the shadow.** Madjedbebe, 2015. The overhang, the heat, the sieve, the total station's red dot ticking off artefacts in three dimensions. Someone works in the shade of a tarpaulin and drives a light-proof tube into the section, because a single second of sunlight would wipe the clock. Establish that the thing being excavated is not an object but a *timestamp*.
2. **What comes out of the sand.** The hatchet with a ground edge. The grinding stones with red pigment worn into them. The mica that throws light. The pandanus kernels that took a stone tool and real patience to open, and the palm pith that took hours of roasting before it was food. Concrete, sensory, and all of it verified. Let the reader feel that this is a *household*, not a beachhead.
3. **The people whose country it is.** May Nango and Djaykuk Djandjomerr working the excavation; the Mirarr's veto; the fact that the site's name in the literature was wrong until senior Mirarr Traditional Owners corrected "Malakunanja II" to Madjedbebe. Understate it. One sentence of dignity beats three of praise.
4. **The number, and the fight.** The laboratory reads the grains. Sixty-five thousand. Land the reader on what that number does: it is roughly 15,000 years older than the consensus it walks into, and within a year serious archaeologists are arguing that termites moved the tools down through soft sand into grains older than themselves.
5. **The far side of the water, briefly.** One paragraph, not more, gesturing at what the date implies out west: a Sulawesi cave wall where a hand was stencilled with its fingers narrowed into a claw, under calcite that says at least 67,800 years, and nobody yet certain whose hand it was.
6. **Pivot.** End on the sand grain and the living owner in the same frame: a clock nobody can read without argument, and a people who never stopped living on top of it. The reader should arrive at the question significance answers: if the number is contested, what exactly *is* certain here, and why does it matter which century of the ice age we are talking about?

**Governing image: the grain of quartz.** Every grain is a tiny battery that charges from background radiation while buried and discharges the instant it sees sun. Bury it, and it starts counting. Dig it into daylight, and you have destroyed your own evidence. Return to it once at the fight and once at the close, changed: the second time, let it stand for the whole discipline, which can only read the past by keeping it in the dark.

**Words and framings to ban.**
- "Mysterious," "vanished," "lost civilisation," "Stone Age people," "primitive," "prehistoric" used as a synonym for "unsophisticated."
- "Discovered Australia." Nobody discovered it. They arrived, and then people lived there.
- "Unchanged for 65,000 years," "frozen in time," "living fossils," "the world's oldest living culture" as a bare phrase. See traps 7 and 8.
- Any framing where Sahul is a cul-de-sac, a waiting room, or a rehearsal for something that happens later in Europe. The reader should end this lesson feeling that the Pacific is a centre, not a margin.
- Do not call the crossing "the first sea voyage in history" without the qualifier. It is the oldest widely accepted evidence of deliberate seafaring, which is a different and more honest claim.
- "Walkabout." "Dreamtime" (prefer "the Dreaming," and better still name a specific tradition, such as GunaiKurnai *mulla-mullung* practice or the Seven Sisters, rather than a pan-Aboriginal abstraction). "Isolated" or "cut off" as a description of Aboriginal Australia before 1788: see trap 11.
- The past tense applied to living peoples. Mirarr, GunaiKurnai, Yolŋu, Martu, Gunditjmara and Balanggarra people are present-tense nouns.
- **The deficit model in any form.** Do not organise a single sentence around what these societies lacked. No "without writing, they nevertheless…", no "despite having no agriculture," no "although they never developed X." Every such construction makes Eurasia the measuring stick, which is exactly what this node exists to refuse. Name specific peoples wherever the sources allow; "Aboriginal Australians" as a single undifferentiated actor is the grammatical form of the same mistake, given 250-plus languages and pre-Holocene population structure inside Sahul.

## Significance spine

Four to six paragraphs. Six movements below; compress 5 and 6 if the word budget bites.

1. **What is certain, and it is not the date.** Wallacea, the belt of islands between Sunda and Sahul, was *never* connected to either continent by dry land, at any sea level, in the whole Pleistocene. Kealy, Louys and O'Connor state it flatly in the opening line of their 2018 abstract: the Australian record is the earliest indirect evidence for maritime crossings by early modern humans precisely because the islands to the northwest "have never been connected to the mainland." So whatever the date, somebody built craft and crossed water out of sight of at least one shore. O'Connell and colleagues, who are the *sceptics* on the 65,000 figure, concede in the same breath that the peopling "required multiple crossings, including one >70 km" and paddle- or sail-powered vessels able to hold a heading against contrary currents. Bird and colleagues put at least one crossing at approaching 100 km. **The crossing is not in dispute. Only its century is.** Lead with that; it is the single most useful sentence in the lesson.

2. **How we know, and why sand is the whole argument.** The 65,000-year claim does not rest on radiocarbon, which runs out of usable signal around 50,000 years and is precisely why the older Australian record was invisible for decades. It rests on single-grain optically stimulated luminescence: measuring, grain by grain, how long each quartz particle has been in the dark. That method's strength is its reach; its weakness is that it dates the *sediment*, not the tool, so the entire dispute reduces to one question, which the lesson should pose exactly this baldly: **did the tools and the grains go into the ground together?** Then give the evidence for a household rather than a scatter: the edge-ground hatchets, the seed grinding, the pigment-bearing grinders, the ten-plus plant taxa with real processing behind them (pandanus kernels extracted with stone tools, palm pith roasted for hours then pounded, tubers cooked and peeled with the charred peelings still in the deposit). And note the reach, in two directions. Inland: during Phase 2 Madjedbebe itself sat about 300 km from the sea, so the oldest occupation we can see is already deep in the interior, and the coastal camps of that era are under fifty metres of water. Upward: in the New Guinea half of Sahul, the Ivane Valley in the highlands, 2,000 metres up, was occupied 49,000 to 44,000 years ago, with nuts, yams and possible forest clearance. Sahul was not a coastal strip. People went in, and people went up, fast.

3. **The named live debate. Present both sides. Do not adjudicate.** In 2025 *Archaeology in Oceania* ran the argument back to back in one issue, volume 60, issue 2. **Jim Allen and James F. O'Connell, "Recent DNA Studies Question a 65 kya Arrival of Humans in Sahul" (pages 187 to 190):** recent genomics places Neanderthal introgression into all non-African populations *after* 50,000 years ago, so ancestral Sahul populations carrying Neanderthal DNA must have arrived after that date, and the 65,000-year claim has no support. (The genomics they lean on: Sümer and colleagues, *Nature* 638, 2025, dating the single Neanderthal admixture event to roughly 45,000 to 49,000 years ago from the Ranis and Zlatý kůň genomes. The same window is reached independently by Iasi, Chintalapati, Skov, Bossoms Mesa, Hajdinjak, Peter and Moorjani, *Science* 386(6727), 2024, eadq3010, who date the great bulk of Neanderthal gene flow to a single extended episode between 50,500 and 43,500 years ago, centring near 47,000.) **Peter Veth, Lisa Matisoo-Smith, Adam Brumm, Huw Groucutt and Eleanor Scerri, "Do Recent DNA Studies Refute a 65 kya Arrival of Humans in Sahul?" (pages 191 to 194):** the reply, arguing the DNA does not close the question because the molecular-clock assumptions being used to attack the archaeology are not themselves secure enough to overturn it.

   **Write the subtraction onto the page.** This is what makes the argument legible rather than academic, and no popular account does it. Every population outside Africa, Aboriginal Australians included, carries Neanderthal ancestry. If that ancestry was acquired around 47,000 years ago, and Sahul was settled 65,000 years ago, then the ancestors of Aboriginal Australians would have had to reach Australia roughly eighteen thousand years *before* acquiring DNA they demonstrably carry. That is the short chronology's sharpest weapon and it is not a quibble about sand. Then give the long chronology's two answers: challenge the clock (Veth et al. 2025), or run a *different* clock and get a different number. **Gandini and 26 colleagues, with Martin B. Richards as senior author, "Genomic evidence supports the 'long chronology' for the peopling of Sahul," *Science Advances* 11(48), December 2025, eady9493**, analysed 2,456 indigenous mitogenomes from Australia, New Guinea and Oceania and concluded for settlement by about 60 ka via at least two distinct routes, a major northern route from the Philippines and a minor southern one, explicitly against the 47-to-51-ka recombinational dating. **This is the freshest state of the argument and it means genetics is now on both sides, not one.** Do not write "DNA now shows."

   Behind that exchange sits the geoarchaeological objection: Martin Williams, Nigel Spooner, Kathryn McDonnell and O'Connell, *Geoarchaeology* 36(1), 2021, pages 92 to 108, argue that termite activity in seasonally wet tropical sand can build false stone lines and let heavy artefacts settle into older grains, and that Madjedbebe's radiocarbon anomalies (including a calibrated date near 12,800 to 13,000 years from a Phase 2 deposit) fit disturbance better than antiquity. Note also that in 2017 Peter Hiscock, a long-standing sceptic of early Australian dates, publicly accepted the new Madjedbebe work as the earliest reliable date for occupation. The argument is not a bad-faith holdout; it is a real disagreement between careful people about how sand behaves. **No verdict.** The honest range to leave the reader holding is roughly 50,000 to 65,000, with the archaeology pulling early and most of the genetics pulling late.

4. **Mechanism: why then, why there, and what it caused.** Three pieces, all quantified. *Why the water was crossable at all:* sea level. Kealy and colleagues' Wallacea-adjusted curve puts 65 ka at 52.5 metres below present, the lowest and best-connected moment in the whole 40-to-70 ka window, and 70 ka at only 25 metres below present. Their models find that a southern route through Timor only becomes competitive when sea level is above about 40 to 42 metres below present; below that, the northern chain through Sulawesi to a landfall near present-day Misool wins on every run. Bird and colleagues, modelling drift and paddling instead of least-cost walking, favour a Timor-to-Australia crossing, and their 2019 paper concludes that choosing a departure time and making even minimal headway toward a destination transforms the odds of arrival, which is why they title it "Early human settlement of Sahul was not an accident." **Two good models, two different answers, and the honest position is that we do not know which door they came through.** *How many people:* Bradshaw and colleagues, *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 3, 2019, pages 1057 to 1063, model a founding census population of 1,300 to 1,550 to keep quasi-extinction risk at or below 0.1, arriving either at once or as repeated voyages of at least 130 people over roughly 700 to 900 years. That is the number the `tribe-of-strangers` edge needs: a crossing is not a stunt, it is an institution. *What they arrived at:* Norman and colleagues, *Quaternary Science Reviews* 324, 2024, show that during Marine Isotope Stage 4 (which contains 65 ka) the now-drowned Northwest Shelf off Australia was itself an extensive archipelago, later a fully exposed shelf with an inland sea and a freshwater lake, capable of supporting on the order of 50,000 to 500,000 people at various times. The far shore was not a wall of red desert. It was another archipelago, and most of it is now under water. *What it caused:* Crabtree, White, Bradshaw, Saltré, Williams, Beaman, Bird and Ulm, *Nature Human Behaviour* 5, 2021, built 125 billion candidate pathways over a high-resolution model of Sahul and found that a handful of "superhighways" emerge from nothing but terrain and landscape prominence, with the whole continent, every ecological zone, filled in 156 to 208 human generations, roughly 4,368 to 5,599 years.

5. **Threads, named in words.** This node's pigments are **craft** and **ideas**, and the lesson must say both out loud. *Craft:* the vessel that leaves no trace, and then the things that do. A hatchet with a ground edge is a different order of object from a flaked one, because grinding is slow, deliberate, and only worth doing for a tool you intend to resharpen and keep. Madjedbebe's excavators claim the earliest known manufacture of edge-ground hatchets in the world, and the same assemblage carries the earliest evidence for seed grinding outside Africa. *Ideas:* pigment ground on stone and mica chosen for the way it throws light are not subsistence; they are meaning, in the deepest layer of the site, at the moment of arrival. And the idea-craft that matters most on this continent leaves no artefact at all: knowledge stored in song and law, keyed to places, so that geography, genealogy and obligation travel together. Say plainly that we cannot excavate a songline and that its antiquity is argued, then give the reader the one testable version of that argument in movement 6.

6. **Why the modern world still carries this node, and the end of the "vanished" frame.** Six concrete inheritances, in ascending order of weight; cut from the middle if the budget bites. (a) *Managed country.* Rhys Jones coined "fire-stick farming" in *Australian Natural History* in 1969; Bliege Bird and colleagues, *PNAS*, 2008, gave it its first quantitative test, showing that Martu burning in the Western Desert produces a finer-grained mosaic of successional stages than lightning fire does, and that hunters take roughly a quarter more food from the patchwork they make. The mechanism is small, cool, early-season, patchy burns that break up fuel and pre-empt the enormous late-season fires that otherwise sweep the savanna. Over tens of millennia that is not use of a landscape; it is manufacture of one. (a2) *And it is currently a traded commodity.* Since 2006 the West Arnhem Land Fire Abatement project has run exactly that regime across roughly 28,000 square kilometres, operated by Indigenous ranger groups (Djelk, Warddeken, Mimal and others), registered as an eligible offsets project, and generating Australian carbon credit units by preventing late-season wildfire. A technology tens of thousands of years old is presently priced per tonne of avoided carbon dioxide. That is the sharpest available closing image for "the modern world still carries this node," and it is better than any adjective. (b) *Engineering.* At Budj Bim in southwestern Victoria the Gunditjmara cut channels, weirs and dams through a lava flow to trap and hold kooyang, short-finned eel; McNiven, Crouch, Richards, Dolby and Jacobsen (*Journal of Archaeological Science* 39(2), 2012, pages 268 to 286) dated the Lake Condah fish traps, with an age near 6,600 years commonly cited from that work; Budj Bim was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2019, the first Australian property listed solely for its Indigenous cultural values. (c) *Memory that may be testable.* Nunn and Reid (*Australian Geographer* 47(1), 2016) collected stories from 21 coastal locations describing the sea coming in over land, and argued that their recurrence right around the continent is better explained by shared observation of postglacial drowning than by independent invention. Hamacher, Nunn, Gantevoort, Taylor, Lehman, Law and Miles (*Journal of Archaeological Science* 159, 2023, article 105819) made the same kind of case for Tasmanian traditions describing the flooding of the Bassian land bridge and a Great South Star identified as Canopus, over 12,000 years. Deep-orality dating is genuinely contested, and the critics should be named rather than gestured at: David Henige holds that the claim can be neither verified nor falsified, Peter Hiscock agrees, and the conventional expectation for reliable oral transmission is often put nearer 800 years. Present it as a live argument with named parties on both sides, not as a fact. (c2) *Transmission demonstrated rather than asserted, and this is the strongest card in the lesson.* At Cloggs Cave in the foothills of the Victorian Alps, David, Mullett, Wright and colleagues, with the GunaiKurnai Land and Waters Aboriginal Corporation credited as an author, excavated two buried miniature fireplaces dated to 11,000 and 12,000 years ago, each holding a trimmed Casuarina (she-oak) stick smeared with animal or human fat, placed in a low-temperature fire that burned briefly and was then rapidly buried (*Nature Human Behaviour* 8(8), 2024, 1481 to 1492). The configuration matches GunaiKurnai ritual installations recorded from living practitioners by the nineteenth-century ethnographer A. W. Howitt, in which a fat-smeared stick is set in a fire and a name chanted until the stick falls. The cave was never a residence but a secluded retreat for *mulla-mullung*, medicine men and women; there are essentially no vertebrate food remains in the deposit. The paper calls this 500 generations of transmission, and these are Australia's oldest known wooden artefacts. Where Nunn and Reid offer an inference from story to sea level, this offers an excavated object matching a written description of the same rite twelve thousand years later. It converts the lesson's central slogan into evidence, and it should be where the argument lands. (d) *Continuity you can measure.* Tobler and colleagues (*Nature* 544, 2017, pages 180 to 184) sequenced 111 mitochondrial genomes from historical Aboriginal hair samples in the South Australian Museum and found deep, geographically stable structure: a rapid initial settlement reaching southern Australia by 49,000 to 45,000 years ago, after which regional lineages stayed put through tens of millennia of climate and cultural change. Land the final paragraph here: at Madjedbebe the people whose ancestors left the deepest layer hold the veto over the excavation, corrected the site's name in the scientific literature, and are named co-authors on the paper about their own grinding stones. Every other node in this era ends with an absence. This one does not, and that is the point.

## Factual traps

Thirteen. Each is a confident-sounding wrong thing a writer will otherwise reach for. This is the highest-value section of the brief.

1. **Trap:** People walked to Australia over a land bridge when sea levels fell.
   **Truth:** Sahul (Australia, New Guinea, Tasmania and the Aru Islands as one landmass) was joined internally by exposed shelf, but it was never joined to Asia. Wallacea, the island belt between, was never connected to either continent at any Pleistocene sea level. At least one open-water crossing was unavoidable.
   **Source:** Kealy, Louys and O'Connor, *Journal of Human Evolution* 125 (2018), 59 to 70, abstract and introduction (open access PDF fetched and read); O'Connell et al., *PNAS* 115(34), 2018, 8482 to 8490.

2. **Trap:** Sea level was about 120 to 130 metres lower, so the gaps were small.
   **Truth:** That is the Last Glacial Maximum, around 20,000 years ago, tens of thousands of years *after* this node. Kealy and colleagues' Wallacea-adjusted curve puts 65 ka at 52.5 metres below present and 70 ka at only 25 metres below present. The crossing happened at an intermediate, harder sea level, not the deepest lowstand.
   **Source:** Kealy, Louys and O'Connor 2018, Section 3.1 and Figure 2 (built on the Lambeck and Chappell 2001 curve, adjusted for a 0.5 m/ka Wallacean uplift rate). Read directly from the PDF.

3. **Trap:** Humans reached Australia 65,000 years ago. Settled.
   **Truth:** It is one of the most actively contested dates in archaeology, and the argument got sharper, not softer, in 2025. Allen and O'Connell (*Archaeology in Oceania* 60(2), 187 to 190) argue that post-50-ka Neanderthal introgression in all non-African populations rules it out; Veth, Matisoo-Smith, Brumm, Groucutt and Scerri reply in the same issue (191 to 194). Williams, Spooner, McDonnell and O'Connell (*Geoarchaeology* 36(1), 2021, 92 to 108) argue termite bioturbation can produce exactly this pattern. Hedge the date once, properly, and then stop hedging. The node's own `date` field already says "c. 65,000 to 50,000 BCE," which is the right posture.
   **Source:** Crossref records for DOIs 10.1002/arco.70002 and 10.1002/arco.70005, both verified for title, authors, volume, issue, pages, year, plus the Allen and O'Connell abstract verbatim from Crossref; Geoarchaeology citation confirmed via search of the Wiley record and the ADS bibcode 2021Gearc..36...92W.

4. **Trap:** The 65,000-year date comes from carbon dating.
   **Truth:** Radiocarbon cannot reliably reach beyond roughly 50,000 years, which is the historical reason the older Australian record stayed invisible. The Madjedbebe age comes from single-grain optically stimulated luminescence on quartz, with more than 100 samples taken and thousands of individual grains measured, supported by radiocarbon in the younger phases. That is also why the debate is about sediment mixing rather than about contamination in the usual sense.
   **Source:** Clarkson, Marwick, Wallis, Fullagar and Jacobs, "Buried tools and pigments tell a new history of humans in Australia for 65,000 years," *The Conversation*, 2017 (fetched); Clarkson et al., *Nature* 547, 306 to 310, 2017 (Crossref-verified citation).

5. **Trap:** Madjedbebe is where the first Australians landed, so people entered through Arnhem Land.
   **Truth:** Madjedbebe is the oldest *dated* site, which is not the same thing, and the modelling points elsewhere and disagrees with itself. Kealy, Louys and O'Connor recover overwhelming support for a northern route through Sulawesi with a landfall near present-day Misool, off the New Guinea Bird's Head. Bird and colleagues, modelling drift and paddling, favour a southern Timor-to-Australia crossing; their 2019 paper nonetheless found arrival via New Guinea more likely than via northwestern Australia. Write it as an open question with two named model families, not as a route.
   **Source:** Kealy et al. 2018 (read in full); Bird, Condie, O'Connor, O'Grady, Reepmeyer, Ulm, Zega, Saltré and Bradshaw, "Early human settlement of Sahul was not an accident," *Scientific Reports* 9, article 8220, 2019 (citation and findings confirmed via multiple institutional repository records; the Nature-hosted page blocked my fetcher).

6. **Trap:** Madjedbebe holds the world's oldest ground-edge axe, full stop.
   **Truth:** The claim is real but entirely contingent on the disputed date. What is securely dated is younger: Hiscock, O'Connor, Balme and Maloney, "World's earliest ground-edge axe production coincides with human colonisation of Australia," *Australian Archaeology* 82(1), 2016, 2 to 11, report a Kimberley axe fragment in the 44,000 to 49,000-year range. The oldest edge-ground axes outside Australia, in Japan, are commonly given as roughly 35,000 years old, though I did not verify that figure against a publication. Phrase as "the earliest known manufacture of edge-ground hatchets, if the Madjedbebe dates hold," which is exactly how Hayes and colleagues phrase it.
   **Source:** Crossref record for DOI 10.1080/03122417.2016.1164379 (title, four authors, volume, issue, pages, year verified); Hayes, Fullagar, Field, Coster, Matheson, Nango, Djandjomerr, Marwick, Wallis, Smith and Clarkson, *Scientific Reports* 12:11747, 2022 (fetched via PMC). **UNVERIFIED: the Japanese ~35,000-year figure. Confirm or drop.**

7. **Trap:** Aboriginal Australians are "the world's oldest living culture," unchanged for 65,000 years.
   **Truth:** The defensible claim is about *continuity of occupation and connection to specific country*, not stasis. Tobler and colleagues found deep regional structure surviving tens of millennia, which is evidence of people staying in relationship with particular places, not of an unchanging culture: the same 50,000 years contain the peopling of a continent, the drowning of half the Northwest Shelf, the invention of the Budj Bim aquaculture system, the arrival of the dingo, and continuous artistic and technological change. `firelight-economy` already warns the reader, in its own words, that the Ju/'hoansi "are not living fossils, and the Kalahari is not a time machine." Apply the identical discipline here, and consider saying so explicitly.
   Two further precisions worth carrying. "Oldest" depends entirely on what is being counted; some southern African peoples have deeper ancestral continuity in place, and the phrase is doing comparative work it cannot support. And the defensible version of the claim is not rhetorical at all: it is Cloggs Cave, where a specific rite recorded from living GunaiKurnai practitioners in the nineteenth century turns up as an excavated object dated to 11,000 and 12,000 years ago. Prefer the node summary's own careful wording, "some of the world's longest continuous cultural traditions," and then earn it with the evidence rather than the superlative.
   **Source:** Tobler et al., *Nature* 544, 2017, 180 to 184 (citation and findings verified via multiple institutional records and the Nature landing metadata); David, Mullett, Wright et al., *Nature Human Behaviour* 8(8), 2024, 1481 to 1492, DOI 10.1038/s41562-024-01912-w (read in full text from the JCU open repository); SBS NITV, Luke Pearson, "What is a 'continuous culture' ... and are Aboriginal cultures the oldest?" (fetched and read); the atlas's own `data/lessons/firelight-economy.js`.

8. **Trap:** The first Australians were pure nomads who left no mark on the land.
   **Truth:** Three counterexamples, all dated. Madjedbebe's earliest occupants were processing pandanus kernels with stone tools, roasting and pounding palm pith, and cooking and peeling tubers from 65 to 53 ka. Martu burning demonstrably manufactures landscape structure and raises hunting returns by about 25 percent. The Gunditjmara reworked a lava flow into a kilometres-long aquaculture system for eels. Note the boundary: this is landscape management and aquaculture, not cereal agriculture, and the "Aboriginal Australians were farmers" argument is a live and heated Australian public dispute. Its poles are Bill Gammage's *The Biggest Estate on Earth* (2011), arguing the whole continent was managed as an estate, and Bruce Pascoe's *Dark Emu*, pushing toward agriculture and sedentism, against Peter Sutton and Keryn Walshe's *Farmers or Hunter-Gatherers? The Dark Emu Debate* (2021), arguing the evidence was overread. **Do not enter that dispute in a Loom lesson, and do not take a side.** The useful move, if you want one sentence on it, is the meta-point: the argument exists partly because the only compliment our vocabulary knows how to pay is "actually they were farmers," which is the deficit model wearing a better coat. Give the dated engineering and move on. **The publisher records for the Gammage, Pascoe and Sutton/Walshe books were not verified in this pass; name them only if you check them.**
   **Source:** Florin, Fairbairn and colleagues, *Nature Communications* 11, article 924, 2020 (fetched via PMC); Bliege Bird, Bird, Codding, Parker and Jones, *PNAS*, 2008, DOI 10.1073/pnas.0804757105; McNiven, Crouch, Richards, Dolby and Jacobsen, *Journal of Archaeological Science* 39(2), 2012, 268 to 286 (Crossref-verified).

9. **Trap:** The new Sulawesi hand stencil proves modern humans were in Wallacea by 67,800 years ago, which settles the 65,000-year date for Australia.
   **Truth:** Two separate over-reaches. First, uranium-series dating of the overlying calcite gives a *minimum* age for the art beneath it (71.6 ± 3.8 ka on the calcite, hence "at least 67,800" for the motif), not an age for the painting event. Second, the artist's species is not established: John Hawks has argued publicly that archaic hominins already documented in the region, possibly Denisovan-related, are the better null hypothesis, noting that the local stone-tool record shows no cultural disruption until around 40 ka. Use the finding, and use the ambiguity; it is more interesting than certainty and it feeds the `the-other-humans` edge.
   **Source:** Oktaviana, Joannes-Boyau, Hakim, Burhan, Sardi, Adhityatama, Jalandoni and 30+ colleagues, with Adam Brumm and Maxime Aubert as final authors, "Rock art from at least 67,800 years ago in Sulawesi," *Nature* 650, 2026, DOI 10.1038/s41586-025-09968-y (Crossref-verified for title, full author order, journal, volume, year); dating figures and site from the Scitechdaily and National Geographic accounts (both fetched); the attribution caution from johnhawks.net (fetched).

10. **Trap:** The site is called Malakunanja II.
    **Truth:** That name was applied in error in the earlier scientific and popular literature. The site is Madjedbebe, the name given by senior Mirarr Traditional Owners, and the correction is itself a small, precise illustration of who holds knowledge of this country. Do not use "Malakunanja II" except, if at all, as a parenthetical about the correction.
    **Source:** Clarkson and colleagues' 2015 *Journal of Human Evolution* paper carries both names in its title ("Madjedbebe (Malakunanja II)"); the correction and its provenance are stated in the site literature and by the Gundjeihmi Aboriginal Corporation. **Confirm the wording of the correction against the 2015 JHE paper or mirarr.net before printing a sentence about it.**

11. **Trap:** Sahul was sealed off from the rest of the world for fifty thousand years until 1788.
    **Truth:** flatly false, and it is the trap most likely to slip in unnoticed, because "isolation" is doing heavy rhetorical work in the story `great-dispersal` tells about the sealed hemisphere of the Americas. **Do not import that frame here.** The dingo arrived with people from outside: direct radiocarbon dating of dingo bone from Madura Cave on the Nullarbor gives 3,348 to 3,081 years ago. Macassan trepang fleets out of Sulawesi worked the Arnhem Land coast in partnership with Yolŋu people, with Dutch records describing a well-established trade by 1754 and Campbell Macknight's revised assessment putting the industry in full swing by the 1780s; the exchange left rock paintings of praus, tamarind trees, stone-lined processing sites, and loanwords in Yolŋu languages. Torres Strait exchange with New Guinea ran continuously. Continuity of occupation is not the same thing as isolation, and conflating them is how a lesson accidentally turns a connected world into a terrarium.
    **Source:** the Madura Cave dingo dating, *Scientific Reports* 2018, DOI 10.1038/s41598-018-28324-x (**author list not independently verified in this pass; confirm before naming anyone**); National Museum of Australia, "Trade with the Makasar," and the ANU Press volume *Macassan History and Heritage* for the Macknight chronology (**both refused automated fetch; confirm the 1754 and 1780s figures before printing them**).

12. **Trap:** The first Australians hunted the megafauna to extinction, and this is settled.
    **Truth:** it is a second live debate and the spec allows one, so the honest options are to give it two sentences or to leave it out. Saltré and 17 colleagues (*Nature Communications* 7, 2016, article 10511) analysed 659 megafauna fossil ages and found the extinctions broadly synchronous, independent of climate aridity and variability, and roughly 13,500 years after human arrival; other researchers read the same record as climate-driven or as showing long coexistence. And the burnt *Genyornis* eggshell that anchored the human-burning evidence had its identity reassigned to a giant megapode by Grellet-Tinner and colleagues in 2016, then reassigned back to *Genyornis* by Demarchi and 14 colleagues in *PNAS* in 2022 using ancient protein sequences. If you use any of this, use the whiplash rather than the verdict: it is a beautiful miniature of a field correcting itself twice on the same shell.
    **Source:** Saltré et al., DOI 10.1038/ncomms10511, and Demarchi et al., *PNAS* 119(43), 2022, e2109326119, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2109326119, both verified at Crossref including full author lists.

13. **Trap:** Sahul means Australia, and New Guinea is a footnote to it.
    **Truth:** Sahul included New Guinea and Tasmania, and its peoples had already diversified long before the Holocene. Malaspinas and 71 colleagues (*Nature* 538(7624), 2016, 207 to 214) found Aboriginal Australian and Papuan ancestors diverging from Eurasians 51 to 72 kya and from each other 25 to 40 kya, implying pre-Holocene population structure inside Sahul itself. New Guinea will go on to domesticate its own crops at Kuk, which the atlas gives an Era II node. Do not write New Guinea as Australia's annexe; the Ivane Valley highland occupation at 49,000 to 44,000 years is the same story's other half.
    **Source:** Crossref record for DOI 10.1038/nature18299 (title, first twelve authors, total of 72, volume, issue, pages, year verified); Summerhayes et al., *Science* 330(6000), 2010, 78 to 81, DOI 10.1126/science.1193130.

**Two further items I could not verify and that must be confirmed or dropped:** (a) any specific error term on the 65,000-year OSL age; (b) the exact number of ochre pieces recovered from Madjedbebe's lowest layer. `great-dispersal` already printed "thousands of pieces of worked ochre" for this site; I did not find a publication confirming that count in this pass, so if you repeat it, verify it, and if you cannot, use the verified figure instead (seventeen pigment-bearing grinding stones, thirteen of them Pleistocene).

**Further UNVERIFIED items from the second research pass. ## Callback design

Legal earlier targets, from the packet. Three have written lesson files, which I read; five are unwritten peers whose summaries are all I can ground a comparison in.

**Written (strongly preferred).**

1. **`great-dispersal`** (`data/lessons/great-dispersal.js`): **use it.** This is not optional; it is the parent lesson and the reader met it three nodes ago. The comparison must be a genuine contrast, not a recap. The sharpest one available: *that lesson asked how people got there; this one asks how we know, and the two questions have different answers with different certainties.* `great-dispersal` staged the crossing as a bet on inference made by the travellers, reading a standing cloud and a heavy bird. This lesson stages the crossing as a bet on inference made by *us*, reading a grain of quartz. In both cases the evidence is indirect, the conclusion is probabilistic, and someone has to decide whether to commit. Ask the reader to compare what Wara could not verify with what a dating lab cannot verify. The second-best angle, if that one feels too clever: `great-dispersal` ended by insisting that the whole planet was filled by people identical to us before anyone planted a seed. This lesson supplies the one continent where you can watch what those people did with sixty thousand uninterrupted years, and the answer is fish traps, fire mosaics, and law stored in song.

2. **`firelight-economy`** (`data/lessons/firelight-economy.js`): **use it as the second callback.** That lesson contains the exact intellectual antibody this one needs, and says it in a paragraph it calls "the single most important caution in this lesson": the Ju/'hoansi "are not living fossils, and the Kalahari is not a time machine," and anthropologists use them "carefully and provisionally, as one model." Ask the reader to apply that caution to Aboriginal Australia, where the temptation is stronger because the occupation really is continuous and really is ancient. The genuine comparison: continuity of *presence* is a fact about a place; continuity of *culture* is a claim about change, and the two get confused constantly. A reader who can hold that distinction has learned something transferable, which is the whole point of a callback.

3. **`spark-of-mind`** (`data/lessons/spark-of-mind.js`): **strong alternate, rank second.** The comparison is material and exact: Blombos gives ochre ground in an abalone shell, pierced shells worn on a cord, and a cross-hatched pattern drawn on a silcrete flake, all read as evidence of minds that maintain arbitrary shared symbols. Madjedbebe's deepest layer gives ochre ground on stone and mica selected for reflectance, on a continent nobody had ever seen before. The point to draw: the pigment is not a luxury that arrives after survival is secured, it is in the *first* layer, alongside the food. People did not become symbolic once they were comfortable. They arrived symbolic. Hold this in reserve; both prior Era I lessons already used `spark-of-mind` as a callback target, and a third would feel like a rut.

**Unwritten peers (ground strictly in the summary; use only if forced).**

4. **`the-other-humans`**. The best of the unwritten set, and it is the node that wires *into* this one. Its summary establishes Neanderthals, Denisovans, and *Homo floresiensis* overlapping with us until roughly 50,000 years ago, and that most people today carry Neanderthal or Denisovan DNA. The real comparison: Papuans and Aboriginal Australians carry roughly 4 percent Denisovan-related ancestry, and Jacobs and colleagues (*Cell* 177(4), 2019, 1010 to 1021) found not one Denisovan source but at least two deeply divergent lineages, separated for over 350,000 years. The people who crossed to Sahul did not enter an empty archipelago; they entered one whose earlier inhabitants are inside their descendants' genomes. Powerful, but the callback rules prefer written targets, so put this in `threadsOut` instead.

5. **`ash-across-asia`**. Usable. Its summary notes that Jwalapuram's tools sit above and below Toba's ash but cannot name the species that made them, so the site cannot by itself prove an early *Homo sapiens* coastal migration. That is precisely the Liang Metanduno problem transposed: a spectacular date attached to an unidentified hand. A comparison about *what a date does and does not tell you* would be genuinely good. Rank third overall, first among unwritten.

6. **`ochre-for-the-dead`**, **`the-tended-fire`**, **`first-of-our-kind`**. Weak here. Ochre is better spent through `spark-of-mind`; fire is better spent forward, on Australian cultural burning, not backward on Qesem; and Jebel Irhoud's mosaic-origins point has no clean hook into a maritime crossing. ## Node-data flags

Precise problems in the committed graph, in `data/eras/01-long-dawn.js` around line 152. **Do not edit any repo file. Report only.**

1. **The hook stages the scene another written lesson already owns.** "To reach the land that becomes Australia, someone must cross open sea with no far shore in sight. They build a craft, gather a crew, and gamble on the horizon." That is a one-sentence summary of `great-dispersal`'s entire story, published three nodes earlier. The hook is not factually wrong, but as a promise to the reader it sets up a repeat. Codex may want to re-point the hook at the sand, the date, or the sixty-five thousand years that followed. The lesson must, at minimum, not deliver what the hook promises in the same key.

2. **"With no far shore in sight" is defensible at sea level and wrong from a mountain.** Norman and colleagues showed that the paleoislands of the Sahul shelf were visible at 65 ka from Timor's mountaintops, and Kealy and colleagues distinguish "relative" intervisibility (land visible from somewhere at sea) from "absolute" shore-to-shore intervisibility, finding the latter absent between Timor and Sahul but the former present, and finding the northern chain intervisible throughout. So the crossing was almost certainly made toward land whose direction was known and whose existence had been seen from high ground, even though the crew could not see it from the beach. That is a *better* story than a blind gamble, and the lesson should tell it that way. Worth a hook revision.

3. **The summary's "and perhaps as early as 65,000" understates how live the argument now is.** The phrasing implies a settled floor of 50,000 with an optimistic ceiling. As of the end of 2025 the ceiling is being pushed from three directions at once: Allen and O'Connell arguing from Neanderthal introgression (and Iasi et al. 2024 in *Science*, dating the main gene flow to 50,500 to 43,500 years ago) that even 65,000 is impossible; Gandini and colleagues in *Science Advances*, December 2025, running 2,456 mitogenomes and getting about 60,000 by two routes, which puts genetics on *both* sides for the first time; and the 2026 Sulawesi rock-art dates arguing that somebody was making images in Wallacea by at least 67,800 years ago. The node's hedge is honest; it is just no longer current, and it is now hedging in the wrong direction, treating 65,000 as the outer edge when the outer edge has moved. No change strictly required, but Codex should know the field moved twice in eighteen months.

4. **"Their descendants sustain some of the world's longest continuous cultural traditions" invites the misreading it is trying to avoid.** The claim is defensible if "continuous" means continuity of occupation and of relationship with specific country, which is what the mitogenome regionalism actually shows. Read quickly, it says "unchanged," which is false and, worse, is the exact frame that turns living people into exhibits. The summary cannot carry the qualification in its word budget, so the lesson must.

5. **Region and meridian.** `region: 'easia'` resolves to "East Asia & the Pacific" (`data/graph-core.js` line 40), so the data is internally correct, but the atlas has no Oceania meridian at all: the Pacific shares a band with China, Japan and Korea. Since the expansion rationale for this node was explicitly "gives Oceania a dedicated node," Codex may eventually want a region split. Not this lesson's problem, but it does mean this lesson is the only place in the atlas where a reader can feel Oceania as its own world, which raises the bar on its closing paragraph.

6. **Graph shape.** The node has one incoming edge and three outgoing. Of the three targets, `paint-in-the-dark` and `into-the-last-continents` both leave Sahul immediately, and `tribe-of-strangers` is a global-mechanism node. There is no forward wire from `crossing-to-sahul` to any later Oceania or Australia node anywhere in the atlas, so a reader walking the graph forward from Sahul leaves the Pacific and never comes back until the Era II and Era III Lapita nodes, which are not wired to this one. The audit flags note the Lapita adjacency as a coordination item; the missing wire is arguably a defect. Worth a Codex decision.

7. **Date field versus story weight.** `date: 'c. 65,000 to 50,000 BCE'` and `sort: -52000` place the node at the crossing. But the summary's third sentence, and the most valuable half of the lesson, concern the following sixty-five thousand years, right up to a 2015 excavation and a 2025 journal exchange. That is the correct call for chart placement, and it means the lesson carries the whole burden of explaining that its subject runs from the ice age to last year. Say so early rather than letting the reader trip on it.
