**This node's job in the atlas, and the one thing that will sink the lesson if ignored.** Two written lessons already occupy most of this node's obvious ground. `tribe-of-strangers` opens with a woman touching "the strand of ostrich-eggshell beads at her throat," explains hxaro by name, and already delivers the bead-as-passport thesis. `firelight-economy` already delivers gift, delayed obligation, and debt-before-money. Both of those lessons are built from **twentieth-century Kalahari ethnography**, and both say so honestly. This node's distinct job is to supply **the excavated, dated, measured evidence** that those two lessons were modelling, and then to ask the hard epistemological question they deferred: can an institution documented among living people in the 1960s legitimately be read backward across 47,000 years? That question has a live, named, two-sided literature (section 4, movement 4). Write this lesson as the archaeology and the argument, not as a third helping of the anthropology. Its unique cargo is: the manufacturing sequence, the dated horizons, a 3,000 km stylistic signal, a strontium measurement of real transport distance, a climate mechanism for both the connection and its rupture, and the sample sizes that make all of it provisional.

## 2. Preferred scene

**A rockshelter under the Irangi Hills escarpment, Kondoa, north-central Tanzania (Kisese II), around 47,000 years ago: one person drilling ostrich eggshell blanks, and the hole going in before the bead is a bead.**

Why this site and not another. The node's date, c. 45,000 BCE, is about 47,000 years before present. Kisese II is the only site whose bead horizon has been directly and defensibly dated to that window: Tryon and colleagues report that "the oldest clearly worked OES beads occur in spit XX, associated with one 14C sample dated to 46.2 to 42.7 ka cal BP," with the first ostrich eggshell fragments of any kind in spit XXI at 46.9 to 43.1 ka cal BP. The node's date sits inside those brackets. No other well-published assemblage does that.

Documentation ledger.

- **Attested by excavation.** Kisese II is an east-facing overhang on one of two adjoining boulders roughly 200 m below the Irangi Hills escarpment, in what is now the UNESCO Kondoa Rock-Art Sites area, on the eastern margin of the Gregory Rift; mean annual rainfall around 850 mm, miombo woodland and acacia-commiphora bushland. The deposits run about 6 m deep and span roughly 47,000 years. The excavations recovered more than 5,000 complete ostrich eggshell beads, bead fragments and bead blanks, over 5,900 lithic artefacts, taxonomically diverse fauna, and ochre-processing evidence through multiple layers. (Tryon et al. 2018, fetched.)
- **Attested elsewhere, safe to import into the scene.** The manufacturing sequence. In the dominant pathway the maker fragments the shell, **drills the aperture first**, then trims the outer rim to a rough disc, then grinds it smooth, frequently with many blanks strung together and finished as a batch (Kuhlase and Forssman 2024, fetched, eight stages; batch finishing is the subject of Werner and Surette's 2026 experimental paper, verified by DOI record). The failure rate is attested at Enkapune ya Muto: 13 complete beads against 12 preforms "broken during drilling or grinding" (Ambrose 1998, read in full from PDF). Ostrich eggshell is roughly 2 mm thick; the egg holds about 1.4 litres and weighs about 1.4 kg (see trap 12 on how to phrase this).
- **Reconstruction.** Any named individual, any dialogue, weather, gender of the maker, the occasion, who received the string, the distance it travelled. Kisese II has no writing and no burial that names anyone. `storyContext` must say plainly that the person is a composite assembled from the Kisese II deposits and the published manufacturing sequence.
- **No real named person is available** for the past at this date anywhere in this evidence base. Real named people exist only on the excavation side: Louis and Mary Leakey visited Kisese II in 1935 to study the rock paintings and excavated a roughly 5.3 square metre trial trench to 4 m in 1951; Raymond Inskeep expanded it to 21.3 square metres and about 6 m depth in 1956. That is a legitimate alternate frame (see Alternate 2), not a way to smuggle a named Pleistocene character in.
- **Quantities to check before printing.** Do not state a bead count for the deepest spit; the >5,000 figure is the whole 47,000-year sequence, not one layer. Do not state a bead diameter for Kisese II from memory. Published mean diameters that ARE verified: eastern Africa 6.9 ± 1.2 mm external, 2.6 ± 0.6 mm aperture; southern Africa in later phases 4.5 ± 0.9 mm external, 1.8 ± 0.4 mm aperture (Miller and Wang 2022). Ga-Mohana Hill North at about 15 ka: mean external 4.4 mm, mean aperture 1.4 mm, mean thickness 1.6 mm (Hatton et al. 2022). Little Muck, much later: mean external 5.1 mm, aperture 1.9 mm (Kuhlase and Forssman 2024).

**What the scene must dramatize:** that the hole comes first. The maker is not making a disc and then piercing it. She is making a hole and then making a disc around it, because the object's whole point is the string it will hang on and the person it will hang on after that. Everything the lesson is about is in that inversion.

### Alternate 1 (richest human detail, wrong by a few thousand years)

**Enkapune ya Muto, "Twilight Cave," on the Mau Escarpment west of Lake Naivasha, Kenya, 36 degrees 09 minutes E, 0 degrees 50 minutes S, elevation 2,400 m.** Ambrose's stratum DBL1, associated with the Sakutiek industry, is a working bead floor: 13 complete beads, 12 preforms broken during drilling or grinding, and 593 shell fragments in a layer 30 to 40 cm thick exposed over only 6 square metres, a density of about 11.9 beads and preforms per cubic metre against 1.48 in the whole Holocene sequence above. Virtually every flaked stone artefact at the site is obsidian from sources roughly 10 km away. This is the single most vivid scene available: a workshop with its failures still lying where they broke. Its liability is the date. Ambrose's figure is 39,900 ± 1,600 **uncalibrated radiocarbon years BP** on the eggshell carbonate, which is not 40,000 calendar years (trap 6). Use this as the second movement or a single imported detail, and if you import the broken preforms into a Kisese II scene, say in `storyContext` that the manufacturing detail comes from Enkapune ya Muto.

### Alternate 2 (safest, no invented person at all)

Open in 1956, in Inskeep's trench, and read the section downward: beads at the top, beads a metre down, beads four metres down, beads five metres down, the same object made the same way for longer than everything the atlas covers above this node. Everything in the scene is attested and nobody is invented. Weaker on the body, stronger on the vertigo of the timescale, and it sets up the whole lesson's real question, which is what a distribution of objects can and cannot tell you. Hold in reserve.

**A Kalahari waterhole meeting where two bands exchange beads.** `tribe-of-strangers` already opens with almost exactly that scene, down to the ostrich-eggshell strand at the throat. Repeating it would be self-plagiarism inside the atlas and would spend this node's ten minutes on material the reader already has. ## 3. Story movement

Six or seven paragraphs, one continuous working session, ending on the departure of the string.

1. **The shell.** Curved, thick, heavy for what it is, already broken. Establish that this is not a delicate material: it is closer to a plate than to a snail. Hands, grit, the sound.
2. **The hole first.** The drill point set on the concave inner face, the shaft rolled between the palms with downward pressure, the moment it breaks through. Then the next blank. Then the next. This is where the reader learns the inversion.
3. **The failures.** One splits under the drill. Another goes at the rim during trimming. Roughly as many failures as successes at the one floor where we can count. Let the reader feel that this is a skill with a cost, taught by someone, practised badly first.
4. **The string.** The blanks threaded, the whole string worked against a grinding stone at once so that the discs come out matched. This is the technical heart: uniformity is not an accident of the material, it is a decision executed on the batch. A found seashell has the shape the animal gave it. A disc has the shape someone chose, which is exactly why it can carry a style, and why style can carry information.
5. **The measure.** Someone checks the string against another string. The size is not arbitrary; it is the size everyone here makes. Introduce, without explaining it yet, that the sameness is the message.
6. **The departure.** The finished string is not worn. It is wrapped, carried out, walked away from. End the story at the moment it leaves the maker's hands and goes to a person she may not meet, in a place she may never see. That is the pivot: the reader should be sitting on the question the significance answers, which is how anyone today could possibly know where it went.

**Governing image: the hole before the bead.** Bring it back once at the departure ("the hole was always the point"), and once in the last paragraph of the significance. Do not overwork it.

**Words and framings to avoid.** "Trade," "barter," "currency," "proto-money," "primitive money," "commerce," "the world's first social network" (a press-release phrase, not the paper's claim), "Stone Age internet," "mysterious," "vanished," "primitive," "prehistoric peoples," "surprisingly sophisticated," "already doing what Europe would later do," "ahead of their time," "the dawn of trade." Do not frame Africa at 47,000 years ago as a rehearsal for anything. Do not call the beads "jewellery" without immediately complicating it. Do not describe the San or Ju/'hoansi as ancestors of these people or as a window into them; the atlas has twice told the reader they are contemporaries with their own history, and this lesson is where that caution becomes the actual subject.

## 4. Significance spine

Five movements. Threads to tie explicitly in words: **wealth** (value that lives in a relationship rather than in an object or a hoard) and **ideas** (an arbitrary shared standard carrying information between people who have never met). **Craft** should be named too, because the manufacturing sequence is doing the analytical work; the lesson may note in one clause that **power** is conspicuously absent from this evidence, which is itself a finding.

1. **How we know: the evidence and its size.** The oldest ostrich eggshell beads come from eastern Africa: Mumba, Tanzania at about 52 ka; Magubike, Tanzania, where two beads from Middle Stone Age deposits date close to or older than 50 ka and are the earliest directly radiocarbon-dated ostrich eggshell beads known (Miller and Willoughby 2014); Kisese II, Tanzania, oldest clearly worked beads at 46.2 to 42.7 ka cal BP. Southern Africa follows: Border Cave's ostrich eggshell beads are directly dated to about 42,000 BP (d'Errico et al. 2012), and Miller and Wang put the technology in southern Africa by 42 ka. State the arithmetic in the open: the node's date, c. 45,000 BCE, is about 47,000 years before present, which is a few thousand years after the earliest eastern African beads and a few thousand years before the earliest secure southern African ones. At the moment this node marks, this is an eastern African craft spreading, not yet a continental one.

2. **The mechanism: why a manufactured disc, why there, why then.** Three parts, and they interlock.
   - *Why a disc rather than a shell.* A pierced seashell can only say what the sea made it. A disc ground to a size can say what a community decided. Miller and Wang's method rests exactly here: bead diameter, aperture diameter and thickness are manufacturing choices, and manufacturing choices are cultural norms shared between neighbours, with distance reducing the chance of transmission. That is why sameness across space is readable as contact.
   - *Why there.* Ostriches are birds of open, dry country. Ostrich eggshell is a raw material of savanna and semi-desert, which is why this technology maps onto the arid and open belts of Africa and is absent from forest. It is also, unlike marine shell or obsidian, available across an enormous zone, so uniformity of size cannot be explained away by everyone drawing on one quarry. The uniformity has to be social.
   - *Why then.* Stewart and colleagues place the driver in the climatic instability of Marine Isotope Stage 3, roughly 59 to 25 ka: rapidly changing conditions "likely placed a premium on developing effective means of mitigating subsistence and demographic risks," with the beads providing a uniform medium of decoration and exchange suited to binding open networks together. The mechanism is insurance. A relationship a hundred kilometres away is a place to go when the water here fails.

3. **What was actually measured, and at what scale.** Two different measurements, routinely confused, and the lesson should keep them apart on the page.
   - *Style.* Miller and Wang assembled 1,516 ostrich eggshell beads from 31 sites (22 eastern African, 9 southern African), 1,238 of them published for the first time. Between about 50 and 33 ka, eastern and southern beads are near-identical in diameter and aperture, across a gap of roughly 3,000 km. Eastern means run 6.9 ± 1.2 mm external and 2.6 ± 0.6 mm aperture; southern beads in that earliest phase average about 6.7 mm, matching the east, before settling at 4.5 ± 0.9 mm in later phases. **No individual bead is known to have travelled 3,000 km.** What travelled is the recipe.
   - *Distance.* Stewart and colleagues took 27 beads from two highland Lesotho rockshelters, Melikane and Sehonghong, 24 km apart at about 1,800 to 1,860 m, and read their strontium. Ostriches do not occur in highland Lesotho today, were not seen there in the nineteenth century, and are unlikely to have been present before then, so every bead is an import by definition; the isotopes set the floor. 78 percent of the sample could not have come from closer than 109 km; 22 percent no closer than 164 km; a fraction no closer than 326 km. The oldest bead in that sample is about 33 ka. Those are the only measured transport distances in this literature, and they are 14,000 years younger than this node.
   - Say both numbers, say what each is, and the reader will understand more about archaeology than most popular accounts of this story convey.

4. **The named debate, with no verdict.** *Can hxaro be read backward?* Stanley Ambrose, publishing the Enkapune ya Muto workshop in 1998, argued that among Ju/'hoansi (!Kung) foragers beads are among the most important items in a system of institutionalised delayed reciprocity called hxaro, that hxaro partners are sought where resources are complementary (Wiessner's figures, quoted by Ambrose: 24 percent of partners live 50 to 100 km away, 9 percent more than 100 km), and that the Ju/'hoan word for sewn beadwork is the same as the word for hxaro gifts. His conclusion: the beads "may signify the invention of a symbolic marker for a social security system." Miller and Wang's 2022 continental network is the same argument scaled up. Against it: Peter Mitchell's "Anyone for hxaro? Thoughts on the theory and practice of exchange in southern African Later Stone Age archaeology" (in Mitchell, Haour and Hobart, eds., *Researching Africa's Past*, Oxbow, 2003, pp. 35 to 43) warns against collapsing a specific, historically situated Kalahari institution into a universal explanation for any prehistoric distribution of objects. Mitchell, Stewart, Hopper, Dewar and Schillaci returned to it in *Azania* 59(4), 2024, 483 to 506, arguing that using bead metrics to detect interaction demands hard control of taphonomy and sample size (the wording of that argument is UNVERIFIED; the article's landing page returned 403 and the abstract could not be fetched, so paraphrase from the title and the Hatton et al. cautions rather than quoting it). The most interesting fact about this debate is that it is not sceptics against believers: Mitchell and Stewart are the same team whose strontium work proved beads really did move hundreds of kilometres. They believe in the networks and distrust the ruler. Present both sides and stop.

5. **Honest uncertainty, then the landing.** The ranges to state plainly: the earliest bead dates, near 50 to 52 ka, sit at or beyond the practical limit of radiocarbon and are effectively minimum ages. Miller and Wang's earliest phase rests on 14 southern African beads, **12 of them from Border Cave alone**, so a continental claim is carried by one site's assemblage. Diameter can be altered by use-wear and by taphonomy, and Hatton and colleagues state that these processes are not yet well understood. Similar bead sizes could also reflect a shared inherited tradition rather than ongoing contact. Then land: around 33 ka the eastern and southern styles part. Miller and Wang tie the break to climate, and the direction is counterintuitive: eastern African precipitation fell, while a southward shift of the Intertropical Convergence Zone raised rainfall and periodically flooded the Zambezi catchment between roughly 30 and 16 ka. Not a drought in the middle. A river in the way. Southern and eastern populations stayed apart until herders moved south after 2 ka. Close on what survives: a person today keeps almost nothing that is genuinely theirs alone, and the thing that has kept people alive longest is not a store of goods but a list of people who owe them a welcome. That is the wealth thread in its first and oldest form, and the ideas thread doing the work: a small white disc, made to a size, so that a stranger a hundred kilometres away can read it.

## 5. Factual traps

1. **Trap:** these are the oldest beads, or the oldest jewellery, in the world. **Truth:** they are not. Thirty-three perforated *Tritia gibbosula* shells from Bizmoune Cave, Essaouira, Morocco include specimens dated to about 142,000 years ago, the oldest shell beads yet recovered; Blombos Cave's *Nassarius* beads run to roughly 75,000. What is distinctive about ostrich eggshell beads is that they are **manufactured to a standard**, not found and pierced. **Source:** Sehasseh et al., "Early Middle Stone Age personal ornaments from Bizmoune Cave, Essaouira, Morocco," *Science Advances* 7(39), 2021, DOI 10.1126/sciadv.abi8620 (26 authors, verified at Crossref); the atlas's own `spark-of-mind` for Blombos.
2. **Trap:** the beads travelled 3,000 km. **Truth:** Miller and Wang measured **stylistic** similarity across a roughly 3,000 km gap. No bead is known to have moved that far. The only measured transport distances are the Lesotho strontium minima: at least 109 km for 78 percent of a 27-bead sample, at least 164 km for 22 percent, at least 326 km for a fraction, on beads no older than about 33 ka. **Source:** Miller and Wang, *Nature* 601(7892), 2021, 234 to 239, DOI 10.1038/s41586-021-04227-2; Stewart, Zhao, Mitchell, Dewar, Gleason and Blum, *PNAS* 117(12), 2020, 6453 to 6462, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1921037117.
3. **Trap:** the continental network is well sampled. **Truth:** the earliest phase (50 to 33 ka) rests on 14 southern African beads, 12 of which come from Border Cave. The eastern sample is much larger; the comparison is not symmetrical. Print the number. **Source:** Miller and Wang 2022 (fetched via PMC8755535).
4. **Trap:** the network broke down because of drought. **Truth:** inverted. Eastern African precipitation did decrease by about 33 ka, but the barrier Miller and Wang propose is **more** water in the middle: a southward shift of the Intertropical Convergence Zone caused periodic flooding of the Zambezi catchment roughly 30 to 16 ka. **Source:** Miller and Wang 2022; Max Planck Institute of Geoanthropology release, 20 December 2021.
5. **Trap:** these beads were traded, or were an early currency. **Truth:** the archaeology recovers distributions, not transactions. The ethnographic model everyone reaches for, hxaro, is explicitly **not** trade: it is delayed, unhaggled, non-equivalent, and its point is to leave the account open. The node's own summary already says these gifts were not money; keep that and do not let "trade" or "exchange economy" creep into the prose. **Source:** Ambrose 1998 on hxaro via Wiessner; the atlas's own `firelight-economy`.
6. **Trap:** Enkapune ya Muto's beads are 40,000 calendar years old. **Truth:** Ambrose's figures are **uncalibrated radiocarbon** ages: 39,900 ± 1,600 BP on the interior shell fraction and 37,000 ± 1,100 on the exterior, from stratum DBL1.3. He himself notes that a cosmogenic-nuclide correction could push the age back about 3,500 years, and Tryon et al. group Enkapune ya Muto into a 52 to 47 ka range alongside Magubike and Mumba. Never print a BP radiocarbon figure as if it were a calendar date, and never mix BP and BCE in the same sentence without doing the conversion. **Source:** Ambrose, "Chronology of the Later Stone Age and Food Production in East Africa," *Journal of Archaeological Science* 25(4), 1998, 377 to 392 (PDF read in full); Tryon et al., *PLOS ONE* 13(2), 2018, e0192029.
7. **Trap:** at 45,000 BCE, people all across Africa were making and passing these beads. **Truth:** at about 47,000 years before present the securely dated record is **eastern African**: Mumba about 52 ka, Magubike close to or older than 50 ka, Kisese II 46.2 to 42.7 ka cal BP. Southern Africa's earliest secure ostrich eggshell beads are Border Cave at about 42,000 BP, roughly five thousand years later. The node hook and summary overstate this; see flags. **Source:** Tryon et al. 2018; Miller and Willoughby, *Journal of Human Evolution* 74, 2014, 118 to 122, DOI 10.1016/j.jhevol.2013.12.011; d'Errico et al., *PNAS* 109(33), 2012, 13214 to 13219.
8. **Trap:** we know these people practised hxaro. **Truth:** we know they made standardised beads and that similar beads occur far apart. Hxaro is a named institution documented among living Ju/'hoansi in the twentieth century. Ambrose proposed the deep-time reading in 1998; Mitchell has argued since 2003 that the analogy is being asked to carry more than it can. No verdict. **Source:** Ambrose 1998; Mitchell, "Anyone for hxaro?" in *Researching Africa's Past*, Oxbow, 2003, 35 to 43; Mitchell et al., *Azania* 59(4), 2024, 483 to 506.
9. **Trap:** the Kondoa rock paintings above Kisese II were made by the people who left the deepest beads. **Truth:** the shelter was occupied for roughly 47,000 years and the painted art is far younger than the basal bead horizon. Louis and Mary Leakey came to Kisese II in 1935 for the paintings and only later dug; the two are stratigraphically and chronologically separate stories. Do not fuse the beads and the art into one scene. **Source:** Tryon et al. 2018 (fetched).
10. **Trap:** ostrich eggshell beads are an African signature. **Truth:** they occur well beyond Africa. Shuidonggou in North China has more than eighty finely perforated and polished ostrich eggshell beads, many ochred, on the shell of the extinct *Struthio anderssoni*, with Locality 2 beads around 31 ka cal BP; Patne in Maharashtra, India yielded plain and engraved ostrich eggshell around 25,000 years ago. Use this for planetary reach, and verify the specific figures against a fetched publication before printing them (currently UNVERIFIED at snippet level). **Source flag:** these came from search summaries in this pass, not from a fetched paper.
11. **Trap:** the disc was cut out and then pierced. **Truth:** in the dominant manufacturing pathway the aperture is drilled **first**, into a roughly fragmented blank, and only then is the outer rim trimmed and ground; strings of blanks are often finished as a batch so the discs come out matched. A minority pathway shapes the rim first. **Source:** Kuhlase and Forssman, "Ostrich eggshell beads from Little Muck Shelter, southern Africa," *Southern African Field Archaeology* 19, 2024 (fetched, eight stages, two pathways); Werner and Surette, "Stone Age batch polishing: experimental ostrich eggshell bead finishing techniques," *Azania*, 2026, DOI 10.1080/0067270x.2026.2649093 (metadata verified at Crossref; article not fetched).
12. **Trap:** a bead maker robbed a nest for the egg. **Truth:** possible, but not the only or even the likeliest route. Ostriches nest communally: a major hen lays roughly 5 to 11 eggs and two to five minor hens add two to six each, so a nest may hold 30 to 40 eggs while the major hen can incubate only about 20, pushing the surplus a metre or two out of the nest to perish. Hatched, abandoned and surplus shells are abundant on the ground. Also, shell fragments long outlive the egg's contents, so the shell a bead maker works may be nobody's meal at all. Physical figures that are safe: shell about 2 mm thick, egg about 1.4 kg and about 1.4 litres, the largest egg of any living bird. **Source flag:** the nesting figures come from general ornithological summaries in this pass, not from a fetched primary paper; Bertram's 1979 *Nature* paper on ostriches recognising their own eggs is the canonical reference and should be verified before any number is printed.
13. **Trap:** the Ju/'hoansi show us what these bead makers were like. **Truth:** the atlas has already ruled on this twice, in `firelight-economy` and `tribe-of-strangers`: the Ju/'hoansi are contemporaries with their own long history of trade, herder contact, colonialism and wage labour, not a Pleistocene sample. In this lesson the caution is not an aside, it is the subject. State it once, sharply, and turn it into the debate rather than repeating the earlier lessons' paragraph.

## 6. Callback design

All four legal targets have written lesson files, which were read in full for this brief.

- **`spark-of-mind` (c. 100,000 BCE), STRONGEST, use it.** That lesson's beads are pea-sized *Nassarius* snail shells, chosen because "the ones whose lips have worn thin" can be pierced without shattering: the maker is selecting shells the sea already shaped. This lesson's bead is a disc ground to a size on a string of other discs. The genuine comparison is **found form versus decided form**, and it matters analytically, not decoratively: a natural shell's dimensions are the animal's, so they carry no information about the person, whereas a manufactured diameter is a choice, which is exactly why Miller and Wang can read style from it and why a 3,000 km match means anything at all. Add the arithmetic: Blombos's beads are about 75,000 years old, this node is about 47,000, so roughly 28,000 years separate a pierced shell from a standardised disc, which is about five times the span from the first cities to now. The callback should ask what the second invention added that the first could not do.
- **`tribe-of-strangers`, STRONGEST, use it.** That lesson is built on twentieth-century Kalahari ethnography and says so; its Bau touches an ostrich-eggshell strand and hands beads to a partner's kin. This lesson is the ground under it. The genuine comparison is **model versus evidence**: what does the dirt actually confirm of that waterhole scene, and what remains an analogy? Confirmable: standardised ostrich eggshell beads exist in eastern Africa by about 50 ka; near-identical beads occur across roughly 3,000 km between 50 and 33 ka; beads in highland Lesotho came from at least 109 km away, and often much further, over 33,000 years. Not confirmable: that anyone practised hxaro, that the exchange was delayed and unhaggled, that it created named partnerships, that a name opened a stranger's fire. That is a demanding, honest, non-trivia callback, and it is the pedagogical centre of this lesson.
- **`firelight-economy`, strong alternate, rank second.** Its thesis is that a forager band stores relationships rather than food, and its Toma is deliberately denied status for a huge kill. The comparison here is what a bead adds to that: sharing meat works only where people can see each other, but a bead is durable, portable, and readable by someone you have never met, so it extends the same insurance across space and years rather than across a camp and a night. It also lets the lesson name the absence of power in this record: no accumulation of beads by anyone is visible at this date. Use if either primary target is claimed elsewhere.
- **`great-dispersal`, weaker, rank third, better spent in `threadsOut`.** Its argument is that culture, not biology, is the adaptation, and that dispersal is that ratchet spent at planetary scale. This node is the same claim in a different register: a network is a technology for surviving a variable climate, exactly as a boat is. Real, but abstract, and the lesson risks restating that lesson's own ending.

**Recommended callback pair: `spark-of-mind` and `tribe-of-strangers`.** They form a sequence rather than two checks: the first asks what changed when a bead stopped being found and started being made, the second asks how much of the story we tell about such beads is actually excavated.

**Suggested `threadsOut` (four):** `paint-in-the-dark`, `invention-of-money`, `bronze-web`, `tribe-of-strangers`. Give the `invention-of-money` thread a negative-space "why" (the bead is what money is not), and note that the graph flag proposing a downgrade from `enables` to `echoes` on that edge appears already resolved in the committed data, which shows `echoes`.

## 8. Node-data flags

1. **The hook asserts a distance no evidence at this date supports.** "The finished string is not worn and kept at home but handed to a band camped a hundred kilometers away." The 109 km figure is real but comes from strontium on 27 beads in highland Lesotho whose oldest specimen is about 33 ka, and from twentieth-century Ju/'hoansi partner distances. ## A. Upgrade to section 4, movement 4: a second named debate, and it is younger and sharper

The Ambrose-versus-Mitchell debate above is real and should stay. But there is a second, more specific argument running directly under Miller and Wang's method, and it is the better one to give the reader because it is about a measurement rather than about an analogy.

**The question: can a social boundary be read off a millimetre?**

- **The method's foundation.** Polly Wiessner, "Style and Social Information in Kalahari San Projectile Points," *American Antiquity* 48(2), 1983, 253 to 276, DOI 10.2307/280450 (Crossref-verified: single author, volume, issue, pages). Argued from Kalahari fieldwork that artifact form actively carries group identity and that people notice and use it. Miller and Wang cite Wiessner explicitly; their continental claim is this argument cashed in at scale.
- **The classic objection.** James R. Sackett, "Style and Ethnicity in the Kalahari: A Reply to Wiessner," *American Antiquity* 50(1), 1985, 154 to 159, DOI 10.2307/280642. Most artifact variation is *isochrestic*: the residue of habitual, learned ways of doing a job, not a deliberate message. Wiessner answered in the same issue: "Style or Isochrestic Variation? A Reply to Sackett," 50(1), 1985, 160 to 166, DOI 10.2307/280643. Both Crossref-verified.
- **The objection reopened, on this exact material, this decade.** Joseph Jeffrey Werner and Flannery Surette, "Ostrich eggshell bead size variation: The impacts of material and technology on bead diameter," *Journal of Archaeological Science* 180, 2025, article 106279, DOI 10.1016/j.jas.2025.106279 (Crossref-verified: two authors, volume, article number, year; also an SSRN preprint, DOI 10.2139/ssrn.4930716, 2024). Using archaeological and experimental data, they argue that external diameter is significantly shaped by eggshell thickness and by manufacturing technique, that stylistic and non-stylistic traits are therefore **not independent**, and that assemblage- or region-scale differences in diameter cannot automatically be taken to mark distinct cultural groups with their own traditions. That is a direct challenge to the inferential step Miller and Wang's headline rests on.
  - **Status caveat:** the ScienceDirect and SSRN pages both returned 403 in this pass. The bibliographic record is solid; the summary of the argument above comes from the abstract as surfaced in search. **Do not quote a sentence from this paper.** Paraphrase, and attribute the argument as "recent work has questioned whether diameter can carry that weight."
- **Why this is the debate to run.** It has a clean shape a lay reader can hold: one side says the size of the bead is a choice, so sameness of size is contact; the other says the size of the bead is substantially what the shell and the drill allow, so sameness of size may be nothing but shell and drill. And it pairs beautifully with the strontium: the isotopes are not in this argument at all, because a strontium ratio is not a style. That is the lesson's cleanest teaching moment about method. **No verdict.**

Note also that Werner and Surette's argument sits oddly well with the existing brief's own governing image. If blanks are ground as a batch on a string, then uniformity within an assemblage is partly a *procedural* fact rather than a communicative one, which is precisely Sackett's point arriving 40 years later wearing different clothes. That is worth one sentence in significance and it is a genuinely elegant connection.

## B. Upgrade to section 5: six additional traps (numbered 14 to 19, continuing the list above)

14. **Trap:** ostrich eggshell beads are the first evidence of long-distance exchange, or the first sign that anything travelled far.
    **Truth:** off by roughly 150,000 years. At the Sibilo School Road Site in Baringo, Kenya, dated to about 200,000 years ago, 43 percent of the lithic assemblage is obsidian, sourced to three outcrops at 25 km, 140 km and 166 km, with the majority coming from the **farthest** source. Long-distance raw-material transport is as old as our species. What beads add is not distance but a *durable, standardised, wearable* medium: obsidian moves because someone wanted the stone, and a bead moves because someone wanted the relationship. Make that the distinction rather than claiming a first.
    **Source:** Nick Blegen, "The earliest long-distance obsidian transport: Evidence from the ~200 ka Middle Stone Age Sibilo School Road Site, Baringo, Kenya," *Journal of Human Evolution* 103, 2017, 1 to 19, DOI 10.1016/j.jhevol.2016.11.002 (Crossref-verified: single author, volume, pages, year).

15. **Trap:** the red residue on the beads is deliberate colouring, so these were painted ornaments.
    **Truth:** at Ga-Mohana Hill North, red residue appears on 53 percent of the finished beads (10 of 19), and the excavators judge it more likely to be contact with ochre in the sediment, peri- or post-depositionally, than intentional application, because the residues sit in random positions across both beads and unworked fragments. Blombos's ochre-stained *Nassarius* is a separate case with separate evidence and must not be imported here. Border Cave is a third case again: four of its beads are described as homogeneously blackened **by heating**, which the authors suggest may be intentional. Three claims, three evidentiary statuses. Keep them apart.
    **Source:** Hatton et al. 2022 (fetched in full at PMC9159631); d'Errico et al. 2012 (fetched in full at PMC3421171).

16. **Trap:** the network broke because of "an event called the Intertropical Convergence Zone."
    **Truth:** this exact phrasing circulates in press coverage and is nonsense. The ITCZ is a permanent band of atmospheric circulation, not an event. Miller and Wang's proposal is that eastern African precipitation fell while the rain belt shifted south, causing periodic flooding of the Zambezi catchment and creating a barrier. Their own claim is only that climate "exerted some influence in shaping human social contact," and their paper carries an explicit limitation that beads are unevenly distributed across their phases. (This reinforces trap 4 above rather than replacing it: the direction of the error there is drought-versus-water, and the error here is event-versus-circulation. A writer can make both mistakes in one sentence.)
    **Source:** Miller and Wang 2022, fetched in full including the stated limitations, at PMC8755535.

17. **Trap:** the scene defaults to a man knapping, with bead-making as background women's work or as idle handwork.
    **Truth:** ethnographically documented ostrich eggshell bead making is largely women's work, done seasonally in larger camps, and it is a skilled, time-intensive craft. The best current documentation of the living craft is not Kalahari at all: it is East African, made with the El Molo of two villages on the eastern shore of Lake Turkana, Marsabit County, Kenya, covering the full sequence from stone collection and drill-making through strand preparation, drilling, rounding and threading into *karkarto* and *rumirumi*. Use it, and use it partly to break the reflex that routes every ostrich eggshell bead through the Kalahari and through the San.
    **Source:** James K. Munene, Abdikadir Kurewa, Wanjage Wairimu and Micheal Basili, "Ostrich eggshell bead-making in East Africa: a study of crafting in the El Molo community, Kenya," *Azania: Archaeological Research in Africa* 59(4), 2024, 582 to 605, DOI 10.1080/0067270X.2024.2415258 (Crossref-verified: four authors, volume, issue, pages). Endangered Material Knowledge Programme project page, fetched. The general "women's work" statement is well attested ethnographically but was verified here only at synthesis level; attribute it as ethnographic practice, not as a universal.

18. **Trap:** the Asian ostrich eggshell beads are a curiosity at the edge of the story.
    **Truth:** they are the point, if the lesson wants the reader to feel the whole planet. Denisova Cave in southern Siberia has ostrich eggshell beads whose raw shell was carried in from northwestern Mongolia, because the bird's Pleistocene range did not reach west of central Siberia. Wherever ostriches and people overlapped, the same solution appeared independently: a shell nobody could eat, turned into a standard nobody could counterfeit cheaply. That is convergence, not diffusion, and it is a much better anti-Euro-teleology beat than a sentence asserting that Africa was first.
    **Source flag:** search-level only in both passes. The Denisova, Shuidonggou and Patne details remain **UNVERIFIED**; confirm the Shuidonggou figures against Wei et al., *Journal of Archaeological Science*, 2017, before printing a date or a species, and treat the Denisova sourcing claim as a lead rather than a fact.

19. **Trap:** rounding the Border Cave beads to "about 45,000 years old," or letting BP and BCE touch.
    **Truth:** the node's date, c. 45,000 BCE, is about **47,000 years before present**, whereas the phrase "45 kya" in the literature means 45,000 years *ago*, which is c. 43,000 BCE. Those are 4,000 years apart and the collision is easy to miss. The Border Cave bead's direct date is 38,020 +1240/-1070 BP (lab KIA 44423), calibrating to **44,856 to 41,010 cal BP**, with Bayesian modelling putting layer 1WA at 44 to 43 ka and 1BS Lower B-C at 43 to 42 ka. The existing brief's trap 6 makes the same point for Enkapune ya Muto; this is the southern African instance of it. Pick one convention for the whole lesson and hold it.
    **Source:** d'Errico et al. 2012, fetched in full at PMC3421171.
