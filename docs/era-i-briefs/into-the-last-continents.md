## 0. Read this first: the overlap problem

**`great-dispersal` has already told most of this node's obvious story, and told it well.** Its fourth significance paragraph runs: Beringia as bare grassland rather than an ice bridge, the Beringian pause, Clovis-first, Monte Verde at roughly 14,500 years ago, the shift to a coastal route, the White Sands footprints at 21,000 to 23,000 years ago, the reservoir-effect objection, and the 2023 answer. It also spends a full `why` question on "why has the date of the first Americans been rewritten so often, and what does the White Sands dispute actually turn on." Its fifth paragraph then delivers the line "the sealed hemisphere of the Americas would run the entire experiment of civilization a second time, alone."

If this lesson re-narrates arrival dates, the reader gets a rerun of a lesson eleven nodes back. **The brief is built to prevent that.** The three things this node can do that `great-dispersal` did not:

1. **Stand inside the evidence rather than summarizing it.** `great-dispersal` reported the footprints in one clause. This lesson can spend a whole story on what a footprint actually is: the one thing in the atlas that nobody made. Every other node opens on an object somebody manufactured. This one opens on an act.
2. **State the route paradox, which `great-dispersal` softened.** Its prose implies the coastal route resolved the problem. It does not. At 23,000 years ago both known doors were shut (see 4.2). The honest position is that no current route model delivers people to New Mexico at that date, and saying so is more interesting than any tidy corridor.
3. **Make the separation, not the arrival, the subject.** The node's forward edges all point at consequences of isolation: maize, Cahokia, 1492. `great-dispersal` gestured at the sealed hemisphere in one sentence. This node is where that sentence gets its arithmetic and its weight.

Working thesis to land: *this is the node where a species that had spent sixty thousand years connecting itself finally cut itself in two, and did not know it for two hundred and twenty centuries.*

So the dog belongs to a later wave than this node's headline evidence. Say so; it is a gift, not a flaw, because it forces the reader to see the peopling as episodes rather than one event.

**This node's job in the atlas.** It is the last node of the Long Dawn's outward motion and the only one whose real subject is division. Everything before it is a species spreading and staying connected: beads travelling a hundred kilometres, ochre recipes taught hand to hand, a raft crew reading swell. This node ends that. It hands two whole continents to Era II onward and quietly installs the control group that makes the rest of the atlas an argument rather than a chronicle: when maize, cities, kings, monuments, and writing appear a second time on the far side of an ocean, with no contact, the atlas can say what is universal about human societies and what is contingent. That is the node's real cargo. The arrival dates are the ticket, not the freight.

## 2. Preferred scene

**White Sands, New Mexico, WHSA Locality 2, on the eastern margin of pluvial lake Otero, roughly 23,000 to 21,000 years ago: a group of adolescents and children crossing wet lake-margin mud barefoot, among the trackways of mammoth, camel, dire wolf, and ground sloth.**

The scene's spine is a fact most writers do not know and will not reach for on their own: **the dated tracks were not made by adult hunters.** Bennett and Reynolds, two of the excavators, writing for The Conversation on the day the Science paper appeared, describe the footprints as "made by a group of teenagers, children and the occasional adult," and offer the interpretation that adolescents did the fetch-and-carry labour while adults worked at skilled tasks at the lake edge, adding that "children and teenagers are more energetic and playful than adults." The oldest widely accepted evidence of human beings in the Americas is a crowd of teenagers running errands in mud.

**Documentation ledger.**

- *Attested by excavation and publication:* human footprints in a stratified sequence at Locality 2 (also LA195628), on the east side of paleolake Otero; radiocarbon on seeds of the aquatic plant *Ruppia cirrhosa* in undisturbed layers above and below the tracks, calibrated by NPS as 22,860 ± 320 and 21,130 ± 250 years; the age-model range of roughly 23,000 to 21,000 years; the demographic reading (teenagers, children, occasional adults); the presence in the same lake deposits of contemporaneous megafauna tracks including mammoth, ground sloth, canid, felid, and artiodactyls (Holliday et al. 2025 lists exactly these); the sediment as clay, silt and dolomite of a lake-margin sequence with tracks formed on subaerially exposed surfaces; "ghost tracks" invisible to the eye and imaged in 3-D by ground-penetrating radar (Urban et al. 2019), which also found co-associated mammoth and human prints and that track density is far greater than the unaided eye detects; White Sands as, per NPS, the largest known collection of fossilized human footprints.
- *Reconstruction:* every person, every name, every word spoken, the errand, the weather, the time of day, what anyone was carrying, whether they were related. There is no writing, no burial, no dwelling, no hearth at this locality. `storyContext` must say plainly that the figures are a composite built from the trackways at Locality 2.
- *Real named person available:* **no.** None exists for any American site of this age.
- *Quantities to check before printing:* do not state a footprint count, a group size, a stride length, a walking speed, or a body-mass estimate from memory. Do not print an exact age for any individual trackmaker. The two safe numbers are the two calibrated seed dates above (from NPS) and the 23,000-to-21,000 range. If a third number is wanted, use Holliday et al.'s 26 new radiocarbon dates from two labs.

**Why this scene.** It dramatizes the mechanism instead of decorating it. The mechanism this node turns on is that the deepest American past survives as *behaviour*, not as artifacts, which is exactly why it was contested for twenty years and why the argument had to be won with seeds, pollen, quartz grains and lake mud rather than with a spearpoint. Opening on teenagers in mud also refuses, without a word of editorializing, the pith-helmet image of first contact with a continent.

### Alternate 1: the long double trackway (strong, but carries a dating caveat)

**A young woman or older adolescent walking out across the flats and back, carrying a small child in at least one direction.** Bennett et al. (2020), *Quaternary Science Reviews* 249: article 106610, verified abstract: the tracks "record more than one and a half kilometres of an out-and-return journey and form the longest Late Pleistocene-age double human trackway in the world. An adolescent or small adult female made two trips separated by at least several hours, carrying a young child in at least one direction. Despite giant ground sloth and Columbian Mammoth transecting them between the outbound and return journeys, the human tracks show no changes indicative of predator/prey awareness. In contrast, the giant ground sloth tracks show behaviour consistent with human predator awareness, while mammoth tracks show no such apparent concern." The prints show left-right asymmetry the authors suggest may come from child carrying. NPS adds that the child's prints appear beside hers where "she carried the child, shifting them from side to side and occasionally setting the child down."

This is the single most affecting documented human scene in the entire American Pleistocene. **A later pass has now resolved the age question, and the answer disqualifies it as this node's scene.** Bennett and Reynolds, writing about their own trackway in *The Conversation*, place it "from the end of the last ice age (about 11,550 years ago) to sometime before about 13,000 years ago," roughly ten thousand years younger than the horizons at Locality 2 that carry this node's headline date. The trackway belongs to the terminal Pleistocene, not the glacial maximum. **Downgraded from "promote if you can pin it" to "do not use."** It cannot appear in a lesson whose date field is c. 23,000 to 14,000 BCE without either a date error or a hedge so heavy it kills the scene. If Fable wants that image, it belongs to a later node about the terminal Pleistocene, not here. See trap 5.

### Alternate 2: the sloth (vivid, but the paper itself hedges and the age is looser)

Bustos et al. (2018), *Science Advances* 4(4), verified via PMC5916513, title ends in a question mark on purpose: "Footprints preserve terminal Pleistocene hunt? Human-sloth interactions in North America." Attested: more than a hundred sloth and human tracks; over ten human prints laid *inside* sloth prints, with human stride length departing from natural gait, read as deliberate stepping in the animal's tracks; "flailing circles," deformed sloth pes tracks ringed by irregular impressions and claw marks, read as an animal rearing and swinging; sloth trackways becoming markedly more tortuous where human tracks concentrate, a pattern absent from sloth trails alone; geological evidence that both track types formed under identical moisture conditions and are therefore contemporaneous. The authors conclude humans were "harassing, stalking, and/or hunting" the sloth.

Liabilities: the paper says *terminal* Pleistocene, not Last Glacial Maximum, so this is probably not the 23,000-year-old material; the interpretation is explicitly provisional; and a hunt scene tilts the lesson toward the predation story that `world-the-ice-made` already owns (megafauna extinction, overkill versus climate). Best used as one sentence inside significance, on the point that these tracks record encounters, not just presence.

### Scene to AVOID as a cold open

**A band of fur-clad hunters walking east across the Bering land bridge into an empty continent.** Three reasons. First, `great-dispersal` already opened on a crossing (Wara's raft) and already narrated Beringia in prose the reader will remember; repeating the beat wastes the node. Second, nothing about a Beringian crossing is attested by any excavated scene, so it would be pure invention dressed as archaeology, which the forge spec forbids and which this project has been burned by. Third, and most important, the image of an empty continent awaiting arrival is the exact Euro-teleological posture the atlas refuses: it turns two hemispheres into a stage waiting for a story, and it sets up 1492 as a homecoming. Also avoid opening on 1492, which is `columbian-exchange`'s node and would tell the reader how to feel before the lesson has earned it.

## 3. Story movement

Six to seven paragraphs. One place, one horizon, one sustained act of looking.

1. **Feet in mud.** Locality 2, at the lake margin. Cold, thin, dry air; a shallow gypsum-bitter lake; carbonate mud that takes a print like wax and holds it. A boy or girl of maybe fourteen, barefoot because bare feet grip mud that would suck a wrapped foot off. Make the reader feel the physical act of walking on a surface that records you.
2. **The crowd.** They are nearly all young. Adults are at the far edge doing something skilled; the young are the legs of the operation, sent out and back. Let the reader notice, without being told, that this is a labour system.
3. **The other tracks.** Mammoth, camel, dire wolf, ground sloth, crossing and re-crossing. This is a shared shoreline, not a wilderness. If Alternate 2 is used at all, use it here, in one sentence, as an encounter rather than a hunt.
4. **The mud closes.** Water comes back over the flats; the prints fill, silt over, are buried under gypsum. Nothing here is *made*. Nothing here is kept on purpose. The whole record is an accident of weather.
5. **The turn.** These people are ten thousand years earlier than the earliest arrival anyone credited a lifetime ago. Behind them, somewhere north, the ice sheets are at their maximum and closing. Whatever door they came through is closing behind them, and it will not open again for two hundred and twenty centuries.
6. **The pivot.** End on the reader's own question, which significance answers: how do you prove a person was somewhere when all they left was a walk, and what happens to a species when half of it stops being able to reach the other half?

**Governing image: the footprint as negative space.** A print is not a thing somebody made; it is the exact shape of an absence, mud pressed out of the way by a body that is gone. Return to it once at the close, transformed: the Americas after this node are also a negative, a whole human world defined by the shape of the connection that was no longer there. (This also sets up the strongest callback: the hand stencil at Chauvet is the same trick, "the stone gone red everywhere she was not," a person recorded as the space they occupied.)

**Words and framings to avoid.** "Mysterious." "Vanished." "Prehistory" used to mean "before things mattered." "The New World" without irony or quotation. "Virgin," "pristine," "untouched," "empty," "unspoiled" continent. "Primitive." "Discovered." "Land bridge" without immediately correcting it (see trap 1). "Finally reached," which smuggles in a destination. Any sentence in which the Americas are waiting for something. Any sentence in which a non-European world is a rehearsal for a European one. Also avoid "Indians"; use Native American, Indigenous, or the specific nation. And avoid calling these the oldest human footprints: they are not, by three orders of magnitude (trap 11).

## 4. Significance spine

Five movements. Threads to name **in words**: **craft** and **ideas**, matching the node's pigments. Craft is the sewn clothing, the boats, and the transportable technique that made a glacial lakeshore survivable at all. Ideas is twofold: the taught knowledge that had to be rebuilt for a hemisphere of unfamiliar species, and the intellectual point that isolation turned the Americas into the only rerun of human social history we will ever have. Wealth and power belong to the forward edges (maize, Cahokia, 1492), not to this lesson.

### 4.1 Evidence: how you date a walk

The chain, told as a story of an argument being won rather than a list of methods. Bennett et al. (Science 373:6562, 2021, pages 1528 to 1531, fifteen authors) dated seeds of *Ruppia cirrhosa*, an aquatic plant, in undisturbed layers above and below the tracks: roughly 23,000 to 21,000 years ago. Madsen, Davis, Rhode and Oviatt (Science 375:6577, 2022) filed a formal Comment: aquatic plants take up dissolved ancient carbon from hard groundwater, so the seeds may read older than they are. This is the "old water" or hard-water reservoir effect, and it was a good objection, not obstruction. Pigati and colleagues answered twice: a Response in the same issue (Science 375:6577, 2022), and then, in Pigati et al. (Science 382:6666, 2023, pages 73 to 75, eleven authors), with two entirely different clocks: calibrated radiocarbon on **terrestrial pine pollen** from the same stratigraphic horizons as the seeds, and **optically stimulated luminescence** on the quartz grains themselves, which dates the last time the sand saw sunlight. The pollen matched. The OSL gave a minimum near 21,500 years. Then Holliday et al. (Science Advances 11:25, 2025) added a third, from a team independent of the original investigators: 26 new radiocarbon dates, 21 of them on palustrine mud, run at two laboratories with no connection to the first work, tracing a lake, wetland and stream sequence spanning more than 23,600 to about 17,000 calibrated years before present, stratigraphically continuous with the track-bearing alluvium.

Land the epistemics: three independent chronometers on three different materials, one of them from a rival team, is close to the strongest case archaeology knows how to build, and it is still not unanimous (Steffen filed a Comment in *PaleoAmerica* in December 2025). Say plainly that this is what a healthy science looks like from inside. Do not write "settled."

### 4.2 Mechanism: why there, why then, and the paradox of the closed doors

*Why there.* Lake Otero existed because the ice-age climate did: with the ice sheets diverting the jet stream south, the Southwest was wet and the Tularosa Basin held a large pluvial lake. Its margin was a magnet, water and forage in a cold dry world, so people and megafauna were on the same shoreline. And the mud was the right mud: fine carbonate silt and clay that takes a print, backed by a rising lake that buried it and a later gypsum dunefield that sealed it. This is a preservation accident with a mechanism, and the mechanism is worth stating: we know about White Sands and not about a thousand other places because of sediment chemistry, not because of what people did there.

*Why then.* The Last Glacial Maximum is conventionally 26,500 to 19,000 years ago (the figure Becerra-Valdivia and Higham use). The tracks at 23,000 to 21,000 sit inside it.

*The paradox, and this is the movement's payload.* Both routes people usually name were shut at that moment. Pedersen et al. (Nature 537:7618, 2016, pages 45 to 49, seventeen authors) took sediment cores from a bottleneck in the ice-free corridor and found steppe vegetation, bison and mammoth only by about 12,600 calibrated years ago, concluding that the first Americans "are unlikely to have travelled by this route." Lesnek et al. (Science Advances 4:5, 2018) dated maximum Cordilleran ice in southeastern Alaska, the bottleneck on the coastal route, to about 20,000 to 17,000 years ago, concluding an open and ecologically viable coastal pathway existed only **after 17,000 years ago**. Do the arithmetic on the page: the coastal door opens roughly 4,000 to 6,000 years *after* the White Sands tracks; the interior door roughly 8,400 to 10,400 years after. Whoever left those prints either came before the ice sheets coalesced, by a route nobody has evidence for, or the prints are not what they seem. The node's summary is right that coastal movement is "a leading explanation, not a traced itinerary"; the lesson should go one step further and say that no current route model accounts for a 23,000-year-old presence in New Mexico. That is a better sentence than any corridor.

*What it caused.* Not the extinctions; `world-the-ice-made` owns that debate and this lesson must not re-run it. What it caused is the seal (4.4).

### 4.3 The named live debate: two disciplines that do not agree

**Use this one, and give both sides their strongest form with no verdict.**

*Archaeology's position.* People were in the Americas before, during and immediately after the LGM. Becerra-Valdivia and Higham (Nature 584, 2020, pages 93 to 97) Bayesian-modelled chronometric data from 42 North American and Beringian sites and concluded exactly that, while also finding that "more widespread occupation began during a period of abrupt warming, Greenland Interstadial 1," about 14,700 to 12,900 years ago. White Sands now rests on three independent chronometers.

*Genomics' position.* The ancient DNA record has no room for those people. Moreno-Mayar et al. (Nature 553:7687, 2018, pages 203 to 207, eighteen authors) sequenced USR1, one of two infants from Upward Sun River in Alaska dated to about 11,500 years ago, and modelled a single founding population that split from East Asians around 36,000 ± 1,500 years ago with gene flow persisting to about 25,000 ± 1,100; Ancient Beringians branching off about 22,000 to 18,100 years ago; and the basal northern and southern Native American branches, to which all other Native Americans belong, diverging **about 17,500 to 14,600 years ago, probably south of the ice sheets**. Willerslev and Meltzer (Nature 594, 2021, pages 356 to 364) summarize the record: the first peoples "once south of the continental ice sheets, spread widely, expanded rapidly and branched into multiple populations," and every ancient individual sequenced in the Americas, except later Arctic arrivals, is closer to contemporary Indigenous Americans than to any other population on Earth.

*Sharper figures, from the May 2026 eLetter.* Meltzer, Moreno-Mayar, Pinotti, Heintzman, Pedersen and Willerslev, writing in *Science* on 4 May 2026 to rebut Surovell, compiled the current clock estimates in one place, and these are the numbers to use because they come with intervals. Native Americans originate in an admixture between East Asian and Ancient North Eurasian related groups about **25,000 to 20,000 years ago**, most probably in Siberia during or just after the glacial maximum. Complete mitochondrial genomes give divergence of ancestral Native Americans from Asian populations at more than **24.9 ka**, formation of the founder lineages at **18.4 ka**, and a major expansion no later than about **16 ka**. Y-chromosome data give divergence at **18.9 ka** (95% CI 16.7 to 21.4) and expansion at **15 ka** (95% CI 13.2 to 16.9). Ancient Beringians split at **20.9 ka** (95% CI 18.1 to 22); Northern and Southern Native Americans split at **15.7 ka** (95% CI 14.6 to 17.5), and demographic modelling finds continuous gene flow after the first split but not after the second. Two further hooks in the same letter: ancient mitochondrial DNA of Native American lineages appears in the human coprolites from Paisley Caves, Oregon, at about **14.6 ka**; and pre-Columbian dogs, which could not have reached the Americas without people, descend from a lineage that split from all other dogs and expanded about **14.6 ka** (95% HPD 13.74 to 17.65). Every one of these numbers is younger than the footprints.

*The arithmetic to put on the page.* The White Sands trackmakers walked 3,500 to 8,400 years before the split that founded every genetically known Native American lineage, and at or just before the moment the Ancient Beringians branched off in the far north. So either they left no descendants anyone has yet sampled, which is not a fringe idea (Willerslev and Meltzer explicitly note founding movements included "some for whom there are no currently known descendants"), or the footprint chronology is wrong somewhere nobody has found. Both possibilities are held by serious people. Give the reader that discomfort and leave it.

*Secondary debate, if a second is wanted, and it should be brief.* Chiquihuite Cave, Zacatecas: Ardelean et al. (Nature 584, 2020, pages 87 to 92) reported about 1,900 stone artifacts in a 3 m stratified sequence with more than 50 radiocarbon and luminescence dates, pushing possible human presence to 33,000 to 31,000 years ago. Chatters and nineteen co-authors (PaleoAmerica 8:1, 2022, pages 1 to 16) replied that the assemblage is best explained as geofacts from roof fall and mass movement of cave fill. Present as unresolved, lean sceptical, and do not lean any weight on Chiquihuite.

### 4.3b The other named live debate, four months old: is Monte Verde even Pleistocene?

**If the lesson wants one debate rather than two, use this one instead of 4.3.** It is fresher, it is angrier, it is about the node's own second pillar, and it teaches the reader something 4.3 cannot: that in this field a thirty-year consensus can be attacked in a first-rank journal and defended within seven weeks, in public, by name.

*The attack.* Surovell, Méndez, García, Lüthgens, Thompson and Latorre (*Science* 391:6791, 19 March 2026, 1283 to 1288) sampled and dated nine alluvial exposures along Chinchihuapi Creek and made three arguments: that an approximately 11,000-year-old volcanic ash, the Lepué Tephra, lies stratigraphically *beneath* the archaeological component; that an erosional unconformity implies a temporal gap; and that the wood and artifacts were carried down from an upstream terrace and redeposited. Their conclusion: the surface the Monte Verde material rests on formed after about 8,600 years ago, so the site is 8,200 to 4,200 years old, and, in Surovell's own quoted line, "dating wood at Monte Verde does not date the occupation of the site."

*The defence, in three parts, all published as *Science* eLetters on 4 to 5 May 2026 and all read in full for this brief.*

- **Michael R. Waters, Jessi J. Halligan, Rolfe D. Mandel, Justin A. Holcomb, Loren G. Davis and Vance T. Holliday** (5 May 2026, geoarchaeology): the Lepué Tephra also occurs in the upper deposits of an older terrace, so the terrace, and therefore the creek incision, predates 11 ka; two OSL ages of about 18.1 ± 1.4 ka and 14.3 ± 1.2 ka show the alluvial surface existed in the late Pleistocene; and the correlation of Surovell's sediments with the site's own stratigraphy fails. Note that Holliday, the man who defended White Sands, is here defending Monte Verde against a co-author of the 2018 "multiple models" paper. This field's alliances are not tribal.
- **Tom D. Dillehay, Mario Pino, Luis Lara, Ana Abarzúa, Loren Davis, David Madsen, Donald Grayson, Jack Rossen and nine others** (4 May 2026, the excavators): Surovell's team never excavated at Monte Verde, sampling exposures 50 to 4,000 metres away. The site's own cultural layer is only **two to three centimetres thick** with clear spatial patterning, bone and hide of at least seven individuals of *Notiomastodon* (an extinct gomphothere) and one *Paleolama* among more than 400 vertebrate fossils, a *Notiomastodon* tusk fashioned into a gouge, a fire-hardened wooden lance, wooden digging sticks and stakes with burned tips, potato tubers and stems wedged in the cracks of a wooden grinding slab, and human footprints preserved in the occupation surface, one of them beside a hearth. Their strongest single argument is material rather than statistical: the site yielded **chewed quids of marine seaweed** (*Gigartina*, *Porphyra*, *Mazzaella*) and of the medicinal tree boldo (*Peumus boldus*), dated to 14.2 to 14.3 ka, and a quid of chewed algae cannot survive being rolled down a creek and redeposited. There is no abrasion, rounding or water-polish on the bone.
- **David J. Meltzer, J. Víctor Moreno-Mayar, Thomaz Pinotti, Peter D. Heintzman, Mikkel Winther Pedersen and Eske Willerslev** (4 May 2026, genetics): the genomic case for a pre-Clovis presence was never derived from Monte Verde and does not depend on it; the mtDNA, Y-chromosome and whole-genome clocks independently require people south of the ice sheets well before 13 ka, and the corridor was not passable when the split happened. Their line worth paraphrasing: Surovell's team did not excavate, did not show the tephra occurs in most cutbank exposures, and did not explain why no other exposure holds anything like the Monte Verde assemblage.

*How to handle it.* Give all four positions their strongest form. Give **no verdict** and do not signal one by ordering. The teaching point is not who is right; it is that the deepest American past is held up by a small number of sites, that each of them is doing enormous evidentiary work, and that pulling on any one of them shakes the whole chart. That is a truer thing to know about this node than any date.

### 4.4 The seal: what isolation is worth

Do the arithmetic explicitly, because it is the node's whole reason to exist. Take the footprints: 23,000 years before present is about 21,050 BCE, so the separation runs about **22,500 years** to 1492. Take instead the genomic expansion south of the ice, roughly 16,000 years ago, and it runs about **15,500 years**. Either way it is longer than the entire span from the first farm to now, several times over.

What that separation bought us is the only control group in human history. On the far side of it, with no contact worth the name, people independently domesticated maize from a wild grass in the Balsas drainage, built platform-and-plaza cities, ran labour taxation and terraced agriculture, kept records in knotted cord, raised earthen pyramids by the Mississippi, and in Mesoamerica invented writing. None of it copied. This is where the atlas earns its right to make general claims: when the same institutions appear twice on a planet with the connection cut, they are telling us something about human beings rather than about one lineage's luck. Name the two threads here. **Ideas:** the sealed hemisphere is the experiment, and the knowledge that crossed with these people, which plants poison, how to sew a seam that holds out cold, how to read weather off a shoreline, was the only capital they carried, and it had to be rewritten wholesale for unfamiliar species. **Craft:** the eyed needle, the boat, the hafted point, portable technique standing in for the biology a tropical primate does not have.

Also handle two obligations here. First, the dog: Perri et al. (PNAS 118:6, 2021) date dog domestication in Siberia to about 23,000 years ago, "possibly while both people and wolves were isolated during the harsh climate of the Last Glacial Maximum," and the dogs' arrival in the Americas with people to about 15,000 years ago. The dog and the White Sands walkers are the same age in Siberia and 8,000 years apart in America, which is a compact way to say the peopling was episodes, not an event. Second, and briefly, the Indigenous frame: many Native nations hold origin traditions of emergence in the Americas rather than arrival from elsewhere, and the human remains at the centre of this science, the Ancient One from Kennewick and the Anzick child from Montana, were repatriated and reburied at community request after study. Write this as a live relationship, not a disclaimer, and do not adjudicate between traditions and chronometry. **Two verified details make that paragraph concrete rather than pious, and both are safe to print.** First, Shane M. Doyle, a Crow scholar at Montana State University who convened the tribal consultation, is the tenth of forty-two authors on the 2014 *Nature* paper that sequenced the Anzick child; the consultation was inside the authorship, not appended to it. Second, the two infant girls excavated at Upward Sun River, whose genome gave us the Ancient Beringians, were given names by the local Indigenous community in the Middle Tanana Athabaskan dialect: **Xach'itee'aanenh T'eede Gaay**, sunrise girl-child, and **Yełkaanenh T'eede Gaay**, dawn twilight girl-child. The oldest genome in the American record has a name in a living language, given by the people who live where she was found. If the lesson needs one sentence to make the ideas thread land, that is the sentence.

### 4.5 Landing: why the modern world still carries this node

Two beats. The plain one: half the calories on the planet's tables descend from plants domesticated on the far side of that seal, and the reunion of the hemispheres was the largest demographic catastrophe in the record, which the atlas takes up at `columbian-exchange`. The better one, and the one to close on: the deepest securely dated evidence of human beings in the Americas is a set of children's footprints in mud, established not by a beautiful artifact but by seeds, pollen, quartz grains and lake mud argued over for four years by people who disagreed in print and then went and dated it three more ways. The past does not hand itself over. It has to be argued into existence, and the argument is not finished. That ending also rhymes back to the footprint-as-negative image: what survives of these people is precisely the shape of where they were not.

## 5. Factual traps

The confident-sounding wrong things a writer will otherwise reach for.

**1. Trap:** They walked across the Bering land bridge, a strip of ice or a narrow causeway.
**Truth:** Beringia was exposed continental shelf, a broad cold dry grassland, not ice and not narrow; and it was a place people lived in for millennia, not a bridge they hurried across. The genetic evidence supports long-term structure consistent with the "standstill" model (Tamm et al., PLoS ONE 2:9, 2007, e829; Moreno-Mayar et al. 2018 state their findings "support a long-term genetic structure in ancestral Native Americans, consistent with the Beringian 'standstill model'"). **Also:** `great-dispersal` already corrected this in print, so do not re-teach it; assume the reader knows and move on.
**Source:** 10.1371/journal.pone.0000829; 10.1038/nature25173 (PubMed 29323294, abstract read in full).

**2. Trap:** The Pacific coastal route explains the White Sands date.
**Truth:** It does not. Maximum Cordilleran ice in the southeastern Alaska bottleneck culminated about 20,000 to 17,000 years ago, and Lesnek et al. conclude an open and ecologically viable coastal pathway existed only after about 17,000 years ago. The ice-free corridor was not biologically viable until about 12,600 years ago (Pedersen et al.). At 23,000 years ago both doors were shut.
**Source:** Lesnek, Briner, Lindqvist, Baichtal and Heaton, "Deglaciation of the Pacific coastal corridor directly preceded the human colonization of the Americas," Science Advances 4(5), 2018, DOI 10.1126/sciadv.aar5040 (abstract verified, PMC5976267); Pedersen et al., Nature 537(7618), 2016, 45 to 49, DOI 10.1038/nature19085 (abstract verified).

**3. Trap:** The White Sands dates were debunked. Or: the White Sands dates are settled.
**Truth:** Neither. Madsen, Davis, Rhode and Oviatt raised a real reservoir-effect objection in a formal Comment (Science 375:6577, 2022, DOI 10.1126/science.abm4678); Pigati and colleagues replied in the same issue (DOI 10.1126/science.abm6987) and separately to C. Vance Haynes in PaleoAmerica 8(2), 2022, 99 to 101; Pigati et al. 2023 answered with terrestrial pollen and OSL; Holliday et al. 2025 added 26 independent mud dates from two other labs. A further Comment was published in PaleoAmerica in December 2025 (Martina L. Steffen, DOI 10.1080/20555563.2025.2600141; I could not open its text). Report it as a live argument being won, not as a verdict.
**Source:** Crossref metadata verified for all five items; USGS publication page fetched for the 2023 abstract and full author list.

**4. Trap:** The footprints were left by adult hunters.
**Truth:** The excavators describe the dated tracks as made by "a group of teenagers, children and the occasional adult," and read them as fetch-and-carry labour. This is the single most distinctive fact available for the scene.
**Source:** Matthew Robert Bennett and Sally Christine Reynolds, "Fossil footprints prove humans populated the Americas thousands of years earlier than we thought," The Conversation, 23 September 2021 (fetched and read). **Confirm against Bennett et al. 2021 Science before printing**, since I could not open the Science text (science.org returns 403).

**5. Trap:** The long woman-and-child trackway, and the sloth-stalking sequence, are 23,000 years old.
**Truth:** They are not, and this is now settled rather than merely unproven. **Bennett and Reynolds, the two authors who published the double trackway, state its age in their own popular account of it: the footprints date "from the end of the last ice age (about 11,550 years ago) to sometime before about 13,000 years ago."** That is roughly ten thousand years younger than the LGM horizons at Locality 2. For the sloth sequence, Bustos et al. 2018 bracket the tracks as made "sometime before 10 Ka BP and after 15.56 Ka BP," which they associate with the Clovis period, again outside this node's window. White Sands preserves tens of thousands of tracks across a lake margin that was walked for millennia; they are not all one age, and the popular press has thoroughly fused the famous trackway with the famous date. **This retires the open question in Alternate 1 below: do not promote the woman-and-child trackway to the cold open, and do not print it as 23,000 years old under any hedge.**
**Source:** Matthew Robert Bennett and Sally Christine Reynolds, "Fossil footprints: the fascinating story behind the longest known prehistoric journey," *The Conversation*, https://theconversation.com/fossil-footprints-the-fascinating-story-behind-the-longest-known-prehistoric-journey-147520 (fetched and read; also confirms the 1.5 km length, the child carried outward but not on the return, the sloth rearing to catch the scent, and the mammoth crossing without deviation); Bustos et al. 2018 read in full via PMC5916513; 10.1016/j.quascirev.2020.106610 (Crossref-verified metadata; ScienceDirect full text still 403).

**6. Trap:** Clovis people were the first Americans, or Clovis was a long-lived culture that spread across the continent.
**Truth:** Clovis-first has been, in Becerra-Valdivia and Higham's words, "effectively refuted." And Clovis is astonishingly brief: 13,050 to 12,750 calibrated years before present, about three centuries, from 32 radiocarbon ages at 10 sites. Western Stemmed points in the west are coeval with or older than Clovis, and the Fishtail complex is established in the southern cone of South America by about 12,900 years ago.
**Source:** Waters, Stafford and Carlson, "The age of Clovis, 13,050 to 12,750 cal yr B.P.," Science Advances 6(43), 2020, DOI 10.1126/sciadv.aaz0455 (abstract verified, PMC7577710); Becerra-Valdivia and Higham, Nature 584, 2020, 93 to 97 (abstract read via PubMed 32699413).

**7. Trap:** Chiquihuite Cave proves people were in Mexico 30,000 years ago.
**Truth:** Contested at the level of whether the objects are objects. Twenty archaeologists led by James C. Chatters argued in PaleoAmerica that the lithics are best explained as geofacts from disintegration, roof fall and mass movement of cave fill.
**Source:** Ardelean et al., Nature 584, 2020, 87 to 92, DOI 10.1038/s41586-020-2509-0 (abstract read via PubMed 32699412); Chatters et al., "Evaluating Claims of Early Human Occupation at Chiquihuite Cave, Mexico," PaleoAmerica 8(1), 2022, 1 to 16, DOI 10.1080/20555563.2021.1940441 (abstract verified, 20 authors confirmed via Crossref).

**8. Trap:** Ice-age Europeans crossed the Atlantic first (the Solutrean hypothesis).
**Truth:** Its technological cornerstone, the claim that Clovis overshot flaking was a Solutrean inheritance, was refuted experimentally; and ancient genomics finds no pre-contact European ancestry in the Americas: every ancient individual sequenced, save later Arctic arrivals, is closer to contemporary Indigenous Americans than to any population elsewhere. The hypothesis also has an ugly afterlife in white-nationalist politics. Do not stage it as a live scientific option; if it is mentioned at all, mention it as a closed question.
**Source:** Eren, Patten, O'Brien and Meltzer, "Refuting the technological cornerstone of the Ice-Age Atlantic crossing hypothesis," Journal of Archaeological Science 40(7), 2013, 2934 to 2941, DOI 10.1016/j.jas.2013.02.031 (Crossref verified); Willerslev and Meltzer, Nature 594, 2021, 356 to 364 (abstract read via PubMed 34135521).

**9. Trap:** The earliest Americans were a separate "Paleoamerican" people, physically unlike Native Americans, later replaced.
**Truth:** The skull-shape argument has not survived ancient DNA. The genome of the Ancient One (Kennewick) is closer to contemporary Native Americans than to any other population, and among tested groups closest to the Confederated Tribes of the Colville Reservation; the Anzick child from Montana, the only known Clovis burial, is directly ancestral to present-day Native Americans. Both were repatriated: Anzick-1 reburied in 2014, the Ancient One in 2017.
**Source:** Rasmussen et al., "The ancestry and affiliations of Kennewick Man," Nature 523(7561), 2015, 455 to 458, DOI 10.1038/nature14625; Rasmussen et al., "The genome of a Late Pleistocene human from a Clovis burial site in western Montana," Nature 506(7487), 2014, 225 to 229, DOI 10.1038/nature13025. **Update:** the full 42-author list of the Anzick paper was re-verified at Crossref in a later pass, and it confirms **Shane M. Doyle**, a Crow scholar at Montana State University, as the tenth author, which is the load-bearing detail here: the descendant-community consultation was not a courtesy appended to the science, it was inside the authorship. Contemporary reporting gives the reburial as **28 June 2014**, in the Shields River valley near the discovery site, with representatives of six Native nations present and red ochre placed with the bones. **The reburial date and the "six nations" figure rest on news reporting only; confirm against one named outlet before printing, or write it without the number.** The Ancient One's 2017 repatriation is likewise unverified in this pass.

**10. Trap:** Dogs came into the Americas with the people who left these footprints.
**Truth:** About 8,000 years too early. Perri et al. date dog domestication in Siberia to about 23,000 years ago and the entry of dogs into the Americas alongside people to about 15,000 years ago.
**Source:** Perri, Feuerborn, Frantz, Larson, Malhi, Meltzer and Witt, PNAS 118(6), 2021, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2010083118 (abstract verified via Crossref; open access at PMC8017920).

**11. Trap:** These are the oldest human footprints in the world.
**Truth:** They are the oldest widely accepted evidence of humans in the Americas, and per NPS the largest known collection of fossilized human footprints. They are nowhere near the oldest: hominin footprints at Happisburgh in Norfolk date to the Early Pleistocene (Ashton et al., PLoS ONE 9(2), 2014, e88329, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0088329), and Laetoli's are older still by millions of years. Use the NPS phrasing, not a world record.
**Source:** NPS White Sands "Fossilized Footprints" page (fetched); Crossref for Ashton et al.

**12. Trap:** The peopling of the Americas was one event with one date.
**Truth:** At minimum three things: a sparse presence before, during and just after the LGM; a widespread expansion during Greenland Interstadial 1, about 14,700 to 12,900 years ago; and much later Arctic arrivals whose genomes stand apart. Writing it as a single migration erases the structure that is the most interesting part.
**Source:** Becerra-Valdivia and Higham, Nature 584, 2020 (abstract read); Willerslev and Meltzer, Nature 594, 2021 (abstract read).

**13. Trap:** The Americas were the last land people reached.
**Truth:** The last *continents*, yes. Remote Oceania was settled thousands of years later and Aotearoa New Zealand only around 1300 CE. The node's own title is careful; the prose must be too.
**Source:** internal to the atlas (`crossing-to-sahul` flags the Lapita nodes in Eras II and III); the 1300 CE figure is **UNVERIFIED in this pass** and should be confirmed or left out.

**14. Trap:** Ninety percent of Indigenous Americans died after 1492 because the hemisphere had no diseases.
**Truth:** The mechanism is naivety to particular Eurasian crowd diseases that co-evolved with dense sedentary populations and domestic herd animals, not an absence of disease. And this belongs to `columbian-exchange`. Gesture in a clause; do not spend it here.
**Source:** the `columbian-exchange` node packet; no independent verification attempted, since the lesson should not carry the argument.

**15. Trap:** Monte Verde is the secure 14,500-year-old site that broke Clovis-first, and can be stated as fact.
**Truth:** It was, for thirty years, and it may still be. But as of 19 March 2026 it is contested in *Science*, with a proposed mid-Holocene age of 8,200 to 4,200 years, and three rebuttals from more than thirty researchers followed in May 2026. Do not write a declarative Monte Verde sentence. If the lesson names the site at all, name the fight with it. Note also that Pino and Dillehay had already published a defensive chronology in *Antiquity* in 2023: 43 radiocarbon measurements from two sediment cores dating the sediments that seal the occupation to about 14,550 cal BP.
**Source:** Surovell, Méndez, García, Lüthgens, Thompson and Latorre, *Science* 391(6791), 2026, 1283 to 1288, DOI 10.1126/science.adw9217 (Crossref-verified, abstract read); the three *Science* eLetters of 4 to 5 May 2026 (read in full via the Texas A&M CSFA compilation); Mario Pino and Tom D. Dillehay, *Antiquity* 97(393), 2023, 524 to 540, DOI 10.15184/aqy.2023.32 (Cambridge Core page fetched).

**16. Trap:** The White Sands dating is now airtight, so the site is beyond argument.
**Truth:** The *chronometric* case is close to as strong as archaeology gets. The *archaeological* case is not, and the objection that has been raised since 2021 is that there are no stone tools, no hearths, no camp debris and no human bone associated with the dated tracks. Vance Holliday, who led the 2025 confirmation, says publicly that this is "a fair question" for which he has no peer-reviewed answer, and offers only the reasoning that people moving briefly across a lake flat far from toolstone would not casually shed artifacts. Teach the reader to hold both halves: the date is strong, the archaeological silence is unexplained. This is a better sentence than either triumph or doubt.
**Source:** University of Arizona news, "Earliest evidence of humans in the Americas confirmed in new U of A study," https://news.arizona.edu/news/earliest-evidence-humans-americas-confirmed-new-u-study (fetched; Holliday quotes verified, along with the 55-date total across three materials and two research groups).

**17. Trap:** The interior ice-free corridor opened in time to be the route, or opened only after the ice-free corridor debate was settled.
**Truth:** Three independent lines say no, and they are worth naming separately because they are different kinds of evidence. Cosmogenic exposure ages put the final opening of the corridor well after pre-Clovis occupation, with initial separation of the Laurentide and Cordilleran sheets beginning about 17.5 ka and the "unzipping" complete by about 14 ka. Bison populations north and south of the ice stayed genetically distinct until about 13 ka. And sedimentary DNA and pollen from a lake in the corridor's bottleneck record steppe vegetation, bison and mammoth only from about 12.6 ka. Open is not the same as habitable, and habitable is not the same as provisioned. Later people almost certainly used the corridor, moving north.
**Source:** Jorie Clark, Anders E. Carlson, Alberto V. Reyes, Elizabeth C. B. Carlson, Louise Guillaume, Glenn A. Milne, Lev Tarasov, Marc Caffee, Klaus Wilcken and Dylan H. Rood, PNAS 119(14), 2022, e2118558119, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2118558119 (Crossref-verified, abstract read); Pedersen et al., *Nature* 537, 2016, 45 to 49; the bison figure as compiled in the Meltzer et al. *Science* eLetter, 4 May 2026.

## 6. Callback design

Six legal earlier targets, all with written lesson files, all of which I read.

- **`world-the-ice-made`** (`data/lessons/world-the-ice-made.js`). **Strongest. Use it.** Its whole argument is that the Pleistocene was a place of ice and whiplash where clever people could raise cities of mammoth bone but never fill a storehouse, and that civilization waited on the Holocene's calm. This node puts people *inside* that world on the far side of the planet, at its coldest, and shows the same fact from the other end: the lake they walked beside existed only because the ice-age climate made the Southwest wet, and the door they came through closed because the ice was at maximum. The comparison is real and it has arithmetic: Enu's bone house on the Dnieper is set "roughly fifteen thousand years ago"; the White Sands tracks are 23,000 to 21,000 years old, so the American footprints are about six to eight thousand years *older* than the European scene the reader met a node ago. Two ice-age lakeshore or steppe worlds, one about to thaw into farming, the other about to be sealed off from every other human being alive. Ask the reader to say what the ice gave and what it took in each case.
- **`paint-in-the-dark`** (`data/lessons/paint-in-the-dark.js`). **Second strongest. Use it.** The comparison is a gift and almost nobody would find it: Chauvet's clay floor "holds the footprints of a child of perhaps eight, walking beside the paw prints of a wolf or a large dog." Chauvet and White Sands both preserve a child's footprints beside an animal's, on two continents, twelve thousand years and an ocean apart. And the methodological contrast is exact: at Chauvet the deliberate marks (the blown hand stencil, "the stone gone red everywhere she was not") tell us what people *thought* and cannot be read; at White Sands the accidental marks tell us what people *did* and can be measured. That lesson ends by saying we can date the paint and measure the child's stride and still cannot read a single image. This lesson is the mirror: we can read the behaviour completely and know nothing at all about the mind. Ask which kind of evidence is more trustworthy, and which is more valuable.
- **`great-dispersal`** (third; recommend spending it in `threadsOut`, not as a callback). Its thesis, that culture rather than biology does the adapting, is genuinely tested here: people carrying a Siberian ice-age toolkit had to rebuild their entire botanical and zoological knowledge for a hemisphere of species no human had ever handled, and did. But that lesson already narrates White Sands, Monte Verde and the reservoir effect, and already asks a `why` question about the dispute, so a callback risks restating its own content back to the reader. Use it as a thread with a fresh why: the dispersal was about connection, and this is the one place where dispersal produced its opposite.
- **`tribe-of-strangers`** (fourth, and the most interesting of the alternates). Its argument is that human cooperation runs on portable shared fictions, beads, names, totems, that let strangers trust one another across distance, and that removing them snaps cooperation back to the scale of a band. This node severs the largest such network ever built. Everything on the Asian side, the hxaro-like chains that carried ochre and obsidian hundreds of kilometres, kept growing; the American side started its own from the founder group's stock and grew a wholly separate one. Useful angle: what happens to a fiction when it can no longer be exchanged with anyone outside your hemisphere? Good, but it asks the reader to do more work than the two recommended.
- **`firelight-economy`** (fifth). The connection is thin here and mostly re-treads `world-the-ice-made`'s storage callback. Skip unless the writer finds an angle in the labour reading of the footprints: a workforce of teenagers is a division of labour, which is the one thing the forager-levelling lesson does not describe.
- **`spark-of-mind`** (sixth; do not use). Already carried three callbacks in this era (`great-dispersal`, `paint-in-the-dark`, `firelight-economy`, `tribe-of-strangers` all reach for it). Over-mined.

**Recommended pair: `world-the-ice-made` and `paint-in-the-dark`.**

**Suggested `threadsOut` (4):** `maize-and-the-americas`, `columbian-exchange`, `great-dispersal`, `wolf-at-the-fire`. Swap `mound-and-mountain` in for `wolf-at-the-fire` if the ending leans on independent statecraft rather than on the episodic peopling. Each `why` must be fresh prose, not the edge label from the graph file.

## 8. Node-data flags

Precise problems in the committed graph. Do not edit the file; report them.

1. **The `date` field is about two thousand years too old for its own evidence, and the packet already knows it.** `c. 23,000 to 14,000 BCE` treats before-present figures as BCE. White Sands at 23,000 to 21,000 years before present is about 21,050 to 19,050 BCE. The era's existing nodes do the same thing and the flag file says to keep it consistent, which is defensible. But the lesson prose must not compound it: if the lesson says "23,000 BCE" while the sources say "23,000 years ago," the reader can be handed a date that is wrong by two millennia. Recommend the lesson use "years ago" throughout for this node and never convert, and that Codex decide the convention question separately.

2. **The `summary` says "during or before the Last Glacial Maximum."** "During" is well supported (LGM 26,500 to 19,000; tracks 23,000 to 21,000). "Before" rests only on Chiquihuite Cave, whose lithics twenty archaeologists argue are geofacts, and on the older Monte Verde horizons, which Dillehay himself calls ephemeral. Recommend "during the Last Glacial Maximum, and possibly before," or drop "before."

3. **The `summary`'s route sentence is right but stops one step short.** "Pacific coastal movement is a leading explanation, not a traced itinerary" is accurate and admirably hedged. What it omits is that the coastal route as currently dated (viable after about 17,000 years ago, Lesnek et al. 2018) *cannot* deliver anyone to New Mexico by 23,000 years ago. The two clauses of the summary, White Sands and the coastal route, sit next to each other implying a connection the chronology forbids. The lesson must not inherit that implication.

4. **The `wolf-at-the-fire` incoming edge is chronologically mismatched with this node's headline evidence.** "Domesticated dogs accompanied later human migrations into the Americas" is saved by the word "later," but only just. Perri et al. put dogs' arrival at about 15,000 years ago, roughly 8,000 years after the White Sands tracks. Worth a Codex look at whether the edge's `why` should carry the number.

5. **The `hook` is the cleanest thing in the node data and should survive.** "Human footprints cross lake-edge mud between layers now dated to roughly 23,000 and 21,000 years ago" is exactly right: the seeds were in undisturbed layers above and below, not in the print fill. Keep the word "between." If anything, NPS's calibrated pair (22,860 ± 320 and 21,130 ± 250) could sharpen it.

6. **`threads: ['craft', 'ideas']` is a slightly odd fit and the lesson must earn it.** The node's most consequential downstream content is wealth (maize, the Columbian exchange) and power (Cahokia, the Andean states), and neither pigment is on this node. Craft and ideas are defensible for the arrival itself, but the writer has to name them in words or the chart's colouring will read as arbitrary. See section 4.4 for the framing that makes both honest.

7. **Graph-architecture observation, not a defect.** This node has three incoming edges, all from Era I, and three outgoing edges spanning Eras II, V and VII. It is the only node in the era whose forward wires leave the Long Dawn entirely, which makes it the era's exit door for the Americas. Nothing in Era I after it is American. Worth Codex confirming that is intended.

8. **Content-overlap flag.** `great-dispersal` (earlier in the same era, written and released) already contains a full paragraph on Beringia, Clovis-first, Monte Verde, White Sands, the reservoir effect and the 2023 confirmation, plus a `why` question devoted to the White Sands dispute and a `threadsOut` entry to `maize-and-the-americas` using the sealed-hemisphere argument. Not a defect in either node, but the two lessons will collide unless this one is written to the plan in section 0. Codex should read them side by side before release. **This is now less dangerous than it was:** section 4.3b gives this node a debate `great-dispersal` could not have carried, because it did not exist when that lesson was written.

9. **The `summary` states Monte Verde as settled, and as of March 2026 it is not.** "the settlement at Monte Verde in Chile overturn the old model of one late arrival through an ice-free corridor" reads as fact. Surovell et al. (*Science*, 19 March 2026) argue for 8,200 to 4,200 years BP; Waters et al., Dillehay et al. and Meltzer et al. answered in *Science* eLetters on 4 to 5 May 2026. **Recommend Codex either soften the summary (name Monte Verde as long-standing and now contested) or shift the load onto White Sands and Cooper's Ferry, whose chronologies are not currently under this kind of attack.** Whatever Codex decides about the graph data, the lesson must carry the dispute; a lesson that says "Monte Verde proved" will be four months out of date on the day it ships.

10. **The `summary` also cites a site that falls outside the node's own date field.** The node runs `c. 23,000 to 14,000 BCE`, roughly 24,950 to 15,950 years before present. Monte Verde II at about 14,550 cal BP is roughly 12,600 BCE, some 1,400 years past the node's closing bound. Compounded with flag 1 (the BP-versus-BCE convention), this means the summary names as evidence a site the node's own dates exclude. Either the field extends or the summary should say the Monte Verde evidence sits just past this window.

11. **The `summary`'s "supported by multiple dating methods" is true and one-sided.** Three independent chronometers and 55 dates support the White Sands chronology. Nothing supports the archaeology: no tools, no hearths, no camp debris, no human bone, a gap the lead author of the 2025 confirmation publicly concedes. Recommend the summary acknowledge that the dating and the archaeological context are in very different states of health. See trap 16.

12. **Story-movement amendment, following flag 9.** Section 3 as written plans a single-place, single-horizon story at Locality 2. That still works and is still recommended. But if Fable wants the Monte Verde material as a second movement (the thin cultural layer, the seaweed quids, the footprint by the hearth), it must be staged as *evidence under argument*, not as a second scene in the past tense, and it sits about 1,400 years past the node's closing date. The safer construction is to keep the story wholly at White Sands and let Monte Verde arrive in significance, where the dispute can be handled in prose rather than dramatized.
