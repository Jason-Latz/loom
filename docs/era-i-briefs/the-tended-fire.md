Incoming edges: **none**. Nothing in the graph points here. This node is a source.

**This node's job in the atlas.** It is the reader's first encounter with the discipline's evidentiary machinery, and it arrives one day after a lesson about a species origin. Its job is not to teach fire; everyone knows fire. Its job is to teach the reader how a claim about deep time is built and how honestly it should be held: micromorphology and infrared spectroscopy on a square of cave floor, an "earliest" record that moved by 350,000 years in December 2025, a famous textbook site whose ash turned out not to be ash, and a hypothesis about human bodies whose own author writes that no solution is satisfactory. If this lesson lands, the reader will spend the next 363 nodes asking "how do we know?" before asking "what happened?" Second: it converts a substance into an institution. Fire is a chemical reaction; a **hearth** is an address, a schedule, and a chore. Every later node about cities, temples, and households is a bigger version of a place people agree to return to.

## 2. Preferred scene

**Qesem Cave, twelve kilometres east of modern Tel Aviv, around 300,000 years ago: the central hearth, a fallow deer roasting, a tortoise cooking in its own shell in the embers, and a leg bone opened for marrow weeks after the animal died.**

Documentation ledger.

Attested by excavation:
- A hearth roughly **4 square metres**, identified by mineralogical and microscopic criteria, in a **central** position in the cave, containing **two superimposed use cycles**, each composed of shorter episodes; the excavators call it "possibly the earliest superimposed hearth securely identified to date," and unusually large for its date, "possibly indicating it has been used by a relatively large group of people" (Shahack-Gross et al. 2014).
- **Spatial zoning around the hearth**: animal remains and flint tools indicating distinct activity areas, meat processing in one place and hide working in another (same paper). Somebody decided where things happened relative to the fire.
- **Anthropogenic sediment up to about 4.5 m thick**, rich in combusted wood ash and burnt bone, concentrated near the former cave entrance; use-wear damage on blades plus numerous cut marks on bone show butchering and defleshing **in the vicinity of fireplaces** (Karkanas et al. 2007).
- **Fallow deer** dominant prey; systematic blade production; systematic flint and bone recycling; social hunting and meat sharing; fire used across the whole occupation "primarily for meat roasting and cooking" (Barkai, Rosell, Blasco & Gopher 2017).
- **Tortoises roasted in the shell**, with cut marks, percussion marks and consistent burning patterns showing an established processing sequence: cook in the shell, deflesh, then direct percussion to reach the viscera (Blasco et al. 2016; Qesem tortoise material spans roughly 420 to 300 ka).
- **Stored marrow**: fallow deer metapodials kept with the dry skin still on, marrow fat degrading only slowly for **up to nine weeks**, with a distinctive chopping signature from removing dried rather than fresh skin. The excavators call it the earliest known food preservation and delayed consumption (Blasco et al. 2019).
- Site occupied roughly **420,000 to 200,000 years ago**; discovered October 2000 when road construction on Route 5 destroyed the cave ceiling; excavated by Avi Gopher and Ran Barkai (Tel Aviv University) from 2001. (Discovery circumstances: multiple secondary sources agree; UNVERIFIED against a primary excavation report, so keep it to one clause or drop it.)

Reconstruction, and `storyContext` must say so plainly:
- Any named person, any dialogue, any gender or age division of labour, weather, season, the order of the day, who fed the fire, whether the fire was *made* or *kept*, and anything about language. A composite is mandatory.

Real named person available: **no.** None exists, and the problem is worse than usual: the eight Qesem teeth described by Hershkovitz et al. (2011) were **not assigned to any taxon**. The authors write that none shows a suite of Neanderthal characters and that the balance of evidence suggests closer similarity to the Skhul/Qafzeh material, while cautioning that the resemblances are probably shared ancestral features. Barkai et al. (2017) describe the cave's behaviours as "most likely practiced by a new hominin lineage." So the scene's people are neither confidently Homo sapiens nor confidently Neanderthal. Write them as people, not as a species. This is a gift, not a constraint: the atlas's second node gets to say that the deep past does not sort itself into the boxes we brought.

Quantities to check before printing: the 4 m² hearth, the two use cycles, the nine weeks, the 420 to 200 ka occupation, the ~4.5 m of anthropogenic sediment. Do **not** invent counts of bones, tools, or people. Do not state a group size; the excavators only say the hearth's size is consistent with a relatively large group.

Why this scene: it dramatizes the node's actual mechanism, which is not combustion but **maintenance**. Everything attested here is about return and repetition: the same square of floor, the same processing sequence for a tortoise, a bone saved for nine weeks against a future meal. The lesson's thesis is that the hearth is a commitment, and the ground at Qesem is the physical residue of a commitment being kept.

### Alternate 1 (best-documented act of cooking anywhere; strong as a second movement)

**Gesher Benot Ya'aqov, the Jordan valley, around 780,000 years ago: fish cooked whole at a low temperature.** Zohar et al. (2022) analysed more than 40,000 fish remains; the archaeological horizons show a marked preference for two large cyprinids (*Luciobarbus longiceps* and *Carasobarbus canis*), a near-total absence of fish bone against an abundance of pharyngeal teeth, and the teeth spatially associated with "phantom hearths," clusters of burnt flint microartifacts. X-ray powder diffraction on the tooth enamel indicates exposure **below 500 °C**, that is, controlled low heat rather than a bonfire: the bones softened away and the teeth survived. The authors call it the earliest evidence of cooking by hominins. Note the paper carries an Author Correction (Nature Ecology & Evolution 8, 175, 2023) that I did not read; check it before printing details.

Liability: it is 480,000 years before the node's date, at a different site, and the node's own summary is anchored to Qesem. Opportunity: it is the same river valley about ninety kilometres north of Qesem, and it lets the lesson do open arithmetic (780,000 minus 300,000 = 480,000 years of cooking before the hearth this node is named for). Recommended as a short beat inside significance, not the cold open.

### Alternate 2 (safest, and the strongest anti-Euro-teleology move)

**Wonderwerk Cave, Northern Cape, South Africa, around 1.0 million years ago.** Berna et al. (2012) found ashed plant remains in exceptional preservation and burnt bone in Stratum 10, about thirty metres inside the cave, with bone heated to roughly **400 to 550 °C** and none fully calcined. They concede there are no constructed combustion features, and argue against wildfire (a burn does not travel thirty metres into a cave) and against guano self-ignition. Opening here would put the oldest secure fire in the world in Africa, a full 700,000 years before the Levantine hearth, and would inoculate the reader against reading the chart's Levant pin as fire's birthplace. Liability: no hearth, no group, no meal, so far less human pressure than Qesem.

### Alternate 3 (newest and most dramatic, but shifts the subject)

**Barnham, Suffolk, around 400,000 years ago.** Davis et al., "Earliest evidence of making fire," Nature 649(8097), 631-637 (published online 10 December 2025). Baked sediment showing repeated burning at one spot, temperatures reported above 700 °C, heat-shattered flint handaxes, and **two fragments of iron pyrite** in a place where pyrite does not occur naturally. The claim is fire *production*, pushed back roughly 350,000 years from the previous benchmark. Liability: it is fresh off the press, it is Europe, and it answers a different question than the node asks. Use it inside significance as the reason the reader should hold all "earliest" claims loosely.

### Scene to AVOID as a cold open

**The discovery of fire.** A lightning-struck tree, a brave individual seizing a burning branch, the tribe gasping. It is attested nowhere, it is Prometheus in archaeological costume, and it teaches exactly the wrong thing: that fire was one event, by one genius, at one moment. The evidence says the opposite. Fire appears patchily across a million years, in more than one hominin lineage, on more than one continent, and it was demonstrably lost and regained: Roebroeks and Villa (2011) find no convincing fire at Boxgrove, Gran Dolina or Arago despite hundreds of thousands of years of occupation, and at Roc de Marsal and Pech de l'Azé IV the fire signal *falls* in the coldest layers. Also avoid the caveman-huddling-for-warmth tableau for the same reason: hominins lived in northern Europe for hundreds of thousands of years without habitual fire.

## 3. Story movement

Six to seven paragraphs. Governing image: **the same square of floor.** Not the flame, the place. Return to it three times, each time meaning something more: a warm spot, a workplace with zones, an appointment that outlives the people keeping it.

1. **Cold open in the dark before dawn, at the ash bed.** One person, hands, breath, the smell of cold ash over warm ash. Do not describe fire in general; describe *this* fire being brought back from coals. First sentence must earn the second.
2. **The bone.** A fallow deer metapodial that has been sitting with its dried skin still on for weeks. The chop through hardened hide, the marrow still good. This is the paragraph that makes the reader realise these people had a *future tense*.
3. **The zoning.** Where you sit is decided by the fire. Butchery close, hide-scraping out toward the cave's edge, blades struck and used within reach of the light. Somebody worked out where the smoke goes, and everyone since has obeyed the same arithmetic.
4. **The tortoise and the deer.** The processing sequence: shell into the embers, deflesh, crack. A recipe with steps, repeated over generations, in a cave used for two hundred thousand years.
5. **Night.** The light stretches the day. Faces, not tasks. Hold this short and do not overclaim: nothing at Qesem proves storytelling. The evidence for *what happens* around a fire at night is ethnographic (Wiessner) and belongs in significance.
6. **The pivot: banking the fire.** Someone covers the coals for morning. That is the whole node. Nobody knows whether these people could strike a fire out of nothing; what the ground proves is that they could **keep** one, which means somebody was always responsible, which means the first schedule in human history was a chore. End here, on the obligation, with the reader feeling the "so what?" arriving.

Words and framings to avoid: "mysterious," "vanished," "primitive," "cavemen," "the dawn of civilisation," "Prometheus," "the discovery of fire," "our ancestors" used to mean the Qesem people specifically (the taxon is unresolved), "man mastered fire" (the whole point is that fire had to be tended, not mastered), and anything that treats the Levant as a corridor on the way to somewhere important. Also: no em dashes or en dashes anywhere, gate-enforced.

## 4. Significance spine

Five movements. Tie explicitly, in words, to **craft** (the node's own pigment: fire as the first process technology, a tool that transforms other materials rather than cutting them) and to **ideas** (the hearth as the place where a procedure gets taught, and the first fixed point around which a group organises itself). Touch **wealth** lightly and precisely: banked coals and a nine-week marrow bone are the earliest documented forms of a stored, maintained asset. Leave **power** alone; there is no evidence for it here and this atlas is not a military history.

1. **How you find a fire that went out 300,000 years ago.** Not by seeing charcoal. By micromorphology on intact sediment blocks, by infrared microspectroscopy that distinguishes wood-ash calcite from cave calcite, by mineral changes in bone that index temperature, and by superposition: the same spot burnt, buried, and burnt again. Make the method vivid, because the reader needs it for the rest of the atlas. Then the cautionary tale that taught the discipline this rigour: **Zhoukoudian**. For most of the twentieth century, "Peking Man used fire" rested on thick dark "ash layers." Weiner, Xu, Goldberg, Liu and Bar-Yosef reexamined Layer 10 (Science 281, 1998) and found burnt and unburnt bone with stone tools but **no ash or charcoal remnants at all**, and the dark organic unit water-laid. Gao, Zhang, Zhang and Chen (Current Anthropology 58 S16, 2017, S267-S277) argue the case back. Do not adjudicate; use it to show that the discipline's most famous fire had to be un-known before it could be re-known.

2. **Mechanism: what fire does, and why the hearth is the bigger invention.** Heat does outside the body some of the work a gut does inside it: it gelatinises starch, denatures protein, kills parasites and pathogens, softens tissue, and detoxifies some plants. Organ, Nunn, Machanda and Wrangham (PNAS 108(35), 2011, 14555-14559) found modern humans spend **4.7% of daily activity feeding against 48% predicted** for a primate of our phylogeny and body mass, an order-of-magnitude change that tracks the molar reduction in *Homo erectus*. Fire is also engineering: by roughly 72,000 years ago at Pinnacle Point people were heat-treating silcrete to make it flake better (Brown et al., Science 325, 2009, 859-862), which is fire used on stone, not on dinner. But the node's real subject is narrower: a **hearth** is fire held at a location. It creates a fixed point in space (Qesem's zoning), a repeated point in time (the two superimposed use cycles), and a job that must be handed to somebody. Craft in one sentence: the first technology whose product is a *state* rather than an object. Ideas in the next: the first place a procedure could reliably be watched and copied.

3. **The named live debate: does cooking explain our bodies?** Give both sides with full names and no verdict.
   - **Richard Wrangham** (Harvard), the cooking hypothesis: *Catching Fire: How Cooking Made Us Human* (Basic Books, 2009) and "Control of Fire in the Paleolithic: Evaluating the Cooking Hypothesis," Current Anthropology 58(S16), 2017, S303-S313. His case rests on anatomy rather than ash: weak jaws and small teeth make any raw diet for early Pleistocene *Homo* problematic, and modern humans do badly on raw wild food (the Giessen Raw Food Study, Koebnick, Strassner, Hoffmann and Leitzmann, Annals of Nutrition and Metabolism 43(2), 1999, 69-79, found average losses of 9.9 kg in men and 12 kg in women, and among women under 45 on fully raw diets roughly half stopped menstruating). The energetics were tested directly: Carmody, Weintraub and Wrangham (PNAS 108(48), 2011, 19199-19203) fed CD-1 mice raw whole, raw pounded, cooked whole and cooked pounded sweet potato and beef; cooked diets held body weight where raw diets lost it, cooking beat pounding for both foods, and pounding meat alone gave no energetic advantage.
   - **Alianda M. Cornélio, Ruben E. de Bittencourt-Navarrete, Ricardo de Bittencourt Brum, Claudio M. Queiroz and Marcos R. Costa** (Frontiers in Neuroscience 10:167, 2016), against: brain expansion runs as a steady increase across the last four million years; *Homo ergaster* at 1.9 to 1.0 Ma already carried 800 to 880 cc and *Homo erectus* reached 940 to 1200 cc with no reliable fire evidence; and their own mouse experiment (Swiss mice, 30 g portions of eye-round beef, roasted 220 °C for 15 minutes) found **similar weight change on raw and cooked meat**, with raw-fed mice needing *less* meat for the same gain. They conclude cooking is "neither sufficient nor necessary," and propose that foraging efficiency, not thermal processing, did the work, revising the metabolic model of Fonseca-Azevedo and Herculano-Houzel (PNAS 109(45), 2012, 18571-18576), whose tradeoff between body size and neuron number Wrangham's camp reads as support.
   - The detail that makes this human: **two labs, both feeding beef to mice, reached opposite conclusions.** Print that. It is the best short lesson in the atlas about what a scientific disagreement actually looks like.
   - The archaeological form of the same problem: the cooking hypothesis needs habitual fire near 1.9 Ma; Roebroeks and Villa (PNAS 108(13), 2011, 5209-5214) find habitual fire in Europe only from about 400,000 to 300,000 years ago, with a roughly 700,000-year gap after hominins arrived. That is a hole of well over a million years between the biology and the ash. Quote Wrangham's own concession from the 2017 abstract: "At the present time no solution is satisfactory, but this does not mean the problem should be ignored." A lesson that ends this argument would be lying; a lesson that stages it honestly is the best thing this node can do.

4. **The honest ranges, and the record that moved last December.** Lay the ladder out with its error bars, and do not let any rung harden.
   - Koobi Fora FxJj20 AB, Kenya, about 1.5 Ma: fire investigated with spatial analysis and FTIR (Hlubik, Berna, Feibel, Braun and Harris, Current Anthropology 58 S16, 2017, S243-S257). Suggestive, not settled.
   - Wonderwerk Cave, South Africa, about 1.0 Ma (1.27 ± 0.19 to 0.98 ± 0.19 Ma): the earliest secure burning in an archaeological context, but no constructed hearth.
   - Evron Quarry, Israel, 1.0 to 0.8 Ma: heat signatures recovered from flint that shows no visible burning at all, using UV Raman spectroscopy plus a convolutional neural network, with proboscidean tusk above 600 °C. The authors state flatly that they "cannot conclusively establish the role of hominins" and that temperature does not discriminate wildfire from managed fire (Stepka, Azuri, Horwitz, Chazan and Natalio, PNAS 119(25), 2022, e2123439119).
   - Gesher Benot Ya'aqov, Israel, about 780 ka: cooked fish, below 500 °C.
   - Habitual fire, Europe and the Levant, about 400 to 300 ka: Beeches Pit and Schöningen in Roebroeks and Villa's survey; Qesem's hearth at about 300 ka.
   - Fire *production*: previously ~50,000 years ago, from bifacial tools with percussion traces from mineral strikers in late Mousterian France (Sorensen, Claud and Soressi, Scientific Reports 8:10065, 2018). As of Nature 649, 631-637 (10 December 2025), 400,000 years ago at Barnham. **An "earliest" in this field has a shelf life measured in years.** Say so, and then say the reader should expect this paragraph to be wrong.
   - The inversion that should unsettle everyone: at Roc de Marsal and Pech de l'Azé IV in southwest France, Neanderthal fire evidence is *stronger in warm layers and weaker in the cold Pleniglacial*, which is the opposite of what a fire-making people who needed warmth would leave. Sandgathe and colleagues read it as fire collected from lightning strikes rather than produced at will (Sandgathe, Current Anthropology 58 S16, 2017, S360-S370). Fire, it seems, could be had and then not had.

5. **Landing: the node is still burning.** Two moves, in this order. First, the word. Latin *focus* meant hearth, the place where the household fire and the household gods were kept, and by metonymy it meant the household itself: Lewis and Short cite *habitatum quinque focis*, inhabited by five hearths, meaning five families. We still count homes by their fires. (If the writer wants the Kepler story about *focus* becoming the geometric focus, that is UNVERIFIED here; verify or drop.) Second, the number. The World Health Organization's household air pollution fact sheet (16 December 2025) reports that about **2.1 billion people, roughly a quarter of humanity, still cook on open fires or inefficient stoves** burning wood, dung, crop waste, coal or kerosene, and that household air pollution caused an estimated **2.9 million deaths in 2021, including over 309,000 children under five.** The oldest technology in this atlas is also, right now, one of the deadliest, and its cost falls hardest on the people who tend it. That is the honest ending: not "fire made us human," but "the chore is still being done, and it still kills the person doing it."

## 5. Factual traps

1. **Trap:** Qesem holds the oldest hearth, or the oldest evidence of fire. **Truth:** Shahack-Gross et al. claim only that it is "possibly the earliest superimposed hearth securely identified to date," and that it is unusually large for its date. Secure *burning* is 700,000 years older at Wonderwerk; fire evidence claims run to 1.5 Ma at Koobi Fora; cooking is claimed at 780 ka at Gesher Benot Ya'aqov. **Source:** Shahack-Gross, Berna, Karkanas, Lemorini, Gopher and Barkai, Journal of Archaeological Science 44(1), 2014, 12-21, DOI 10.1016/j.jas.2013.11.015; Berna et al., PNAS 109(20), 2012, E1215-E1220.

2. **Trap:** the people at Qesem were Homo sapiens, so this lesson follows on naturally from Jebel Irhoud. **Truth:** the eight Qesem teeth were deliberately **not assigned to a taxon**. Hershkovitz and colleagues found no suite of Neanderthal characters and judged the balance of evidence closer to the Skhul/Qafzeh material, while warning the resemblances are probably shared ancestral traits. The excavators elsewhere describe "a new hominin lineage." Never write "our ancestors at Qesem" or name a species. **Source:** Hershkovitz, Smith, Sarig, Quam, Rodríguez, García, Arsuaga, Barkai and Gopher, American Journal of Physical Anthropology 144(4), 2011, 575-592, DOI 10.1002/ajpa.21446; Barkai, Rosell, Blasco and Gopher, Current Anthropology 58(S16), 2017, S314-S328.

3. **Trap:** the Qesem people made fire. **Truth:** nothing at Qesem shows ignition technology. Keeping and making are separate achievements with separate evidence. Direct evidence for fire production was, until December 2025, ~50,000 years old (bifaces with strike traces from late Mousterian France); it is now claimed at ~400,000 years at Barnham, Suffolk, on the strength of baked sediment and two fragments of imported iron pyrite. That is still a hundred thousand years after Qesem's date and in another region. **Source:** Sorensen, Claud and Soressi, Scientific Reports 8:10065, 2018, DOI 10.1038/s41598-018-28342-9; Davis, Hatch, Hoare, S. G. Lewis, Lucas, Parfitt, Bello, M. Lewis, Mansfield, Najorka, O'Connor, Peglar, Sorensen, Stringer and Ashton, "Earliest evidence of making fire," Nature 649(8097), 2026, 631-637, DOI 10.1038/s41586-025-09855-6 (online 10 December 2025).

4. **Trap:** cooking made our brains big; the expensive-tissue tradeoff settles it. **Truth:** genuinely contested, and the two sides have run the same experiment with opposite results (see spine movement 3). Aiello and Wheeler's expensive-tissue hypothesis (Current Anthropology 36(2), 1995, 199-221) explains how a big brain could be *paid for* by a smaller gut; it does not establish that cooking supplied the payment. Wrangham himself writes that no current solution is satisfactory. **Source:** Wrangham, Current Anthropology 58(S16), 2017, S303-S313, DOI 10.1086/692113; Cornélio, de Bittencourt-Navarrete, de Bittencourt Brum, Queiroz and Costa, Frontiers in Neuroscience 10:167, 2016, DOI 10.3389/fnins.2016.00167.

5. **Trap:** fire is what let humans survive ice-age Europe. **Truth:** the survey that defined the field's chronology found no convincing fire evidence in Early and early Middle Pleistocene Europe, including at heavily occupied sites like Boxgrove, Gran Dolina and Arago, and concluded that early hominins moved into northern latitudes *without* habitual fire, with a gap of roughly 700,000 years before it became routine. **Source:** Roebroeks and Villa, PNAS 108(13), 2011, 5209-5214, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1018116108.

6. **Trap:** Zhoukoudian's ash layers prove Peking Man kept fires half a million years ago. **Truth:** reexamination of Layer 10 found burnt and unburnt bone alongside stone tools but **no detectable ash or charcoal**, and the dark organic unit is water-laid; there is indirect evidence of burning and no direct evidence of in situ burning. Chinese researchers have since argued the case back. Present it as a live reversal, not a debunking. **Source:** Weiner, Xu, Goldberg, Liu and Bar-Yosef, Science 281(5374), 1998, 251-253, DOI 10.1126/science.281.5374.251; Gao, Zhang, Zhang and Chen, Current Anthropology 58(S16), 2017, S267-S277, DOI 10.1086/692501.

7. **Trap:** once acquired, fire was never lost, so the record should show more of it in colder times. **Truth:** at Roc de Marsal and Pech de l'Azé IV the Neanderthal fire signal *declines* in the coldest layers, which is read as dependence on lightning-ignited fire rather than production at will. **Source:** Sandgathe, Current Anthropology 58(S16), 2017, S360-S370, DOI 10.1086/691459; Sandgathe, Dibble, Goldberg, McPherron, Turq, Niven and Hodgkins, "Timing of the appearance of habitual fire use," PNAS 108(29), 2011, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1106759108.

8. **Trap:** the Schöningen spears are 400,000-year-old proof of what these people could do. **Truth:** the Spear Horizon was initially placed near 400 ka, revised to about 300 ka, and in 2025 dated by amino acid geochronology to **about 200,000 years**, reassigned to Neanderthals. If the lesson cites Schöningen at all (Roebroeks and Villa used it as a ~400 ka fire site), it must use the current date. **Source:** Hutson, Bittmann, Fischer, García-Moreno, Gaudzinski-Windheuser, Nelson, Ortiz, Penkman, Perić, Richter, Torres, Turner, Villaluenga, White and Jöris, Science Advances 11(19), 9 May 2025, DOI 10.1126/sciadv.adv0752.

9. **Trap:** heat signatures at an old site prove hominins controlled the fire there. **Truth:** the Evron Quarry team, who developed the technique that found the signatures, state they "cannot conclusively establish the role of hominins" and that temperature is not a reliable discriminator between wildfires and managed fires. Burning is not control; control is inferred from repetition, location and association. **Source:** Stepka, Azuri, Horwitz, Chazan and Natalio, PNAS 119(25), 2022, e2123439119, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2123439119.

10. **Trap:** early cooking means roast meat. **Truth:** the earliest cooking claim is **fish**, and the tell is negative: the bones are gone and the pharyngeal teeth remain, with enamel crystal sizes indicating exposure below 500 °C, meaning slow low heat rather than a fire. At Qesem itself the best-attested cooking sequence is a **tortoise roasted in its shell**. **Source:** Zohar, Alperson-Afil, Goren-Inbar, Prévost, Tütken, Sisma-Ventura, Hershkovitz and Najorka, Nature Ecology & Evolution 6(12), 2022, 2016-2028, DOI 10.1038/s41559-022-01910-z (with Author Correction, Nature Ecology & Evolution 8, 2024, 175, DOI 10.1038/s41559-023-02270-y, which I did not read: check it); Blasco, Rosell, Smith, Maul, Sañudo, Barkai and Gopher, Quaternary Science Reviews 133, 2016, 165-182.

11. **Trap:** the people at Qesem chose where to put the hearth to minimise smoke. **Truth:** that finding belongs to a different cave. Kedar, Kedar and Barkai simulated smoke dispersal from 16 hypothetical hearth positions at **Lazaret Cave, France**, found an optimal zone of about 5 × 5 m, and showed the excavated hearth sits in it. Media coverage dates Lazaret unit UA25 to about 170,000 years ago; the paper itself says "Lower Paleolithic." Qesem's hearth is centrally placed and its surroundings are zoned by activity, which is a real and separate fact. Do not merge them. **Source:** Kedar, Kedar and Barkai, Scientific Reports 12, 27 January 2022, DOI 10.1038/s41598-022-05517-z; Shahack-Gross et al. 2014 for the Qesem zoning.

12. **Trap:** citing "6% of daytime talk was stories" from the firelight study. **Truth:** I searched the paper's text and could not find that figure. What Wiessner does report: by day, 31% of talk was economic (foraging plans, resources, hunting, technology), 34% complaint or criticism, 16% joking sessions, 4% land rights; at night, **81% of lengthy multi-person conversations were stories**, largely about known people. Use those and no others. **Source:** Wiessner, "Embers of society: Firelight talk among the Ju/'hoansi Bushmen," PNAS 111(39), 2014, 14027-14035, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1404212111.

## 8. Node-data flags

Precise problems in the committed graph. Do not edit the file; report them.

1. **The hook overstates the duration of one hearth.** "A hearth at Qesem Cave holds ash returned to the same spot for millennia." What the excavation reports is **two superimposed use cycles**, each composed of shorter episodes, in a feature of about 4 m². The multi-millennial claim belongs to fire use across the site's ~220,000-year occupation, not to the single hearth feature. Recommend softening the hook to repetition rather than millennia, and having the lesson carry the correct distinction explicitly.

2. **"Habitual control of fire is documented by roughly 300,000 years ago" is conservative in a way that hides the interesting number.** The field's habitual-fire threshold is usually given as roughly **400,000 to 300,000** years ago (Roebroeks and Villa), and fire *production* is now claimed at ~400,000 years (Barnham, December 2025). The node reads as though 300 ka is the frontier when it is the comfortable middle of a moving range. The lesson should print both ends.

3. **The threads are incomplete for what this node actually does.** The node carries only `craft`. Its two forward `enables` edges are about social hours and a nightly gathering place, which is `ideas` territory, and the forge spec requires the lesson to tie to at least two threads in words. Recommend Codex consider adding `ideas` to `threads`. Whatever is decided about the data, the lesson must name craft and ideas explicitly so the chart's pigments do not contradict the prose.

4. **The `swasia` region pin risks teaching a Levantine origin for fire.** This is the flag raised in the packet, and it is real but not a data error: `swasia` reads consistently against `ochre-for-the-dead` (Qafzeh) and the later Levantine nodes, and Qesem is genuinely the anchor site. The mitigation is prose, not geography: the lesson must say plainly that the oldest secure burning is African (Wonderwerk, ~1.0 Ma), that the oldest cooking claim is 780 ka and 90 km up the same valley, and that habitual fire shows up across Africa, the Levant and Europe in the same window. Without that, the second day of the atlas silently plants technology's birthplace in southwest Asia.

5. **The summary's "one influential, contested hypothesis" leaves the argument anonymous.** Correct hedging, but a lesson that names Wrangham on one side and Cornélio and colleagues on the other, and shows both camps running mice on beef to opposite conclusions, is dramatically and pedagogically stronger than an unattributed "some scholars." Not a data defect; a note that the summary is doing less work than the lesson should.

6. **Nothing points at this node.** `edgesIn` is empty, which is correct for the atlas's second node, but it means the reader arrives here only in reading order. Worth a Codex check that the map's first two nodes read as a pair rather than as two unconnected sources.

7. **Sequencing constraint, not a data error, but it will fail the gate.** `scripts/check.mjs` requires every callback target to be in `_manifest.js`. This lesson's only legal callback target is `first-of-our-kind`, which is unwritten. Forge and manifest that lesson first, or this file cannot go green.

8. **Species implication to watch in the surrounding data.** Nothing in this node's own fields claims the Qesem people were Homo sapiens, which is correct.
