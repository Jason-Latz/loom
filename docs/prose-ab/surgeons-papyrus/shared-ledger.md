## Controlling question

A man who is not a physician must preserve, exactly, a medical tradition he
cannot verify: instructions about injuries he has never seen, written in
language old enough to need built-in explanations. The story's controlling
problem: exact copying can carry a technique beyond any particular pair of
hands, but the copyist can only preserve the words, not the knowledge of the
hands that made them. The lesson's larger question, answered in the View from
Above: what kind of medical text has room for injuries its author could not
cure, and what does that room tell us about how its author worked?

## Lesson mechanism

1. A repeated case architecture (title, examination, diagnosis with words to
   say, verdict, treatment) turns injury into transferable procedure.
2. The three verdicts are the practitioner's own declared course of action,
   not predictions; sorting into treat, contend, refuse is triage.
3. The third verdict matters because the treatise records injuries the
   practitioner cannot cure and mostly will not treat: observation stays
   valuable when treatment fails. A recipe collection has no slot for failure.
4. Writing separates a technique from a particular pair of hands: an older
   treatise could be copied, glossed, corrected, and used after its vocabulary
   had gone dark.
5. Empirical trauma care and incantation coexisted; the working distinction is
   visible versus invisible cause, not rationality versus superstition.
6. The evidence is partial: the treatise lacks its beginning, stops mid
   sentence, and its original date and authorship are unresolved. No
   demonstrated line runs from this roll to modern triage.

## Measurements and dates

- Surviving roll: about 4.68 m; 12 sheets; roll height about 32 to 33 cm.
  (NLM's "about 15 feet" is a looser approximation of the same object; use
  4.68 m.)
- Original roll: minimum five metres, Breasted's reconstruction from the lost
  opening. Never merge this with the 4.68 figure.
- Copy date: c. 1650 to 1550 BCE (17th or early 18th Dynasty). Breasted: Hyksos
  age, Theban scribe, "not less than a generation older than the Papyrus
  Ebers."
- Original treatise date: unresolved. Proposals on the Saxon page run from
  Breasted's Old Kingdom (3000 to 2500 BCE) through Meltzer's roughly 2200 to
  2000 BCE down to one proposal of only 200 to 300 years before the copy.
- Purchase: Luxor, 20 January 1862; the forged second roll two months later.
- To the New York Historical Society 1906; Breasted asked to translate 1920;
  edition published 1930; the roll now belongs to the New York Academy of
  Medicine.
- Counts: 48 cases (Breasted's modern numbering) or 49 (the Saxon division);
  27 head cases and six spinal cases in van Middendorp's summary; 69 glosses;
  verdict 3 fourteen times, thirteen genuine, ten with no treatment, three
  with comfort measures; about a quarter of the surviving treatise.

## Scholarly uncertainty

Preserve these hedges exactly as hedges:

1. The original treatise's date and authorship are unresolved; the Imhotep
   attribution was Breasted's own "pure conjecture" and is now regarded as
   highly unlikely.
2. The brain and meninges "firsts" are Breasted's claims about the documents
   known in 1930; the autonomic-dysfunction and Lasegue claims belong to van
   Middendorp, Sanchez, and Burridge. Attribute; do not state as timeless fact.
3. Breasted's relapse-and-interpolation reading of Case 9 is his
   interpretation; the textual oddity (no observations, no diagnosis, no
   verdict) is the fact. Keep the two separable.
4. The visible-cause versus invisible-cause sorting principle is the Saxon
   record's framing, better supported than a rational-versus-superstitious
   split, but still a framing.
5. Whether the copyist was a professional employed in a copying office is
   Breasted's guess ("He may have been an employe on the staff of some ancient
   copyist's office"); that he was not a medical man is Breasted's inference
   from the hand and errors. Hold both at their strength.
6. No demonstrated transmission from this roll to any later medical tradition,
   including modern triage. The resemblance is convergence, not descent.

## Permitted reconstruction

The storyContext must plainly label reconstruction, as the active lesson does.
Under that label, modest connective action, sensory experience, and interior
response consistent with the documented setting are permitted: handling pen,
ink, and roll; heat and light; fatigue; a limited personal response to the
copper comparison or to the old words. Keep inventions modest, keep them
consistent with the two-ink, correction-marked manuscript, and never let them
smuggle in a factual claim.

## Forbidden inference

- No invented quotations presented as historical speech; the only quoted
  matter is the formulae above.
- No invented measurements or manuscript features (no asserting fine joints,
  drifting lines, or any physical detail not in the documented list).
- No biography, name, age, family, motive, or belief for the copyist; no
  reason for the interruption (Breasted himself says "whatever it may have
  been").
- No claim that the copyist understood or misunderstood any particular case
  beyond what the documented errors support.
- No causal claim that this roll influenced any later medicine.
- No presenting the visible/invisible sorting principle as the Egyptians' own
  stated theory; it is a modern description of the pattern.
- Do not call the papyrus "the first medical document" or similar; the
  priority claims are only those attributed above.

## Conflicts resolved

1. **Roll length.** The active story's "better than five metres long" merged
   Breasted's reconstructed original with the surviving object. Resolution:
   the story does not state a total length at the desk (the twelve glued
   sheets are documented and sufficient); the significance separates the two
   figures explicitly: the surviving roll at 4.68 m, and Breasted's judgement
   that at least a column is lost at the beginning, so the original ran at
   least five metres. Never present either number as the other.
2. **"No other papyrus uses that verdict."** This is Breasted's explicit claim
   about the surviving papyri known to him. The shared recall answer now says
   "is known to use," the only wording change to the question block.
3. **The forgery fragments.** The Saxon page says the fake contained "the
   fragments of the first preserved column," so the saw-edwin-smith source
   note now says "the roll's first preserved column" instead of "the lost
   first column of this one." Only that phrase changed.
4. **Correction mechanisms.** Two distinct documented instances: an omitted
   word placed in the margin with a cross (Breasted's "earliest known
   asterisk"), and, in Case 9, an omitted word inserted above the line with a
   tiny red x marking its place. Use either or both accurately; do not merge
   them into one invented habit.
5. **The active story's "a line drifts toward its neighbour"** and "joints so
   finely made you have to tilt them into the light" are not documented
   manuscript features. Dropped from the shared record; candidates must not
   assert them. (Tilting sheets toward raking light as the copyist's action is
   permissible reconstruction; the fineness of these joints as a fact is not.)
6. **Case counts.** 48 is Breasted's modern division (and his numbering);
   the Saxon division counts 49. Candidates present the forty-eight with its
   modern-editor caveat and may note the forty-nine.
7. **The blank at the break.** Breasted: space "for several columns more";
   Saxon: the last 39 cm of the recto uninscribed. Use the measured 39 cm
