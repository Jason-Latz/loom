THE EVIDENCE

Five ways to put citations into a Loom lesson, two modifiers, and a recommendation. All of it on real prose.
Palette
Width

Every specimen below renders the same real paragraph: the opening of The View from Above in Ochre for the Dead. Three claims in it are load-bearing and genuinely sourced, so the density here is honest, not a mock-up: roughly what a real lesson would carry.

The options differ on one axis, and it is the axis your constraint lives on: how much the apparatus touches the prose. They run from an apparatus that never enters the sentence at all, to one that puts a numeral inside it. Everything else follows from where you land on that line.

Option A

The back of the book

Nothing enters the sentence. The evidence waits at the end, keyed to the exact words it supports.

prose untouched · apparatus at the end
What it buys

The prose is byte for byte what it is today. An apparatus that never touches the reading cannot damage it, which makes this the only option with zero literary risk. It is also what serious popular history actually does: trade publishers key their endnotes to a quoted phrase and put no superscript in the running text, precisely because a general reader is not a scholar. Cheapest thing on this page to build and to validate.

What it costs

A skeptical reader has to go looking, and most never will. The apparatus is invisible during the read, so it does very little of the work you actually want from it: the lesson does not feel more authoritative while you are reading it. Matching a quoted phrase back to its sentence is also slower than following a numeral. The obvious worry, that two passages share a phrase and the key becomes ambiguous, turns out to be measurably tiny: a sweep of the existing lessons found six-word spans unique within their own lesson in 7,153 of 7,155 cases, so an exactly-once match is a sound rule for the gate to enforce.

Option B

The quiet link

The smallest thing that could work. The sourced phrase is simply a link to the paper. No numerals, no notes, no apparatus, nothing to build.

what JSTOR Daily, Works in Progress and Quanta actually do
What it buys

Almost nothing to implement and nothing to maintain: a phrase becomes an anchor and the job is done. Identical on every device, no reveal, no state, no positioning. It is what the best magazines on the web do, and it reads clean because a citation is textually indistinguishable from any other link. If you want this shipped without a new component, this is the option.

What it costs

It throws away everything except the URL. The reader cannot see who wrote the paper, what year it is, whether it is open access, or what specifically it establishes, until they have already left the lesson. Worse, it cannot distinguish “here is my evidence” from “here is something else to read,” which is the one distinction this whole feature exists to make. And a bare date or a count is exactly the kind of claim it handles least well.

Option C

The claim carries the mark

No numerals anywhere. The sourced words themselves take a fine gilt underline, and pressing them opens the note.

nothing inserted · the text is marked, not interrupted
What it buys

The tightest possible coupling between claim and source: you see the exact span of words the paper backs, not merely the sentence one happened to end. No foreign object is inserted into the prose, so sentence rhythm and line breaks are untouched. The tap target is a whole clause, so it is finger sized for free. And it makes the lesson’s intellectual honesty visible, which is the actual point: you can see at a glance which sentences are reporting and which are the writer thinking.

What it costs

Three underlines sit on the page whether or not you want them, and an underline in running text reads as a hyperlink to a lot of people. It demands more editorial judgment than a numeral does, because you have to decide the exact span every time. And a long marked clause that wraps across a line break is slightly less tidy than a single glyph.

Option D

Superscript and inline reveal

The academic convention, made touchable. A small gilt numeral, and the note opens beneath the paragraph.

the handoff’s recommendation
What it buys

Unambiguous and universally understood: nobody has to be taught what a superscript numeral is. It survives skimming, it is the easiest of the five to author correctly and to validate mechanically, and it is one code path at every width with no positioning math. If Loom ever wants a printed edition, this is the only option that already works there.

What it costs

A numeral inside a sentence is the single most reliable way to make a page read like a textbook, and this is a product whose whole claim is that history can be read like a story. Three of them in one paragraph is felt. It also decouples claim from source: the numeral tells you a sentence ended, not which words were doing the work. And it is the option most likely to creep upward in density across 365 lessons, because adding one is free.

Option E

Marginalia

The atlas convention. Notes hang in the margin beside the line they gloss, the way annotation sits beside a chart. Nothing to click.

always visible · needs the margin, so it changes shape below 1100px
What it buys

By some distance the most beautiful of the five, and the most on-brand: an antique chart is covered in marginal hands. The evidence is ambient rather than hidden, so it is discovered without any interaction at all, and the eye can ignore it completely. It is the only option where the apparatus adds to the page as an object instead of merely not subtracting.

What it costs

It needs a margin to live in, so below roughly 1100 pixels it has to become something else, and what it becomes cuts the paragraph in half at the point of the note. Switch to phone width above and watch it happen. That is two behaviors to maintain, and the one a phone gets is the worse of the two. Mobile is a first-class target here, so this is a real cost and not a rounding error.

Option F · a modifier, not a rival

Evidence off by default

One control at the head of the lesson. Read it clean, then turn the evidence on. Sits on top of C, D or E.

shown over Option C · press the switch
What it buys

It answers the constraint completely and without compromise: the default reading is literally unmarked, and the apparatus is there in full for anyone who wants it. It also lets you be more generous with citation density than you otherwise could, since density costs nothing when it is switched off. Remember the setting and a reader chooses once.

What it costs

A feature nobody turns on is a feature nobody has, and authority you have to opt into is weaker than authority that is simply present. It adds a control to a reading room that currently has exactly one, and it is a second thing to explain. There is also a quiet risk: it makes citing feel optional to whoever is forging the lesson.

Option G · the end apparatus

The sources cartouche

Whatever marks the prose, the lesson should end with the whole list in one place. The question is whether it looks like a bibliography or like a legend.

sits between The Threads and Prove It to Yourself
What it buys

The apparatus degrades gracefully: even if a reader never presses a single mark, the evidence is visibly there, gathered, and clickable. It is where the open-access flag and the paywall admission can live honestly. Styled as a chart legend rather than a reference list, it reads as part of the atlas instead of an academic appendix bolted on.

What it costs

It lengthens a page that already ends with connections, five questions and three further readings, and it sits close enough to If This Grabbed You to blur with it. Needs a clear division of labor between the two: this one is evidence, that one is invitation.

Swept and set aside

Seven patterns I looked at and would not build

Worth naming, because most of them are the first thing anyone reaches for.

  • Inline source pills. The Perplexity-style chip with a domain name and a “+3” count. A published teardown of the pattern calls it citation theater, the look of rigor without the rigor underneath, and it is the loudest object you can put in a sentence.
  • Hover tooltips built on role="tooltip". The ARIA pattern states that tooltips do not receive focus, which makes any link inside one unreachable by keyboard. Wrong primitive for a thing whose entire purpose is a clickable destination.
  • A <details> element forced inline. Tempting because it needs no JavaScript. The technique’s own author documents that on a phone the opened note covers the summary you have to press to close it.
  • Interest invokers (interestfor). Genuinely elegant and genuinely not ready: Chrome only, still marked experimental, and on touch it is a long press into a context menu rather than a tap.
  • Hover previews on desktop, something else on mobile. What Wikipedia does. The instinct to match the interaction to the device is right and worth stealing; two separate mechanisms to maintain is the thing we are trying not to buy.
  • A separate evidence panel. Loom already has a dossier and a reading room. A third overlay is feature creep, and it still cannot tell you which sentence rests on what.
  • Naming the source in the sentence and stopping there. What Asterisk and most print magazines do, and what Loom already does informally. Keep doing it for narrative claims, but it is not clickable and not checkable from the page, so it cannot be the mechanism.
Before you pick

Seven things the codebase has already decided for you

An audit of the actual files turned these up. Two of them quietly rule out options that look fine on paper.

  • A floating card cannot sit on the paper. Loom pins its paper-grain overlay at z-index: 300, and a native popover renders in the browser’s top layer, above every z-index there is. A citation card would be the one flat, ungrained surface on an aged page. This, not browser support, is why the reveal happens in flow.
  • Escape already means “close the lesson.” The handler in js/reader.js tests the reading room first, so any overlay-based citation would have to be spliced in ahead of it or a reader dismissing a card loses their place in a two thousand word page. An in-flow reveal never has the problem.
  • Anchor jumps do not work in the reading room. The body sets overflow: hidden and #reader is the real scroll container, so a classic endnote’s href="#fn3" points at an ancestor that cannot scroll. Option A needs a scripted scroll, and so does its return link. Cheap, but not free, and not obvious.
  • There is no link style to inherit. The only anchor rule in the whole stylesheet is .deeper-link. A bare inline link renders in default browser blue on parchment, so Option B is not actually the zero-CSS option it looks like; it needs its own rule before it can ship.
  • The gate will read the new field too. The dash scan walks objects recursively, so a page range has to be written “491 to 522.” And the 1500 to 2200 word band counts the prose, with warnings failing the release gate, so any in-prose token must be stripped before counting. Several Era I lessons already sit above 2150, which is a quiet argument against anything that adds words to a sentence.
  • The header has no room for a global switch. The tool bar already drops the progress readout at 1360px purely to stop flex crushing the search box. Option F’s control belongs at the head of the lesson, where the specimen puts it, not in the chrome.
  • The permissive escape hatch always gets used. The existing deeper renderer tolerates a bare string instead of an object, and a count of the corpus says 63 of 204 further-reading items across 68 lessons are exactly that: legacy strings with no URL at all, in a field whose own spec asks for three clickable sources. Whatever gets built here should be strict-shape from its first commit, with no lenient branch to fall through.
Where this lands

The list is the spine. The mark is the argument.

Build G first, because it carries the citations whatever else you choose. Then settle the one real question separately: whether anything at all marks the sentence.

two real paragraphs · five citations · press any underlined clause

I had three independent reviews run this design space, one weighing the prose, one the authority, one the durability of maintaining it across 297 unwritten lessons. They ranked the same thing first, unanimously, and it was not the specimen above: a terminal list keyed to the verbatim phrase it supports, with nothing whatsoever inside the sentence. Their reasons hold up. The prose stays byte-identical to what the literary revision signed off on. It is the only option a validator can check as a citation rather than as a URL, because an exactly-once phrase match either resolves or turns the gate red. And it is a plain data field, with no paragraph tokenizer, no invented anchor style, and no second mobile path.

Then each of them, unprompted, made the same case against itself. A citation the reader never sees is a citation the reader never trusts. The list sits after the story, after the significance, after the threads, after five questions: a reader who finishes a lesson and closes it has been shown no evidence at all. Authority is a felt property of the sentence, not of the appendix.

That objection is the whole reason to do this, so my vote is to mark the prose, and the specimen above is the version I would build. But notice what the unanimity actually settles: the end apparatus is not in dispute by anyone, it is the half a validator can enforce, and the mark is purely additive on top of it. So build the list first, alone, and ship it. The mark is the second decision, and it is a question of taste about what authoritative means rather than an engineering one, which makes it yours rather than mine.

If you do mark it, mark the claim rather than the sentence. Look at the second paragraph above. The claim about the ochre beds is marked, and the sentence right after it, the one about people being fussy, is not, because that sentence is the lesson thinking rather than reporting. A numeral cannot make that distinction, and it is exactly the distinction a skeptical reader wants. Pairing it with E’s margin costs nothing extra: Option E is beautiful because it uses the margin and fails because it requires one, so making the note a reveal turns the margin into an enhancement. Nothing has to collapse on a phone, because nothing was there.

Option F can be layered on later without touching any of the data, and Option A’s keyed list is what the cartouche becomes if you ever want a print edition. Nothing here forecloses anything.

On the mechanism: the reveal above is an ARIA disclosure, a control with aria-expanded pointing at the gloss. The obvious modern alternative is the native Popover API with CSS anchor positioning, which buys dismiss-on-Escape and automatic edge flipping in about six lines. Do not use it here, and the reason is Loom-specific rather than a browser-support quibble: a popover renders in the browser’s top layer, above every z-index there is, and Loom pins its paper-grain overlay at z-index: 300. A floating citation card would be the one flat, ungrained, un-aged surface on an otherwise aged page. Revealing in flow keeps the note on the paper. The end apparatus should also carry role="doc-endnotes" so a screen reader announces it as the notes rather than as another list.
